## Module 1 — You Are Already A Brand, Whether You Know It Or Not

**Objective**

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

- Define what a personal brand actually is
- Explain the difference between a brand and a follower count
- Identify the things that already shape how people see you
- Spot the gap between how you see yourself and how others see you
- Take ownership of the first version of your brand

**Core Concept**

A lot of athletes hear the word "brand" and think of two things. A logo and a follower count. That is not what a brand is.

A brand is what people believe about you before you walk in the room.

When your coach talks about you, what does she say? When your teammate explains who you are to a new player, what does he describe? When a fan posts about a game, what stands out to them? That is your brand. It is not a slogan. It is a set of impressions that already exist in the minds of other people.

Here is the key idea. You already have a brand. The only question is whether you are shaping it or letting other people shape it for you.

Most athletes never think about this. They show up, train, compete, and assume their performance speaks for itself. But your performance is only part of the picture. People also notice how you carry yourself. How you treat the equipment manager. How you talk in interviews. How you post on social media. How you handle a loss. How you respond to a recruit on a visit. All of that builds the brand around you.

A brand is built on three things. Reputation, performance, and signals.

Reputation is what people say about you when you are not in the room. It is the result of years of small actions. Showing up on time. Keeping your word. Being someone people want to be around. Reputation moves slowly, but it sticks.

Performance is the actual quality of what you do. On the field. In the classroom. In your content. In how you treat others. People watch what you do more than what you say.

Signals are the small things that tell people who you are. Your tone in interviews. The way your social media looks. The kind of music in your highlight reel. The causes you care about. Signals are easier to control than reputation, but they only work if they line up with the truth.

When these three things match, your brand feels real. When they do not match, people sense it. They may not be able to explain why, but they stop trusting you.

This is the trap a lot of athletes fall into when NIL deals come in. They try to be someone they are not. They take a deal that does not fit. They post a brand of energy drink they would never actually drink. Their audience smells it instantly. And the brand is worse off too.

A strong brand starts with truth. You do not have to be flashy. You do not have to be loud. You do not have to be funny. You have to be clear about who you are, and consistent enough that people learn to expect it.

There is also a difference between a brand and a follower count. A follower count is just a number. A volleyball player with 1,800 local followers who watch every post can be worth more to a hometown business than a TikTok star with 200,000 followers who scroll past everything. The first one has a brand. The second one has a number.

You do not need to be famous to have a brand. You need to be known for something specific.

Why this matters: If you ignore your brand, people will define you with whatever they have, you will feel pressure to fake a personality for deals, and you will lose chances because you cannot explain who you are. If you take ownership, you stop being only your stat line, you build value that lasts past your season, and you stay in control of how your story gets told.

**Scenario**

Maya is a junior on a Division II soccer team. She has about 2,400 followers on Instagram. Mostly teammates, friends from high school, classmates, some local soccer fans, and her family.

She has never thought about her brand. She just plays.

One day a sports drink company emails her assistant coach. They are looking for athletes for a regional campaign. The coach forwards a few names, including Maya's. The brand looks Maya up online. They see two things. First, her game footage and a strong record. Second, a feed full of posts that have no theme. Some are recovery photos. Some are random reposts. One is a meme about hating Mondays. There is no clear idea of who she is.

The brand passes on Maya. They go with another player who has fewer followers but a clear story about training while studying for nursing school.

A month later Maya hears about it from a teammate. She is frustrated. She is a better player. She has more followers. Why did she get skipped?

The answer is simple. Maya has attention. She does not have a brand.

Her teammates already think of her a certain way. She is the calm one. She studies film harder than anyone. She mentors freshmen. She volunteers with a local youth team. That is real. That is a brand. But none of it shows up online, in interviews, or in the way she presents herself.

The brand did not see Maya. They saw a player with a messy feed.

Maya now has a choice. She can keep playing and hope someone notices. Or she can take what is already true about her and start making it visible. Not by faking a personality. By showing the version of herself that already exists when she is not paying attention.

**Student Action**

- The Outside Test — Ask three people who know you well to describe you in three words. A teammate, a coach, and someone outside sports. Write down what they say.
- The Inside Test — Now describe yourself in three words without looking at what the others said. Compare the two lists. Where do they match? Where do they not match? What does the gap tell you?
- The Signal Audit — Open your most-used social account. Look at the last ten posts as if you were a stranger. What story do they tell? What kind of person would somebody guess you are? Does that match who you really are?
- The Reputation Check — Write one or two sentences answering this: "When my name comes up in a room I am not in, I want people to say ___ because ___."
- The First Decision — Pick one small change you can make this week that closes the gap between who you are and what people can see. Not a rebrand. One small change. Write it down.

   - [ ] The number of followers you have
   - [✓] What people believe about you before you walk in the room
   - [ ] A type of contract
2. Which is the most important sign of a strong brand?
   - [ ] Going viral once
   - [✓] Reputation, performance, and signals lining up
   - [ ] Having a famous teammate
   - [ ] Posting every day
3. Why can a small-audience athlete still have a strong brand?
   - [ ] They post less
   - [ ] They have free time
   - [✓] Local trust and clear identity can mean more than wide reach
   - [ ] They cost less to hire
4. What happens when an athlete takes a deal that does not match who they are?
   - [ ] The audience usually does not notice
   - [✓] The audience senses it and trust drops
   - [ ] It always boosts their following
   - [ ] It removes the need for content

   - [ ] Picking a color palette

   - [ ] Signing your first deal

## Module 2 — Marketing 101 For Athletes

**Objective**

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

- Define marketing in plain language
- Apply the basic parts of marketing to your own career
- Tell the difference between attention and trust
- Explain why audience size is not the same as audience value
- See yourself as both the product and the marketer

**Core Concept**

Marketing has a bad reputation. People think it means tricking someone into buying something. That is not marketing. That is manipulation.

Real marketing is simple. It is the work of helping the right people know, trust, and choose you. That is it.

Every marketing decision answers one of those three questions.
- Know: Do the right people see what I do?
- Trust: Do they believe what I say?
- Choose: Do they pick me when there are other options?

Now apply that to your life as an athlete. The right people might be fans, scouts, local businesses, classmates, future employers, a national brand, or even a future business partner. Different goals mean different audiences. But the three questions stay the same.

To understand how marketing works, you need to know the basic parts. People call these the 4 Ps. We are going to add a fifth one that matters more than most people admit.

1. Product. For most companies, product is the thing they sell. For you, the product is the full package. Your athletic ability. Your story. Your personality. The way you treat people. What you stand for. Your name, image, and likeness.

2. Price. Price is what you charge. For you, this shows up as a flat fee for a post, a speaking rate, a booking rate for an appearance, a percentage on a code or link. Price is also a signal. If you charge too little, brands assume your value is low. If you charge too much without proof, they walk away.

3. Place. Place is where people find you. For you, it is the platforms where your audience lives. Instagram. TikTok. YouTube. The local newspaper. Your team's official channel. A podcast. A camp. Each place reaches different people. You cannot be everywhere. Pick the places that match your goals.

4. Promotion. Promotion is how you tell people about yourself. Content. Interviews. Public appearances. Word of mouth. Promotion is not selling. It is showing. The best promotion shows the truth in a way the right people can find it.

5. People. This is the one most marketing books skip. Marketing happens through people. Your coach. Your sports information director. Your teammate. A local reporter. Your agent if you have one. A business owner who knows your family. The people in your circle shape what your marketing can do. Take care of them.

That is marketing. Five parts. None of them require you to be fake.

Attention and trust are not the same thing. Attention is the number of eyes. Trust is how much weight those eyes give your words. A viral post can get a million views and zero trust. A post seen by 800 people in your hometown can move real money if those 800 people trust you.

This matters because brands pay for trust. They can buy attention from anyone with a credit card. They cannot buy trust. You have to build it. Slowly. Through showing up, telling the truth, and being someone the audience actually believes.

You are both the product and the marketer. That is rare. In most companies, the product is made in one room and sold in another. With athletes, the same person creates the value and tells the story. That is power if you use it. It is a problem if you do not.

The athletes who win are the ones who stop seeing themselves as just an athlete. They start seeing themselves as a small business. A brand. A media company of one.

**Scenario**

Devon is a junior linebacker at a Division I school. He gets about three message requests a week from local businesses. Most are vague. "Hey, we'd love to do something with you."

Until now, he has said yes to whatever comes in. He posted for a barber shop in exchange for two free cuts. He shouted out a smoothie bar for a $40 gift card. He wore a t-shirt for a local clothing line for free.

He has 7,500 followers. He thinks his ceiling is low because his number is small compared to his teammates with 80,000 plus.

Then a local car dealership reaches out. They want him to come to an event, take photos with three cars, and post twice. They offer $1,200.

Devon almost says yes right away. The number is bigger than anything he has gotten. But his teammate, who has gone through this before, asks him three questions.
- Do you actually like that brand of car?
- How many of your followers live in this city?
- What else do you have lined up this month?

Devon does not have answers. He has never tracked who his audience is. He has never sat with the question of fit. He has been saying yes to whatever shows up.

Here is the math the dealership did. They looked at Devon's recent posts. They saw two things. First, he posts mostly about his hometown, which is the same city as the dealership. Second, his comments are mostly from real people, not bots. That is rare. That is trust. They were willing to pay for it.

Devon ended up taking the deal, but the lesson was bigger than the paycheck. He had been pricing himself based on his follower count. The dealership priced him based on trust. The gap between those two numbers was a lot of money.

After that, Devon started thinking about every offer through the five parts of marketing. He stopped being a free spokesperson and started being a marketer.

**Student Action**

- The Five-Part Audit — For one offer you have gotten in the past, or one you want to land, fill in the five parts: Product (what am I actually offering?), Price (what is it worth?), Place (where will it live?), Promotion (how do I show it?), People (who in my circle should know?).
- Attention vs Trust — List three accounts you follow. For each, mark whether you mostly give them attention or trust. What is the difference?
- Your Audience Math — Open your most-used platform. Look at the comments on your last three posts. How many are from real people you can name or place? How many are accounts you do not recognize? What does that say about your trust level?
- The Yes/No Filter — Write a simple two-sentence rule that helps you decide what offers to say yes to and what to pass on.
- The Reframe — Finish this sentence: "I am not just an athlete. I am also ___, which means I need to start ___."

**Quiz**

1. What is marketing in plain language?
   - [ ] Tricking people into buying
   - [ ] Posting every day on social media
   - [✓] Helping the right people know, trust, and choose you
   - [ ] Running paid ads
2. What is the difference between attention and trust?
   - [ ] There is none
   - [✓] Attention is eyes on a post; trust is weight on your words
   - [ ] Trust comes from a follower count
   - [ ] Attention always leads to trust
3. Why is "people" added to the marketing list?
   - [ ] To make it five
   - [✓] Because real marketing happens through the people in your circle
   - [ ] To replace promotion
   - [ ] Because brands say so
4. A local business pays an athlete with 2,500 trusted followers more than one with 80,000 random followers. Why?
   - [ ] The smaller athlete has worse content
   - [ ] The smaller athlete is closer in age
   - [✓] Trust converts; reach without trust does not
   - [ ] The bigger athlete is more expensive
5. Who is responsible for marketing your career as an athlete?
   - [ ] Your coach
   - [ ] Your school
   - [ ] A future agent only
   - [✓] You

**Reflection**

Answer in full sentences. 1) What is one part of the five Ps you have been ignoring, and what is it costing you? 2) If a brand asked you today, "What can you offer us?", what would your real answer be?

## Module 3 — Your Story Is Your First Asset

**Objective**

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

- Identify the parts of your real story that brands and audiences care about
- Tell the difference between a backstory and a brand story
- Use your background to set yourself apart in a crowded space
- Share your story without sounding fake or scripted
- Build a short story that you can repeat in any setting

**Core Concept**

Every athlete has a story. Most never tell theirs. They assume it is not interesting enough. They assume only the top recruits and viral players have stories worth hearing. That is wrong.

Your story is your first asset. It is the thing nobody else can copy. Two athletes can run the same time, score the same points, or play the same position. They cannot have the same story. That is your edge.

A story is not the same as a backstory. A backstory is everything that happened to you. A brand story is the small set of facts and values that explain why you do what you do, in a way other people can repeat.

Backstory: "I grew up in a small town. My mom worked two jobs. I played three sports. I broke my ankle sophomore year and almost quit. I came back stronger. I went to community college first because of grades. Now I am at a Division I program."

Brand story: "I am the small-town walk-on who almost quit, finished community college, and now plays D1. I do this for everyone told they were not enough."

The second one is shorter and stronger. It hits the parts that move people. It is also easy to repeat. A brand can use it. A reporter can use it. A teammate can use it. You can use it without feeling like you are bragging.

A strong brand story has four parts.

1. The Where. Where you come from. Town, school, family, neighborhood, faith, culture. This grounds you.

2. The Why. Why you play. Why you train. Why you get up early. "I just love the game" is weak. "I play because my younger sister sat in the stands every weekend and I am the first in my family to get this far" is strong.

3. The Hard. Something you had to get through. Injury. Setback. Doubt. Loss. Being cut. Being overlooked. The hard part is what makes your story human. Without it, you sound like a robot.

4. The Where Next. What you are working toward, beyond the game. A career. A business. A community. A goal. A degree. This is what tells people your story is going somewhere.

There is a common trap with stories. Athletes try to make their story bigger than it is. They reach for drama that is not there. Audiences see through that fast. Tell the truth. The truth is enough.

Another trap is going small. Athletes downplay their story because they think it is boring. The college tennis player who grew up training on a single public court. The volleyball player who is the first in her family to go to college. Those are good stories. You do not need a movie ending.

The last idea is reps. A story gets stronger every time you tell it. The first time you say it out loud, it might feel awkward. The tenth time, you have found your phrasing. The hundredth time, it lands. Athletes who can tell their story in 30 seconds, in two minutes, and in an hour are athletes who can sit in any room and hold their own.

**Scenario**

Imani is a sophomore on a Division III track team. She runs the 800. She is not the fastest in the conference. She is not getting recruited by sponsors. Her account has 1,100 followers. She thinks her story is not worth telling.

Her story, in her own words, sounds like this: "I run track. I am studying biology. I want to be a physical therapist."

That is not a story. That is a bio.

A communications professor pushes her to dig deeper. Over a few weeks, this is what comes out.

Imani's older sister was born with a condition that affected her legs. She had surgeries through her childhood. She works with a physical therapist three times a week. Imani started running because her sister could not. Every race is a way to use the body her sister was not given. She wants to be a physical therapist because she watched the one her sister sees change her life.

Now look at her story with the four parts.
- The Where: A small school. A close family. A sister with a physical condition.
- The Why: She runs for her sister.
- The Hard: She is not the fastest. She gets passed by faster runners every week.
- The Where Next: She wants to be a physical therapist who works with kids.

That is a story. It is short. It is true. It is hers.

A local rehab clinic sees one of her posts about her sister and reaches out. They want her to be the face of a community awareness campaign. They pay her $600 and offer her an internship for the next summer. The internship turns into a job offer after graduation.

Imani never had to fake anything. She just stopped hiding the part of her life that already mattered.

The lesson is not that every athlete has a sister with a medical condition. The lesson is that every athlete has a real reason they show up. You just have to find yours and learn to say it out loud.

**Student Action**

- Brain Dump — Write down at least ten things from your life that shaped why you play your sport. Do not edit. Do not judge. Just write.
- The Four Parts — Pick the strongest items from your brain dump. Fill in The Where (where I come from), The Why (why I play), The Hard (what I had to get through), The Where Next (what I am working toward).
- The 30-Second Version — Write your story in 30 seconds out loud. About 60 to 80 words. Read it. Say it out loud. Rewrite until it sounds like you, not a press release.
- The Two-Minute Version — Write a longer version. About 250 words. Use the four parts. Add one moment that shows the hard part, not just tells about it.
- The Honesty Test — Show your 30-second version to one person who knows you well. Ask: "Does this sound like the real me?" If they pause, rewrite.

**Quiz**

1. What is the difference between a backstory and a brand story?
   - [ ] Nothing
   - [✓] A backstory is everything that happened; a brand story is the parts that matter and can be repeated
   - [ ] A backstory is for older athletes
   - [ ] A brand story has to be made up
2. Which is NOT a part of a strong brand story?
   - [ ] The Where
   - [ ] The Why
   - [ ] The Hard
   - [✓] The Follower Count
3. Why is "the hard" part important?
   - [ ] It makes you look weak
   - [✓] It makes you human and gives the audience a reason to care
   - [ ] It is required by NIL rules
   - [ ] It impresses sponsors
4. What is the biggest risk of stretching the truth in your story?
   - [ ] Going viral
   - [ ] Looking too humble
   - [✓] Losing trust when people see through it
   - [ ] Getting paid less
5. Why is repetition important for your story?
   - [ ] It tricks people into believing you
   - [✓] It helps you find the version that sounds like you, not a script
   - [ ] It makes you forget the truth
   - [ ] It gets you more followers

**Reflection**

Answer in full sentences. 1) What part of your story have you been hiding because you thought it was not interesting? 2) What is one place this week where you could practice telling your 30-second story out loud?

## Module 4 — Understanding Your Audience

**Objective**

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

- Identify the different audiences who pay attention to you
- Explain why different audiences value different things
- Pick the audiences that matter most for your goals
- Adjust how you talk to each audience without changing who you are
- Build a clearer picture of who your real audience is

**Core Concept**

Every athlete has more than one audience. Most never stop to think about who is actually watching.

An audience is the specific group of people who pay attention to you and trust what you say. That is the whole definition. Pay attention. Trust your word. If they do both, they are part of your real audience. If they only scroll past you in a feed, they are not.

You do not have one audience. You have several. They overlap, but they are not the same. The athletes who win at marketing know which audience they are talking to in each moment.

Your audiences can include:

Fans of your sport. People who follow the game itself and notice you as part of it. They care about stats, highlights, and team success.

Your school's community. Students, alumni, faculty, and parents. They care about pride in the program and about how you represent the school.

Your hometown. The local store owner, your high school coach, family friends, classmates from middle school. They care about pride in where you come from.

Local businesses. Small companies in the area around your school. They care about reaching the people in their zip code. They often have real budgets for athletes who can reach those people.

National brands. Big companies. They care about reach and story. They look for athletes who can move attention at scale or who fit a specific identity.

Future employers and partners. People who are watching for who you will be after sports. They care about how you carry yourself.

Recruits and younger athletes. Kids who look up to you. They care about who you are as a person, not just a player.

Media. Reporters and content creators who tell stories about your sport. They care about story, access, and personality.

Each one of these audiences is paying attention for a different reason. The biggest mistake athletes make is treating them all the same. They post the same thing for every group and wonder why nothing lands.

This does not mean you have to be a different person for each audience. The core of who you are stays the same. What changes is what you put first.

You cannot speak to all audiences at once. You have to choose. If your goal is local sponsorships and community impact, focus on hometown and school audiences. If your goal is to build a national brand, focus on the audiences that have national reach. If you have no goal, you will end up scattered.

Most athletes only talk. The athletes who build real audiences listen. They read the comments. They notice who is showing up. They watch what gets a reaction. Listening tells you who your actual audience is, which is sometimes different from who you think it is.

**Scenario**

Trey is a senior offensive lineman at a Power Four program. He has 18,000 followers. He has never had a real NIL deal.

His feed is a mix. Workout clips. Game day photos. Some humor. A few posts about his hometown. He thinks his lack of deals is because he plays a position that nobody pays attention to.

His sports information director sits him down and asks one question. "Who is your audience, really?"

Trey says, "Football fans."

The SID pulls up his data. Out of his 18,000 followers, here is what they actually look like.
- 38 percent are from his hometown area, two hours from campus
- 28 percent are students or alumni from his current school
- 14 percent are youth football players and their parents in his region
- 12 percent are casual football fans
- 8 percent are random accounts and bots

Trey is not talking to football fans. He has a deep local audience and a regional youth football audience. He never knew, because he never looked.

A local insurance agency, a regional truck dealership, and a youth football camp all reach out within a month after his SID quietly shares his real audience info with a few partners. The local insurance agency pays him $800. The truck dealership offers a four-month deal at $700 a month. The youth football camp offers a partnership where he gets a percentage of every registration that uses his code.

None of those brands cared about his stats. They cared about who was already paying attention to him. Trey did not need more followers. He needed to understand the ones he had.

**Student Action**

- List Your Audiences — Write down the eight audience types from the core concept. For each one, write Yes or No based on whether they are part of your current audience.
- Rank Your Top Three — Out of the audiences you marked Yes, pick the three most important for the next twelve months. Write one sentence for each saying why.
- Check Your Real Audience — Look at the comments and likes on your last five posts. Which audiences are showing up? Which are missing that you thought were there?
- One Message Per Audience — For each of your top three audiences, write one sentence that speaks directly to them. Same core values. Different angle.
- The Listening Habit — Pick one habit you will keep this season. Reading every comment for the first hour after a post. Asking three followers a month how they found you. Pick one and commit.

**Quiz**

1. What is an audience?
   - [ ] Anyone who follows you online
   - [ ] People at your games
   - [✓] The specific group of people who pay attention to you and trust what you say
   - [ ] Your team
2. Why do different audiences care about different things?
   - [ ] They do not
   - [✓] Because each group is paying attention for a different reason
   - [ ] Because they are different ages
   - [ ] Because of the sport
3. A small local audience that trusts you can be worth more than a big distracted one. Why?
   - [ ] Local people are louder
   - [✓] Trusted audiences actually act when you say something
   - [ ] Big audiences cost more to run
   - [ ] Local audiences post more
4. What is the risk of trying to talk to every audience at once?
   - [ ] You go viral
   - [✓] You speak for everyone and connect with no one
   - [ ] You build more trust
   - [ ] You make more money
5. What is "listening" in marketing?
   - [ ] Going to live games
   - [✓] Watching what gets a real reaction and noticing who is showing up
   - [ ] Reading other athletes' posts
   - [ ] Replying to every DM

**Reflection**

Answer in full sentences. 1) Which audience do you spend the most time talking to right now, and is that the audience you actually want? 2) Which of your audiences have you been ignoring that could matter most in the next year?

## Module 5 — Content Without Feeling Like An Influencer

**Objective**

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

- Make content that fits who you are without faking a personality
- Choose lanes that are sustainable in season and out
- Avoid the most common content mistakes athletes make
- Use simple formats that work even when you are busy
- Stay consistent without burning out

**Core Concept**

A lot of athletes resist content. They do not want to look fake. They do not want to look thirsty. They do not want to be one of "those" people who post all the time.

Good news. You do not have to be.

The point of content is not to become an influencer. It is to make your existing life visible. You already work. You already train. You already live. Content is just choosing what parts of that to share, in a way that fits who you are.

Strong content for athletes is not about adding something fake to your life. It is about showing the real parts of your life that already exist.

Common content lanes that work for almost every athlete. You do not need all of them. Pick the two or three that feel natural.

1. Training. Workouts, drills, film study, recovery. People who follow your sport want to see how it gets done.

2. Game Day. Walking out of the tunnel. Pre-game routines. After the final whistle. The lockerroom. People love the stuff cameras usually miss.

3. Lessons. Something you learned this week. A piece of advice from your coach. A mistake you fixed. You only have to be one chapter ahead of someone else.

4. Community. Where you grew up. A youth camp visit. A teacher who shaped you. Anything that connects you to the people around you.

5. Identity. Faith. Family. Culture. Causes. Used carefully, this lane builds the deepest connection.

6. Off the Clock. Hobbies. Food. Music. Fits. Friends. Used in small doses, this shows you are a real person.

7. Behind the Numbers. Stats are boring. Stories about stats are not. "First start of the season. Three years of work to get here." That is content.

Pocket formats are the ones you can run from a locker room, a hotel, or a car ride. A 15-second clip with a one-line caption. A photo with a short story behind it. A voice over a B-roll clip. A simple repost from a teammate with your own thought added. A weekly highlight summary that takes ten minutes to make.

The bar is not "professional content." The bar is "real and clear."

Pick a rhythm you can actually hold. Twice a week, three times a week, once a week. Whatever you can do for six months without burning out.

Trends are tempting because they get fast attention. But chasing every trend turns your feed into a copy of everyone else's. Use trends only when they fit who you already are. If a trend would make you cringe in real life, skip it.

You can say no to content. You are allowed to keep parts of your life private. You do not owe an audience access to your relationships, your family pain, or your worst days. Strong content does not mean total transparency. It means honest sharing on your terms.

**Scenario**

Camille is a junior on a Division I gymnastics team. She has 4,800 followers. She is in season. Practice runs three hours a day. She has labs, a part-time tutoring job, and class.

She knows content matters. But every time she opens her phone to post, it feels fake. She watches her teammates film three-take videos with trendy audio and thinks, "That is not me."

So she does nothing. Weeks go by with no posts.

A coach passes her a simple plan. She is going to post twice a week for six weeks. That is it. No more. Two posts. One has to be from training. One has to be a lesson or a thought.

Camille thinks twice a week is nothing. But then she tries to make her first training post and realizes she has to find time to actually film. She gets it done. A short clip of a beam routine, with one line of caption about the part she is still trying to master.

Six weeks later:
- A local PT clinic offers her a deal because they like that her posts show training, not just highlights.
- A community center invites her to lead a free clinic for young gymnasts. The clinic gets local press.
- Her account passes 9,000 followers without her chasing any trends.
- More importantly, she does not hate posting anymore.

The reason it worked was not that her content was great. It was that her content was real, on a schedule she could actually keep.

She did not become an influencer. She became an athlete whose work was visible.

## Module 6 — NIL, Sponsorships, And Brand Deals

**Objective**

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

- Define what NIL actually means in plain language
- Explain what brands really buy when they sign athletes
- Evaluate whether a deal fits you before signing
- Spot common types of NIL deals and what each one is worth
- Avoid the most common mistakes athletes make in early deals

**Core Concept**

NIL stands for Name, Image, and Likeness. It is the rule that lets college athletes get paid for the use of who they are. Your name on a jersey. Your image on a sign. Your face on a poster. Any work that uses you as the person, not the player, can be paid.

NIL is not pay to play. A booster cannot pay you to commit to a school or to play a certain number of minutes. NIL is payment for real work. A post. An appearance. A video. A camp. A speaking gig. An endorsement.

A real NIL deal has these parts.

1. A real business purpose. A company wants something from you. Content. Attention. Endorsement. Access.

2. A fair market rate. You get paid an amount that matches what your work is worth.

3. A written contract. What you do. What they pay. When. For how long. What rights they get. What happens if either side does not deliver.

4. Disclosure when required. Under current rules, deals worth $600 or more from third parties must be reported through the NIL Go system.

Most athletes think brands buy follower counts. They do not. Brands buy six things.

1. Attention. Eyes on a post, a sign, or an appearance.
2. Trust. The chance that what you say will move someone to act.
3. Story. Your background and what you stand for.
4. Access. A way into a community they could not reach on their own.
5. Association. The reflected glow of being connected to you and what you stand for.
6. Content. The actual photo, video, or post they can use.

Common types of deals:

Social posts. Pay for one or more posts on your channels. Usually short timeline. Watch for usage rights and exclusivity.

Appearances. Show up at a store, an event, or a camp. Sign autographs, take photos, speak. Usually paid by the hour or by the event.

Content rights. A brand pays to use your image or video in their own ads. This often pays more than just a post on your channel, because the brand controls how it gets used.

Camps and clinics. You run or co-run an event. You get a flat fee, a percentage, or both.

Affiliate or referral. You share a code or link. You get a piece of every sale or sign-up.

Long-term partnerships. Multi-month or multi-year deals. Bigger money. More commitment. Watch for exclusivity clauses.

Equity or product deals. You get a piece of the company or free product. Useful when the brand is small and growing, but only if you believe in it.

The biggest mistake new athletes make is taking deals that do not match their real life. They post a fast food brand they never eat. They wear gear from a company that does not fit their values. The audience smells it.

A simple test. Ask yourself, "If this deal did not exist, would I still use this product or talk about this brand?" If the answer is no, you are about to weaken your trust for short money.

**Scenario**

Marcus is a sophomore basketball player at a mid-major. He has 6,200 followers. He is averaging eight points off the bench. He has gotten two small NIL offers, both for free product.

Then two offers land in the same week.

Offer A. A local energy drink brand. They want one post a month for three months. They will pay him $400 a month and give him free product. Total: $1,200 plus product.

Offer B. A national supplement company. They want Marcus to be part of a campaign with fifteen other college athletes. They want unlimited use of his image, name, and content for one year. They are paying him $4,000 flat, paid at the end of the year.

The bigger number looks better. Marcus is ready to sign Offer B.

Before he does, his older sister, who has worked in marketing, asks him three questions.
- Do you actually drink the supplements they sell?
- What does "unlimited use" mean in their contract?
- Is the deal exclusive, and if so, what does that block you from doing?

Marcus does not know any of the answers. He goes back and reads. Here is what he finds.
- The national supplement is one he has never used. The taste, in his honest opinion, is bad.
- "Unlimited use" means they can put his face on any ad, billboard, or website for the next year.
- The deal is exclusive. He cannot sign with any other supplement, drink, or nutrition brand for the year. Including the energy drink brand that offered him $1,200.

Suddenly the comparison is different.

If Marcus signs Offer B, he loses Offer A and any chance to sign other beverage or nutrition brands for a year. He gets paid at the end, not along the way. He has to promote a product he does not use.

He goes with Offer A. A year later, he has signed three local deals worth $3,600 total. He has built a name in his city. He has a clean record. The national brand he passed on stopped using most of their fifteen athletes after six months.

The lesson is not that big deals are bad. The right deal is not always the biggest one.

**Student Action**

- Define Your Real NIL — In your own words, write what an NIL deal is and what it is not. Use two short sentences.
- What Are You Selling — From the six things brands buy (attention, trust, story, access, association, content), pick the top two you can offer right now. Write one sentence for each.
- Brand Fit Test — List three brands you would be proud to work with. For each: Do I actually use or believe in this? Does my audience care? Would I post about this for free?
- Decode A Deal — If you have had an offer (real or hypothetical), answer: What type of deal is this? Is there exclusivity? What rights am I giving the brand? When and how do I get paid?
- The Walk-Away Number — Write your own rule for when you will walk away from a deal. Example: "I walk away if the brand is something I would never use in real life, no matter the money."

## Module 7 — Pricing, Pitching, And Negotiating

**Objective**

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

- Set a starting price that reflects your real value
- Build a simple media kit you can send to any brand
- Pitch a local business in plain language
- Ask better questions in any negotiation
- Avoid the most common pricing mistakes athletes make

**Core Concept**

Most athletes either price themselves too low or too high. Both come from the same problem. They are guessing.

You do not have to guess. Pricing comes from three pieces of information. What you can deliver. What the market pays for similar work. What the brand needs from you.

Step 1: Know What You Deliver. Before you put a price on anything, list what you are actually doing. One post on Instagram? One post and one story? One appearance for two hours? One video that the brand can use in ads? A campaign of three posts over a month?

Step 2: Know Your Range. There is no perfect formula. But there are ballparks. The numbers below are starting points for athletes at most college levels.

- Local social post for a small business: $100 to $500
- Local appearance, two hours: $150 to $750
- Multi-post campaign for a regional brand: $500 to $2,500
- National brand single post: $1,000 to $10,000 plus, depending on reach
- Camp or clinic, day rate: $300 to $2,000

These are guides. Your real range is shaped by your audience, your fit, and what the brand needs.

Step 3: Build A Simple Media Kit. A media kit is one or two pages that explain who you are, what you can offer, and what it costs. A simple media kit has:
- A clean photo of you
- Your sport, school, year, and position
- Your story in 100 words
- Your audience numbers (followers, average likes, average views)
- The audiences that follow you (location, age range, interests)
- What you offer (posts, appearances, content rights, partnerships)
- Past brands you have worked with, if any
- A short list of starting rates or a "rates on request" line
- A way to contact you

A pitch is when you reach out to a brand instead of waiting for them to find you. Most athletes never do this. They wait.

A good pitch has three parts.
- A real reason you are reaching out. Not a fake compliment. Something specific.
- A clear offer. What you can do for them. What it costs. What they get back.
- An easy next step. A short reply, a phone call, a one-question response.

Example pitch for a local restaurant: "Hi, I'm Sara Lopez, a softball player at State. I've been eating at your place since freshman year. I noticed you do a special on game days but I haven't seen it promoted around campus much. I have around 4,500 followers, most of them students and softball families in the area. I could do a post and a story on the next home game day for $250, and you can use the content in your own ads after. Open to a quick call this week?"

Negotiating is not arguing. It is asking better questions until both sides are clear. Five questions that change negotiations:
- "What is your budget for this kind of campaign?"
- "What does success look like for you on this?"
- "Is this exclusive, and if so, what does it block me from?"
- "Who else are you working with for this?"
- "When and how will payment happen?"

The worst mistake is pricing yourself too low because you are scared the brand will walk. When you charge too little, you set a price ceiling for yourself that is hard to raise later, and the brand often values the work less.

**Scenario**

Aisha is a junior on a Division II volleyball team. She has 3,800 followers. She has had one paid deal so far, $75 for a single post.

She wants to do better. She decides to try pitching local businesses.

She builds a one-page media kit in Canva over a weekend. Her story. Her audience. What she offers. Three starting prices. Her contact info.

She lists ten local businesses near her school. A juice bar. A salon. A boutique. A coffee shop. A pet store. A bike shop. A bookstore. A car wash. A pizza place. A gym.

She sends ten short pitches. Two days work, total.

Here is what comes back.
- Six no responses.
- Two polite no thank yous.
- One "send more info" that goes nowhere.
- One yes.

The yes is from the juice bar. The owner is a former college athlete. He likes that her pitch is short and specific. He offers her $300 for two posts and an in-person appearance on a Saturday. She counters by asking for $400 and a small percent off a code her followers can use. He agrees to $350 with the code.

That one deal is more than four times her last one.

She also gets a lesson. Out of ten cold pitches, she got one deal. Pitching is a numbers game. The next month she sends twelve more. She gets two more deals.

By the end of the season she has earned about $1,800 in NIL income from local pitches alone. The deals do not always come find you. Sometimes you have to go find them.

**Student Action**

- Build Your Starter Media Kit — In a single page document, fill in: photo, name/sport/school/year, 100-word story, audience numbers, audience location and types, what you offer, starting rates, contact info.
- Pick Five Local Businesses — List five local businesses that match your real life. You eat there. You shop there. You believe in them.
- Write One Pitch — Write a single pitch you would send to one of those businesses. Keep it under 120 words. Use the three parts (real reason, clear offer, easy next step).
- Practice The Five Questions — Write the five negotiating questions in your own words. Read them out loud until you can ask them without hesitating.
- Set Your Floor — Write your "no lower than" rate for: a single social post, a two-hour appearance, a one-month campaign. You can raise it later. You cannot raise a number you already accepted.

   - [ ] A team uniform
   - [ ] A media interview

   - [ ] Charging too much
   - [ ] Refusing free product
   - [✓] Charging too little out of fear
   - [ ] Asking questions

   - [ ] "Will you pay me more if I post twice?"
   - [✓] "What is your budget for this kind of campaign?"

   - [ ] "Why did you pick me?"

## Module 8 — Protecting Your Name, Money, And Eligibility

**Objective**

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

- Spot red flags in a deal before you sign
- Understand the basics of disclosure and reporting
- Recognize the most common ways athletes lose money in NIL
- Know when to slow down and ask for help
- Build a simple checklist that protects your name and your future

**Core Concept**

Bad marketing is not just bad deals. Bad marketing is the deal that gets you fined, suspended, or sued. It is the contract you signed without reading. It is the post that costs you a scholarship. It is the agent who took 30 percent for doing nothing.

Three things you have to protect. Your name. Your money. Your eligibility.

Your name is your reputation. Once it is connected to a product, a brand, or a moment, it sticks. Protecting your name means thinking about every public action like it is going to be searchable in ten years. Because it is.

Your money is the dollars you earn from NIL. Money you do not protect is money you lose. To bad contracts. To taxes you did not plan for. To agents and managers who take too much. To products that never paid you. To deals that paid in product instead of cash.

Your eligibility is your right to keep playing college sports. Most current NIL rules are written so that real, fair deals do not threaten your eligibility. But you still have to follow the rules. Disclose deals when required. Get school compliance involved when needed. Avoid deals that look like pay to play.

The most common red flags in a deal:

1. No written contract. If someone wants to pay you but does not want anything in writing, walk away.
2. Pressure to sign fast. "We need an answer today." That is almost always a trap.
3. Vague payment terms. When you get paid, how, what triggers payment. If it is not clear, it will not happen.
4. Wide usage rights. "We can use your image anywhere, forever, in any way." That is a giveaway, not a deal.
5. Hidden exclusivity. A clause buried in the contract that says you cannot work with any similar brand.
6. Auto-renew clauses. Contracts that quietly renew unless you cancel by a specific date.
7. Penalty clauses that only hurt you. Big fines if you miss a post, with no matching responsibilities on the brand side.
8. Cash up front for a school commitment. This is pay to play. Run.
9. An agent or manager who wants a long, exclusive contract right away.
10. A brand that asks you to deceive your audience. Pressuring you to skip the "ad" or "sponsored" label is illegal in many cases. Always disclose paid promotions.

Under current rules, certain third-party NIL deals worth $600 or more from outside your school have to be reported through the NIL Go system before they are completed. If you are getting paid by anyone other than your school for using your name, image, or likeness, talk to your school's compliance office.

Money rules that protect your dollars:
- Treat NIL income like a small business. Track every dollar earned and every related expense.
- Set aside 20 to 30 percent of every dollar for taxes. NIL income is taxable.
- Use a separate bank account if you can.
- Read every contract before you sign. If you do not understand it, get someone you trust to read it with you.
- Never pay to get into a deal. A real brand pays you. They do not charge you for "exposure."

The skill is not memorizing every eligibility rule. The skill is the habit of checking first. Your compliance office, a trusted advisor, and your team's rules are all faster than guessing. When in doubt, ask first.

**Scenario**

Jordan is a freshman on a Division I baseball team. A man he has never met messages him on Instagram. The man says he runs a marketing agency. He offers to "make Jordan rich" through NIL deals. All Jordan has to do is sign a one-year exclusive deal with the agency for 25 percent of all his income.

The contract is sent through email. It is 14 pages. It includes language that is hard to follow. Jordan is excited. He almost signs that night.

Two things stop him.

First, his older cousin asks him a few questions.
- Have you met this person?
- Does he have a track record with athletes you can check?
- Why does he need a one-year exclusive on day one?

Jordan does not know the answers.

Second, Jordan reads section eight of the contract. It says the agency keeps 25 percent of all NIL income for one year, plus 10 percent of any deal that "originates during the term" even after the contract ends. So a deal that lands in his ninth month would still pay the agency 10 percent for years after.

Jordan asks the agency for a thirty-day trial period. They say no. He asks if he can have a lawyer review it. They say "we move fast around here." He asks for a list of athletes they currently work with. They send three names. Jordan reaches out. None of them respond. One of the accounts has not posted in seven months.

Jordan passes.

Two weeks later, Jordan's compliance office runs a meeting on common NIL scams. The agency Jordan almost signed with comes up in a slide. The compliance officer says three other freshmen at the school got the same pitch. None of them should sign.

The lesson is not that all agencies are bad. There are real, helpful ones. The lesson is that the best protection is slowing down. The people who try to rush you are almost never working for you.

**Student Action**

- Find Your Compliance Office — Look up the name, email, and office hours of your school's NIL or compliance contact. Save it in your phone. If your school has a written NIL policy, download it.
- Build Your Red Flag List — Pick the five red flags that scare you the most. Write them in your own words. Save them somewhere you can pull up quickly when a deal lands.
- Tax Setup — Pick the percentage of NIL income you will set aside for taxes. Most people start at 25 percent. Decide where it will go (a separate account, savings folder). Decide today, not the day you get paid.
- The Contract Read Habit — Commit: "I will never sign any contract on the same day I receive it." Twenty-four hour minimum.
- The Help List — Make a list of three people you will call before signing anything. A family member, a friend in business school, your compliance officer, a coach. Anyone who is on your side.

## Module 9 — Building A Personal Marketing System

**Objective**

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

- Build a weekly routine that fits around practice, class, and games
- Set a small number of marketing goals you will actually keep
- Use simple tools to track posts, deals, and outreach
- Avoid burnout by reducing the work to its smallest useful form
- Adjust your system when the season changes

**Core Concept**

There is a difference between doing marketing and having a system.

Doing marketing means posting when you remember. Replying when you feel like it. Saying yes when a deal shows up. Forgetting about everything else when finals hit.

Having a system means the work happens whether you feel like it or not. Not because you are a robot. Because the routine is small enough to survive a hard week.

Small things done often beat big things done rarely. The system has three parts.

1. Goals. Two or three for the year. No more.
2. Routines. Weekly habits that move you toward the goals.
3. Tracking. A simple way to know whether the routines are working.

Goals. Most athletes set zero marketing goals or twenty. Both lead to nothing. The right number is two or three. Each one should be specific.

Bad goal: "Grow my audience." Better goal: "Add 3,000 followers this season by posting twice a week."
Bad goal: "Get NIL deals." Better goal: "Land three local deals worth at least $1,500 total by the end of the school year."
Bad goal: "Be a thought leader." Better goal: "Speak at two events related to my sport before I graduate."

Routines. A weekly habit, not a daily one. Daily is too much when you are in season. Weekly is doable. A solid athlete marketing routine has four pieces.

- Content time. One block, 30 to 60 minutes a week, to make and schedule posts.
- Outreach time. One block, 15 to 30 minutes a week, to send a pitch or follow up on one.
- Listening time. One block, 15 minutes a week, to read comments, DMs, and check who is showing up.
- Pause time. Five minutes at the end of the week to look at what worked and what did not.

That is two hours a week, on the high end. The trick is to put it on the calendar. Same day, same time, every week. If it is not on the calendar, it will not happen.

Tracking. You do not need a fancy spreadsheet. A simple list with three columns: what I posted this week, outreach I did this week, what worked and what did not. Update it Sunday night. Five minutes. After three months, look back. You will see patterns you missed in the moment.

Season versus off-season. In season, your time is tight. Cut the system to the bone. One post a week. Pause outreach. Keep listening. Keep tracking. In off-season, expand. Two or three posts a week. More pitching. The system flexes.

The smallest useful unit. When you are tired, do not skip the system. Cut it to the smallest useful version. One post. One pitch. One look at comments. One note. The system survives because the floor is low.

**Scenario**

Marcus is a senior football player at a Power Four. He has had a great career on the field. He has had no plan off it.

For four years he has posted when something good happened in a game. He has taken every NIL deal that came in. He has never tracked anything. He has never reached out to anyone.

He looks up senior year. His audience is 22,000. His total NIL earnings over four years are around $11,000. He realizes that for his level and sport, that number should have been much higher.

He sits down and builds a system for his last year.

Goals:
- Add 8,000 followers by graduation.
- Land at least $25,000 in NIL income in the final season.
- Speak at three career events for high school athletes in his region.

Routines:
- Sunday night, 45 minutes, content prep and scheduling.
- Wednesday afternoon, 20 minutes, send or follow up on a pitch.
- Friday, 10 minutes, check comments and DMs.
- Sunday again, 5 minutes, weekly note.

Tracking: A simple list in his phone notes. Three columns. Updated every Sunday.

He sticks with it. The first month is rough. By month two, the routine is set. By month four, he has hit two of his three goals and is on track for the third. By the time he graduates, he has earned more in his final year than in the previous three combined.

The biggest change was not effort. It was structure. He worked the same hours, with a system, and the results compounded. Without a system, you get random results. With a system, you get a record.

**Student Action**

- Set Two To Three Goals — Write down two or three marketing goals for the next twelve months. Be specific. Use numbers.
- Schedule Your Routines — Look at your real calendar (practice, class, travel, recovery). Find the four routine slots (content, outreach, listening, pause). Put them on your calendar with reminders.
- Pick A Tracker — Pick one simple way to track. A note app. A doc. A whiteboard. Three columns: what I posted, what I sent out, what I noticed.
- Set Your Floor — Decide what the smallest useful version of your system looks like. The version you can do even on a bad week. Example: one post, one pitch, one look at comments, one note.
- Pick An Off-Ramp — Decide one thing you will quit doing because it is not part of the system (checking your follower count daily, answering every DM, chasing every trend).

   - [ ] Twenty or more

   - [ ] Zero
   - [ ] As many as possible

   - [ ] Daily and intense
   - [✓] Weekly with set blocks
   - [ ] Whenever you feel like it
   - [ ] Once a season

   - [ ] A full agency

## Module 10 — Life After The Jersey

**Objective**

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

- Connect your marketing skills to your life after sports
- Identify the transferable skills you are already building as an athlete
- Build a bridge from athlete identity to a career, business, or community role
- Use your network and reputation while it is hottest
- Make decisions today that protect your value for decades

**Core Concept**

There is a hard truth at the center of college athletics. Most of you will not play professionally. Even the ones who do will eventually stop playing. The body has a clock. The career does too.

That is not a sad fact. It is just a fact. The athletes who do best are the ones who plan for the bridge.

The bridge is the move from athlete to whatever you are next. A professional player. A coach. An entrepreneur. A media person. A trainer. A nonprofit founder. A teacher. A lawyer. A nurse. A community leader. A regular professional doing regular work.

Marketing is one of the skills that builds the bridge. Marketing teaches you how to:
- Tell your story in a way people remember
- Understand audiences and what they need
- Build trust through consistency
- Pitch your ideas
- Negotiate fair deals
- Protect your reputation

Those are not just athlete skills. They are life skills. They show up in every job interview, every business meeting, every fundraising call, every speech, every networking conversation. You are building them now, whether you know it or not.

You already have skills most people would kill for.
- Discipline. You show up when you do not want to.
- Coaching. You give and take feedback.
- Performance under pressure. You have done it for years.
- Time management. You balance school, sport, and a life.
- Working with people who are not like you. Your team is diverse by definition.
- Recovery. You know how to come back from hard.

Brands and employers want all of those. The mistake most athletes make is assuming that everyone knows they have those skills. Nobody knows unless you say it. The translation is up to you.

Take this answer from a typical athlete interview: "I just love the game and I work hard."

Now compare it to: "I have spent four years balancing 25 hours a week of training, 18 credits a semester, and weekly travel. I have led groups, taken hard feedback, and made calls under pressure. Those habits don't end with my last game."

Same person. Same record. The second version translates.

Your network as an athlete is one of the most valuable things you have. Coaches. Teammates. Alumni. Donors. Local business owners who hosted you for an appearance. Every person you met through your sport is a potential door. The mistake is waiting until you need something to reach out. Start now. Send the thank-you message. Stay in touch. Show up to alumni events. Your network is hottest while your jersey is on. Use it before the lights dim.

Your brand outlives your eligibility. The audience you build, the story you tell, the trust you earn — none of it stops when your last game ends. The athletes who treat their college years as the foundation of a 30-year career build something that compounds. The ones who treat it as one final season leave a lot of value on the field.

The decisions you make today shape the next decade. The deals you sign. The way you handle a bad moment. The skills you practice. The relationships you nurture. None of it disappears when you graduate. It either becomes a runway or a regret.

**Scenario**

Two athletes finish college sport in the same year at the same school.

Athlete A played four seasons of basketball. Solid career. Never went pro. He treated his college years as just sport. He posted when he felt like it. He never built a media kit. He never reached out to a single business outside of the deals that came to him. When he graduated, he had a degree, a few highlight clips, and a vague plan to "get into business." Six months later, he was working a job he did not love, with no audience, no contacts, and no clear story about who he was.

Athlete B played four seasons of soccer. Never went pro either. But she treated her college years as the runway for whatever was next. She built a brand around women's sports media and coaching. By her senior year, her bio read "athlete, storyteller, future coach." She had pitched local businesses, run a small content series, and stayed in touch with three coaches she respected. She also built a relationship with a regional youth league through one of her NIL deals.

When she graduated, here is what showed up.
- The youth league offered her a part-time coaching role.
- A regional sports network hired her as a freelance contributor based on her content history.
- Two former teammates joined her on a podcast she launched her senior year.
- A local business owner she had pitched as a junior offered her a marketing role with a flexible schedule.

She turned her "after sport" career into her main career within 18 months. She did not need to be famous. She needed to be ready.

The lesson is not that everyone needs a podcast. The lesson is that the work you do now becomes the foundation for everything next. The athletes who plan for the bridge cross it. The ones who do not stand on the edge.
