# Rep1 Financial Literacy Curriculum (12-Week Classroom Program)

A 12-week, 60-minutes-per-session financial literacy course for Rep1 student-athletes, built in partnership with Wealthi. The first six weeks build the core concepts — saving, budgeting, investing, credit & inflation, financial decision-making, and financial strategy. The final six weeks apply those concepts under realistic pressure — adjusting a budget when income drops, when an unexpected expense appears, and when both happen at once — with students taking on more of the process independently each week, building to a capstone in Week 12. Every lesson follows the same structure: a pre-check, a concept explanation, a guided activity, an independent activity, a reflection, and a post-check.

---

## Week 1 — Saving
**Phase:** Foundation · **Theme:** Needs, Wants, and Why Saving Matters · **Session length:** 60 min
**Primary skill:** Distinguishing needs vs wants to support saving decisions. **Secondary skill:** Explaining why saving creates future options.

**What Students Practice:** Understanding the difference between needs and wants; making simple saving tradeoffs; thinking about future goals.

**Success Criteria:** Correctly classify needs vs wants. Explain why saving matters using a real example. Identify one personal saving tradeoff. Demonstrate understanding in both written work and assessment.

**Daily Pre-Check (8 min).** Teacher reads questions aloud; students answer individually: (1) What does saving mean in your own words? (2) Which is a need: your training shoes for practice or a new pair of headphones? (3) True or False: Saving means you never spend money. (4) You receive $20 in spending money at a tournament and want a $40 item at the merch table. What could saving help you do? (5) Name one thing you would like to save for. (6) True or False: Saving is only for adults.

*Teacher checkpoint: Listen for students confusing saving with hoarding or never spending.*

**Concept Explanation (10 min).** Teacher script, read aloud:

"Saving means setting money aside now so you can use it later. It does not mean the money disappears — it means you are choosing to protect it for a purpose. Saving gives you choices in the future that you would not have if you spent everything today."

"People save for three main reasons: emergencies, goals, and opportunities. An emergency is something unexpected — like needing to replace a piece of gear that breaks. A goal is something you are working toward — like paying your own way into a showcase or ID camp. An opportunity is something that comes up that you want to be ready for — like a last-minute chance to travel for a trial."

"Not everything you want is a need. Needs must be met first so wants can be enjoyed safely. A need is something required to live and train — like food, a place to stay, or the gear your team requires. A want is something optional — like a video game, a second pair of cleats you don't need yet, or extra takeout after practice."

"Here is an example: Imagine you have $20 of spending money at a weekend tournament. You could spend it all on snacks and merch today, or you could save $10 and still enjoy $10 now. The person who saves has $10 waiting for something better later. The person who spent everything has nothing left if something important comes up — like a bag strap breaking or needing tape for practice."

"Many people get this wrong because they think saving means giving things up permanently. That is not true. Saving is about timing — choosing when to use your money, not choosing never to use it."

"Think about the short-term versus the long-term. In the short term, saving might feel like you are missing out. In the long term, saving means you have options other athletes don't — the ability to cover a registration fee, a travel cost, or an emergency without asking anyone for help. The person who saves is the person who gets to choose."

"When you choose to save, you are becoming a person who plans ahead. You are not just reacting to what is in front of you — you are thinking about what comes next, whether that's next month's travel or next year's opportunities. That is the kind of decision-maker who builds a stronger future, on and off the field."

Board setup: NEEDS = required to live and train. WANTS = optional or extra. Closing line: "Saving is not about saying no forever. It is about saying yes later."

**Guided Activity — Needs vs Wants Sort (15 min).** Draw two columns (NEEDS / WANTS) on the board; students supply examples from their own routine one at a time — training gear, transportation to practice, food, versus streaming subscriptions, brand-name cleats they don't need yet, extra merch; class judges each with "Is this required to live and train?" Required prompts: "Could you still train without this?" / "Would this come before your gear or your travel to practice?"

*Forced tradeoff scenario:* "Imagine you have exactly $30 for the week. You need $20 for transportation to and from training. You also want a $15 jersey from a different brand than your team's. You cannot afford both." — "What are you giving up with this choice?" / "How does this affect you later — today, this weekend, and next week?"

*Consequence twist:* "Now imagine the jersey goes on sale next week for $10. If you saved the $10 this week, you could buy it later and still cover your transportation." — "Does this new information change your decision? Why?" / "Would you revise your original choice now that you know this?"

*Checkpoint: Ensure students are not labeling convenience items as needs; confirm they can articulate what they gain and lose with each choice.*

**Independent Activity — Saving Tradeoffs (15 min).** Write complete sentences, not single words: (1) List two personal needs and explain why each is required to live and train, not just convenient. (2) List two personal wants and explain what makes each optional rather than essential. (3) Choose one want you could reduce or delay; explain what you give up and what you gain by waiting, using "because." (4) Name one thing you could save for instead — a showcase fee, travel cost, or piece of gear — and why it matters to you personally. (5) Forward-looking statement: "If I save for this, in the future I will be able to ______ because ______."

Assessment checks: needs/wants correctly classified with reasoning; tradeoff realistic with "because" justification; personal connection explicit; forward-looking statement shows cause-and-effect.

**Reflection (7 min).** (1) Explain why saving is important using a specific example from your own life as an athlete — what would happen if you did not save in that situation? (2) Describe one habit that could help you save, and how it would change your future money decisions.

**Daily Post-Check (5 min).** (1) Define saving in one sentence, including what it allows that spending everything does not. (2) Give one difference between a need and a want, with an example from your own routine, and explain why it matters for saving. (3) "Because I save, I can ______." (4) True or False: Saving means giving up everything fun — explain. (5) Name one tradeoff identified today: what was given up, what was protected or gained.

**Common Misconceptions:**
- *"Saving means never spending money"* — Saving is about delaying spending, not eliminating it. The goal is intentional timing, not permanent restriction.
- *"Wants are bad"* — Wants are not bad, they are optional. Meet needs first, then choose wants wisely with what remains.
- *"Saving is only for adults"* — Anyone who receives or earns money can save, including athletes managing a stipend or allowance. Building the habit early gives more options and confidence over time.

**Continuity Bridge:** Next week, we build on saving by exploring where your money actually goes. You will learn how to track spending and spot patterns that either help or hurt your ability to save.

## Week 2 — Budgeting
**Phase:** Foundation · **Theme:** Planning Money Before It Is Spent · **Session length:** 60 min
**Primary skill:** Using a budget to make intentional tradeoffs that support priorities. **Secondary skill:** Adjusting financial plans when conditions change.

**What Students Practice:** Defining income, expenses, and budgets; allocating money across categories based on priorities; adjusting a budget when income changes.

**Success Criteria:** Correctly define income and expenses in their own words. Allocate money across categories based on priorities. Adjust a budget when income decreases and keep totals accurate. Explain at least one budgeting tradeoff and why it was necessary.

**Daily Pre-Check (8 min).** (1) What is income in your own words? (2) What is an expense? (3) What is one reason saving gives you more options later? (4) You receive $100 this month and want to save $20 — what must your budget help you do? (5) Name one thing athletes usually spend money on. (6) True or False: Budgets should never change.

*Checkpoint: Listen for students confusing income with savings, or thinking budgets are guesses rather than plans.*

**Concept Explanation (10 min).**

"A budget is a plan for how you use money before you spend it. Without a plan, money tends to disappear on things that do not matter to you. A budget puts you in control instead of letting spending control you."

"Income is the money you receive — from a job, an allowance, a family or host-family contribution, NIL income, or any other source. Expenses are how you use that money — transportation to training, food, gear, entertainment, or anything else you spend on."

"A budget helps you make sure your money supports what matters most to you. It is the difference between spending with purpose and spending by accident. Athletes who budget do not necessarily receive more money — they just use their money more intentionally."

"Every dollar should have a job, even if that job is saving for later. When money has no plan, it gets spent without direction. When money has a plan, it works for your goals — whether that's a showcase fee, a flight home, or gear for next season."

"Here is an example: Two athletes both receive $100 this month. Athlete A spends $60 on wants, $30 on needs, and has $10 left with no plan. Athlete B budgets $50 for needs, $20 for savings, and $30 for wants. Athlete B ends the month with $20 saved and all needs met. Athlete A may run out of money if something unexpected happens — like a piece of gear breaking before a big game."

"Many people get budgeting wrong because they think it means restricting fun. That is incorrect. Budgeting means making room for fun intentionally — so you can enjoy it without putting your training or travel needs at risk."

"In the short term, budgeting requires discipline. In the long term, budgeting creates freedom. The athlete who budgets today has more choices tomorrow. That is what separates someone who reacts to money problems from someone who prevents them."

Board setup: INCOME = money received. EXPENSES = money spent. BUDGET = a plan for money. Closing line: "Savings is the category that protects your future choices."

**Guided Activity — $100 Monthly Budget (15 min).** Write "Income: $100" and categories (Food, Transportation, Gear, Savings, Fun) on the board; class assigns all $100 across categories, confirming the total out loud. Required prompts: "If we add money here, where must it come from?" / "Which category protects future goals?" / "Which category could change first if money gets tight?"

*Forced tradeoff:* "The class wants to put $40 into Fun, but that only leaves $10 for Savings. Is that the right tradeoff?" — "What are you giving up by choosing more Fun?" / "How does putting only $10 in Savings affect you if something unexpected happens next month, like needing new cleats?"

*Consequence twist:* "Now imagine next month, your cleats tear and cost $30 to replace. If you only saved $10, what happens?" — "Would you change your budget now that you know this could happen?" / "Take 30 seconds — revise one category and explain why."

*Checkpoint: Ensure savings is not set to zero without a clear explanation; confirm students can articulate the cost of under-saving.*

**Independent Activity — Budget Change Scenario (15 min).** Scenario: "Your monthly income drops from $100 to $80 — maybe your hours at a part-time job get cut, or a family contribution shrinks for a month." Write complete sentences, show your thinking, check your totals: (1) Name two categories you would reduce and why each can afford to shrink ("because"). (2) Name one category you would keep the same and why protecting it matters more. (3) State the tradeoff — what you gave up, what you protected — connected to a real situation in your life as an athlete. (4) "If I follow this budget, in the future I will ______ because ______." (5) Final line: "My total budget equals $80."

Assessment checks: reductions realistic and justified; explanation reflects priorities/tradeoffs; personal connection explicit; forward-looking statement shows cause-and-effect; total equals exactly $80.

**Reflection (7 min).** (1) What was the hardest budgeting decision you made today, and what did you learn about priorities? (2) What does budgeting require from a person, and how will this skill affect future money decisions — including ones tied to your athletic career?

**Daily Post-Check (5 min).** (1) Define budgeting and the problem it solves. (2) What happens if expenses exceed income — give a specific example from an athlete's life. (3) You have $80 and want to save $20 — what must change, and what tradeoff does this create? (4) True or False: A budget gives you more control over money — explain using "because." (5) Name one budgeting tradeoff made today — what was given up, what was gained.

**Common Misconceptions:**
- *"A budget means you cannot spend money on fun"* — A budget makes room for fun intentionally rather than eliminating it. The difference is awareness, not restriction.
- *"Budgets are only for people who do not have enough money"* — Everyone benefits from a budget regardless of income; even athletes earning NIL money budget to maintain control.
- *"Once a budget is set, it should never change"* — Budgets are flexible tools that should be adjusted when income or expenses change; the ability to adjust is a sign of good financial thinking, not failure.

**Continuity Bridge:** Last week, we learned how saving creates future options. This week, we learned how budgeting protects those options by making tradeoffs. Next week, we will learn how money can grow through investing.

## Week 3 — Investing
**Phase:** Foundation · **Theme:** Ownership, Growth, and Patience · **Session length:** 60 min
**Primary skill:** Explaining how investing differs from saving and how ownership works. **Secondary skill:** Evaluating growth potential and risk using real-world examples.

**What Students Practice:** Defining investing as ownership, not gambling; differentiating investing from saving using time and risk; applying investor thinking to familiar companies.

**Success Criteria:** Define investing as ownership, not gambling. Explain how investing differs from saving using time and risk. Explain what ownership means when a company succeeds or struggles. Identify one potential benefit and one specific category of risk. Apply investor thinking to a familiar company using written explanation.

**Daily Pre-Check (8 min).** (1) What does investing mean in your own words? (2) True or False: Investing means gambling. (3) What is a stock? (4) If income is $100 and you want to save $20, what must your budget include? (5) Name one company or brand you recognize. (6) True or False: Investing always makes money.

*Checkpoint: Listen for students confusing investing with quick profits or guaranteed results.*

**Concept Explanation (10 min).**

"Saving and investing are not the same. They serve different purposes and work over different timeframes. Understanding the difference is one of the most important financial skills you can build."

"When you save money, you keep it safe for later. Your money does not grow much, but it is protected and available when you need it. Saving is best for short-term goals and emergencies — like covering a travel cost or a gear replacement."

"When you invest money, you buy ownership in something that could grow over time. A stock is a small piece of ownership in a company. If that company does well — if it earns more customers, sells more products, or builds something people love — then your ownership becomes more valuable."

"But ownership also means you share in the struggle. If a company loses customers, faces competition, or makes bad decisions, your investment can lose value. That is why investing involves risk — results are not guaranteed."

"Here is an example: Imagine you invest in the company that makes the training gear you and your teammates wear. If more athletes start buying that brand, the company grows and your investment grows. But if a new brand comes along and everyone switches, the company struggles and so does your investment."

"Many people get this wrong because they think investing is about getting rich quickly. That is gambling thinking, not investor thinking. Real investing is about patience — understanding that growth happens over months and years, not hours and days — the same patience it takes to develop as an athlete."

"In the short term, an investment might go up or down. In the long term, companies that solve real problems and serve real customers tend to grow. The person who invests with patience is making a fundamentally different decision than the person who gambles for quick wins. Investors are thinkers. They research, evaluate, and wait."

Board setup: SAVING = keeping money safe. INVESTING = owning part of a company. OWNERSHIP = shared success or struggle. RISK TYPES = customers, competition, change. Closing line: "Investing is about ownership over time, not quick wins."

**Guided Activity — Investor Thinking with Brands (15 min).** Students name gear, apparel, or nutrition brands they or their teammates use; teacher models investor thinking on one; class applies required prompts to each: "What does this company sell or do?" / "Why do athletes give this company money?" / "If this company does well, how do owners benefit?" / "If this company struggles, how do owners feel it?" / "Which risk category could affect this company most?"

*Forced tradeoff:* "You have $50 to invest. Company A makes a popular cleat right now but has one major competitor growing fast. Company B is less popular but has a loyal following of athletes who keep coming back. You can only choose one." — "What are you giving up with this choice?" / "How does this affect you in one year versus five years?"

*Consequence twist:* "Six months later, Company A loses half its customers to the competitor. Company B grew slowly but steadily." — "If you chose Company A, how do you feel now? What happened to your ownership?" / "Would you revise your original choice? Why?"

Risk categories (board): Customer risk — people stop buying. Competition risk — other companies take customers. Change risk — trends or technology shift.

*Checkpoint: Ensure students connect ownership to both success and struggle and can name a specific risk category.*

**Independent Activity — Investor Snapshot (15 min).** Write complete sentences: (1) One company or brand you believe could grow, and why athletes or people use it. (2) One reason it could grow ("because"), tied to customer demand or a real problem it solves. (3) One specific risk category that could affect it, and the impact on the company and its owners. (4) How owners are affected if the company struggles — what happens to ownership value and why. (5) Why investing requires patience: "In the short term ______, but in the long term ______."

Assessment checks: company real and familiar; reason relates to customer demand/usefulness with "because"; risk categorized with impact explained; ownership impact stated with cause-and-effect; patience explanation contrasts short vs long term.

**Reflection (7 min).** (1) Why is investing different from saving — what can investing do that saving cannot, and what risk does it carry that saving does not? (2) Why does investing take patience — describe a situation, on the field or with money, where someone might give up too early, and what they would miss.

**Daily Post-Check (5 min).** (1) Define investing and what makes it different from saving. (2) What does ownership mean when a company succeeds vs struggles? (3) Name one risk category investors consider, with a real example. (4) True or False: Investing is best for short-term goals — explain with cause-and-effect. (5) If a company loses customers, how are owners affected?

**Common Misconceptions:**
- *"Investing means gambling"* — Investing is ownership based on research and reasoning; gambling is based on chance. The investor evaluates evidence, the gambler hopes for a result.
- *"Investing always makes money"* — Investing involves risk; results are not guaranteed. Understanding risk categories helps you make informed choices rather than blind ones.
- *"Investing is only for rich people"* — Anyone can invest, even with small amounts; the key is understanding ownership, risk, and patience, not starting with a lot of money.

**Continuity Bridge:** Last week, we learned how budgeting helps protect savings. This week, we learned how investing uses money to grow over time. Next week, we will learn about credit and inflation and how borrowing and rising prices affect financial decisions.

## Week 4 — Credit & Inflation
**Phase:** Foundation · **Theme:** Borrowing, Cost, and Why Prices Change Over Time · **Session length:** 60 min
**Primary skill:** Explaining how credit works and why borrowing has a cost. **Secondary skill:** Understanding inflation and how rising prices affect long-term planning.

**What Students Practice:** Understanding how credit and borrowing work; recognizing interest as the cost of borrowing; explaining how inflation affects prices and planning.

**Success Criteria:** Define credit as borrowing money that must be repaid with extra cost. Explain interest as the cost of borrowing. Explain inflation as rising prices over time. Identify when using credit can help versus when it can hurt. Explain how interest and inflation together make long-term planning more important.

**Daily Pre-Check (8 min).** (1) What does credit mean in your own words? (2) True or False: Borrowed money does not need to be repaid. (3) What is interest? (4) What does ownership mean when you invest in a company? (5) True or False: Prices usually stay the same over time. (6) Name one thing — travel, gear, or a registration fee — that costs more today than it did a couple of years ago.

*Checkpoint: Listen for students confusing credit with free money or misunderstanding interest.*

**Concept Explanation (10 min).**

"Credit means borrowing money now and paying it back later. It is not extra money you received — it is money that belongs to someone else that you are using temporarily. That is an important distinction, because borrowed money always comes with a cost."

"When you use credit, you usually pay back more than you borrowed. The extra amount is called interest. Interest is the cost of borrowing money — it is what the lender charges you for letting you use their money. The longer you take to repay, the more interest you typically pay."

"Credit can help when it is used for important needs or long-term goals — like covering a recruiting trip that leads to a scholarship, or gear you need for the season. In these cases, the benefit of borrowing outweighs the cost. Credit can hurt when it is used for wants that you cannot afford — like buying something expensive on impulse without a plan to repay it."

"Here is an example: Imagine you borrow $100 to buy new cleats before a showcase. You have to repay $120 over three months. That extra $20 is interest — the cost of wanting it now instead of saving for it. If you had saved $25 a month, you would have had $100 in four months with zero extra cost. The question is: was having the cleats one month earlier worth $20?"

"Many people get credit wrong because they treat it like their own money. They forget that every dollar borrowed must be repaid with extra cost attached. This leads to spending more than they can afford and falling behind."

"Inflation means prices rise over time. This means the same amount of money buys less in the future than it does today. A flight to a showcase that cost $200 a couple of years ago might cost $280 today. Your money did not change — the price did."

"When inflation and interest work together, the impact is powerful. Inflation makes waiting more expensive because prices rise. Interest makes borrowing more expensive because you pay extra. The athlete who plans ahead — saving and budgeting before prices rise, especially for travel and recruiting costs — avoids both traps. That is the kind of thinker who stays ahead instead of falling behind."

Board setup: CREDIT = borrow now, pay back more later. INTEREST = extra cost. INFLATION = prices rise over time. GOOD CREDIT USE = needs or long-term goals. BAD CREDIT USE = wants without a plan. Closing line: "Credit is a tool. How you use it determines whether it helps or hurts."

**Guided Activity — Price Change & Borrowing Scenarios (15 min).** Board: flight to a showcase two years ago $200, today $280 — class discusses why (inflation).

*Goal scenario:* "You want gear that costs $100 today. If prices rise, it may cost $120 in two years." — "What happened to the price?" / "What would saving earlier help you avoid?"

*Forced tradeoff:* "You want a $100 piece of gear today but only have $80." Option A: borrow $20, repay $25 next month. Option B: save $10/month, buy in 2 months, but the price might rise to $105. — "What are you giving up with each choice?" / "How does each option affect you one month from now versus three months from now?"

*Consequence twist:* "You chose to borrow. Next month, an unexpected travel expense of $15 appears. Now you owe $25 for the loan PLUS $15 for the travel." — "How does borrowing make unexpected expenses harder to handle?" / "Would you revise your original decision? What would you do differently?"

*Checkpoint: Ensure students connect interest to borrowing cost and inflation to rising prices; confirm they can articulate the compounding effect of borrowing plus unexpected expenses.*

**Independent Activity — Credit & Inflation Impact (15 min).** Write complete sentences: (1) One example of when using credit could help an athlete, and why the benefit outweighs interest ("because"). (2) One example of when using credit could hurt, connected to a real-life consequence. (3) One way inflation could increase the cost of a future goal — a travel cost, a showcase fee, or gear — what it costs now, what it might cost later, and what this means for saving. (4) "If I plan ahead instead of borrowing, in the future I will ______ because ______."

Assessment checks: credit examples show clear judgment with "because" justification; inflation explanation includes time, rising cost, and impact on saving; real-life connection explicit; forward-looking statement shows cause-and-effect.

**Reflection (7 min).** (1) Why should credit be used carefully — what can go wrong without a plan, and how would it affect your future financial options as an athlete? (2) Why does inflation make saving and planning important — what happens to a goal, like a showcase or trip, if you wait too long to start saving for it?

**Daily Post-Check (5 min).** (1) Define credit and why borrowed money costs more than saved money. (2) What is interest, with a specific example of how it affects total repayment? (3) How does inflation affect prices over time, and what does that mean for someone saving toward a travel or gear goal? (4) True or False: Inflation makes planning more important — explain with cause-and-effect. (5) Scenario: if you wait and prices rise, what happens, and what could you have done differently? (6) Scenario: when might borrowing help vs hurt an athlete — one example of each.

**Common Misconceptions:**
- *"Credit is free money"* — Credit is borrowed money that must be repaid, usually with interest; treating it as free money is one of the most common and damaging financial mistakes.
- *"Interest is optional"* — Interest is the cost of borrowing and typically required as part of repayment; the longer repayment takes, the more interest accumulates.
- *"Inflation only affects adults"* — Inflation affects everyone, including athletes paying for their own travel and gear; starting to save early is one of the best ways to stay ahead of it.

**Continuity Bridge:** We learned that investing involves risk and time. Credit and inflation show why borrowing and rising prices make long-term decisions even more important. Next week, we will use everything we have learned to make better financial decisions.

## Week 5 — Financial Decision-Making
**Phase:** Foundation · **Theme:** Tradeoffs, Constraints, and Better Choices · **Session length:** 60 min
**Primary skill:** Making financial decisions using priorities, tradeoffs, and consequences. **Secondary skill:** Explaining why changing a plan is sometimes the correct decision.

**What Students Practice:** Making financial decisions using priorities and tradeoffs; applying a priority order when conditions change; justifying decisions using saving, budgeting, investing, or credit concepts.

**Success Criteria:** Define a financial decision as a choice with consequences. Explain what a tradeoff is using a real example. Apply a priority order when conditions change. Explain when borrowing may help versus when it may hurt. Justify a decision using saving, budgeting, investing, or credit concepts.

**Daily Pre-Check (8 min).** (1) What is one cost of borrowing money? (2) What does the word tradeoff mean? (3) True or False: Changing a goal means you failed. (4) Why might an athlete adjust a savings timeline? (5) Name one budget category that affects saving. (6) True or False: All financial decisions have consequences.

*Checkpoint: Listen for students equating change with failure or thinking tradeoffs are optional.*

**Concept Explanation (10 min).**

"A financial decision is a choice that affects how money is used. Every time you decide to spend, save, invest, or borrow, you are making a financial decision. These decisions are not isolated — each one affects the options available to you next."

"Every financial decision involves a tradeoff. Choosing more of one thing usually means less of another. If you spend more on wants, you have less for savings. If you save more, you may need to reduce what you spend on fun. There is no way around tradeoffs — the goal is to make them intentionally."

"When conditions change, good decisions change too. If your stipend or allowance drops, if travel costs rise, or if an unexpected expense appears, the smartest thing to do is adjust. Changing a plan is not failure if it improves the outcome — the same way a coach adjusts a game plan without it meaning the original plan was wrong."

"Good decisions follow a priority order: needs first, then savings goals, then wants, then borrowing. This order exists because needs keep you stable, savings protect your future, wants are optional, and borrowing adds cost."

"Here is an example: You planned to save $30 this month toward a showcase fee, but your allowance dropped by $10. Instead of panicking, you reduce your wants by $10 and keep your savings intact. That is a strong financial decision because you protected what matters most."

"Many people get this wrong because they refuse to adjust when things change. They stick to their original plan even when it no longer works — and that stubbornness costs them more than adapting would have."

"In the short term, changing a plan can feel like failure. In the long term, adjusting quickly is one of the most valuable financial skills. The person who adapts protects their goals. The person who ignores change falls behind. Strong decision-makers are not rigid — they are responsive."

Board setup: DECISION = choice with consequences. TRADEOFF = give up one thing to gain another. PRIORITY ORDER = needs → savings goals → wants → borrowing. BORROWING = option with extra cost. Closing line: "We protect needs first, then savings goals, then wants. Borrowing is an option, but it adds cost."

**Guided Activity — Decision Chain Scenario (15 min).** Scenario on board: "You receive $100 this month. You save $30 toward a showcase fee. You spend $50. You invest $20."

*Condition change #1:* "Your income drops to $80." — "Which category should be protected first based on our priority order?" / "Which category should change first and why?" / "What are you giving up with this choice?" / "How does this affect you next month?"

*Condition change #2:* "A price increase makes your showcase fee cost $20 more." — "Do you adjust the timeline, change the goal, or borrow?" / "What extra cost does borrowing add?" / "Which option best follows our priority order?"

*Consequence twist:* "You chose to borrow the $20. But next month, your income drops again to $70. Now you have the loan repayment PLUS lower income." — "How did borrowing make this situation harder?" / "Would you revise your decision from Condition Change #2? What would you do differently?" / "Take 30 seconds to revise your plan. Explain what changed and why."

*Checkpoint: Ensure students justify decisions using the priority order and do not default to borrowing automatically.*

**Independent Activity — Decision Justification (15 min).** Write complete sentences, be specific: (1) One financial decision you would change from the scenario, and what you would do differently. (2) Which category changed first (needs, savings goals, wants, or borrowing) and why, per the priority order ("because"). (3) One reason for the change, connected to a concept from earlier weeks. (4) One tradeoff created by the change — what was gained, what was lost — plus "This is similar to when I ______." (5) One consequence of not changing the decision, short-term and long-term.

Assessment checks: category change explicit with "because"; tradeoff includes gain/loss with real-life connection; consequence shows cause and effect both short and long term; priority order followed; connection to earlier concepts accurate.

**Reflection (7 min).** (1) What is one decision you would change from today's lesson, and how does the revised decision better follow the priority order? (2) How does changing a decision help instead of hurt — describe a situation in your own life, on the field or off it? (3) What is one lesson about money decisions you learned today that you did not know before?

**Daily Post-Check (5 min).** (1) Define a financial decision and why every decision creates consequences. (2) What is a tradeoff — give a specific example of what was gained and lost. (3) True or False: Changing a plan can improve outcomes — cause-and-effect explanation. (4) Name one reason a budget or goal might need to change, and what happens if you refuse to adjust. (5) Scenario: if income drops, which category adjusts first and why (priority order)?

**Common Misconceptions:**
- *"Adjusting before calculating"* — Calculate the total adjustment first before making any changes; adjusting without calculating is like navigating without a map.
- *"Forgetting the 85 remainder"* — After subtracting the unexpected expense, identify the remaining amount for Needs, Savings, and Wants; skipping this leads to incorrect category amounts.
- *"Reducing needs first"* — Needs are protected first; wants adjust before needs are reduced.
- *"Reducing savings before wants"* — Wants adjust before savings; savings represents future options and reducing it should be a last resort.
- *"Forgetting to include the unexpected expense"* — The expense must appear as a line item in the final budget.
- *"Totals not equal to 90"* — Every budget must balance; an unbalanced budget is an unfinished budget.
