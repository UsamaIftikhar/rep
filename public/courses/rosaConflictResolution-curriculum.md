## Module 1 — Understanding Conflict

**Objective**

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

- Define what conflict actually is, on and off the field
- Identify the most common causes of conflict on a team
- Tell the difference between a personal conflict and a team conflict
- Explain why competitive sports environments produce more conflict
- See conflict as something to manage on purpose, not avoid

**Core Concept**

Conflict is a clash between two or more people because of different needs, goals, values, or perceptions. That is the whole definition. It does not require yelling. It does not require enemies. Two teammates who both want the starting spot are already in conflict, even if neither one says a word.

Not all conflict is bad. Healthy conflict can lead to better solutions, stronger relationships, and personal growth — when it is handled well. The goal is not to live without conflict. The goal is to manage it on purpose so it does not run you and your team.

Most conflict on a team comes from a few common places. Competition for playing time and positions. Miscommunication between teammates and coaches. Ego, pride, and difficulty taking criticism. Pressure from results. Personality and culture differences. Perceived favoritism. None of these are unusual. All of them show up on every team, at every level.

There is a real difference between a personal conflict and a team conflict. A personal conflict involves two people — their feelings, their history, their ego. A team conflict affects the whole group's energy, performance, or trust. Personal conflicts that are ignored almost always grow into team conflicts. Knowing which one you are dealing with helps you choose the right response.

Sports environments produce more conflict than most settings for one simple reason. The pressure is higher, the stakes are public, and the people involved spend an enormous amount of time together. Wins and losses are scoreboards in front of everyone. Spots are limited. Bodies are tired. Egos are on display. All of that is normal. Pretending it is not is what makes conflict explode.

The athletes who lead at the highest levels do not avoid conflict. They expect it, prepare for it, and use it to get the team better. Avoidance feels safe. It is not. Quiet teams with unresolved tension lose more games than loud teams who handle it head-on with respect.

There is one mindset shift that changes everything in this module. Conflict is not the enemy. Unmanaged conflict is the enemy. The first step is naming what is happening, instead of pretending it is not there.

**Scenario**

Marcus and Devon are both wide receivers competing for the starting spot. Both are talented. Both work hard. Both feel like they have earned it.

After a close loss, Coach announces the starting lineup for next week. Devon is starting. Marcus is on the bench again.

Marcus walks into the locker room hot. He sees Devon at his locker, and in front of half the team, says loudly: "You know the only reason you are starting is because you are the coach's favorite. I work twice as hard as you."

The locker room goes quiet. Phones come down. Heads turn.

Right there, you can see every layer of conflict at once. There is a personal conflict — Marcus is hurt, embarrassed, and feels overlooked. There is a team conflict — every teammate in that room just got pulled into the situation, and how it gets handled now will shape practice on Monday. There is also an unspoken conflict between Marcus and the coach.

The trigger was the lineup. The deeper causes are weeks of frustration, no clear conversation about what Marcus needs to do to start, and pride on both sides. The setting is a high-pressure space where everyone is exhausted from a loss.

This is not a story about who is right. It is a story about how fast a normal sports moment turns into a defining moment for a team. The athletes in the room who notice what is happening — and choose to slow it down instead of feed it — are the ones who lead.

**Student Action**

- Conflict Map — Write down one current or recent conflict in your sport or school life. Name the two parties, the specific trigger, and the deeper underlying cause.
- Personal vs Team — For that same conflict, decide if it is personal, team, or both. In one sentence, explain why.
- Pressure Audit — List three things about your team environment this season that make conflict more likely (schedule, lineup decisions, culture, social media, recent loss).
- Reframe — Rewrite the conflict you mapped using this sentence: "This is not about ___, it is really about ___."
- One Standard — Write one small standard you commit to this week when you notice conflict around you (lower my voice, walk away for five minutes, step in if it is between teammates).

## Module 2 — Emotional Intelligence

**Objective**

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

- Define emotional intelligence in plain language
- Tell the difference between reacting and responding
- Identify your own emotional triggers
- Use the pause technique in a heated moment
- Use self-awareness to become a better teammate

**Core Concept**

Emotional intelligence is the ability to recognize, understand, and manage your own emotions, and to recognize and influence the emotions of other people. That is it. It is not soft. It is not weakness. Research shows that emotional intelligence often matters more than raw talent for long-term success in sports, school, and work.

For an athlete, high emotional intelligence shows up in three places. Better decisions under pressure. Stronger relationships with teammates and coaches. Faster recovery from failure. Talent gets you on the team. Emotional intelligence keeps you on the field.

There is a critical difference between reacting and responding.

Reacting is automatic and emotion-driven. It is what happens when your feelings take over before your brain has time to process the situation. A teammate criticizes you and you yell back before you even know what you are saying. That is a reaction.

Responding is intentional and thoughtful. You feel the same emotion, but you pause, assess, and choose your action. The best leaders, on and off the field, respond. They do not just react.

Emotional triggers are specific situations, words, or behaviors that cause a strong emotional reaction in you, often connected to past experience or deeply held values. Common triggers for athletes include being disrespected in public, being blamed unfairly, being ignored, being benched without explanation, or having effort questioned. Everyone has triggers. The question is whether you know yours.

The pause technique is the most useful tool in this module. When you feel the heat rising, you take a deliberate moment before responding. A deep breath. Counting to five. Stepping back physically. During that pause, your brain shifts from reactive mode to rational mode. Even a three-second pause can prevent a response you will regret.

Self-awareness changes how you show up as a teammate too. When you know what sets you off, you can prepare for it instead of being blindsided. You can also tell trusted teammates and coaches what your triggers are, which reduces misunderstandings. And once you understand your own emotional patterns, you become better at reading other people's. Empathy starts with self-awareness, not with being nice.

**Scenario**

Jordan is a junior point guard. The team is on a three-game losing streak. Practice is tense.

In the middle of a drill, the assistant coach yells across the court, "Jordan, that is the third lazy pass today. Pick it up or sit down."

Jordan feels the heat hit immediately. Embarrassment in front of the whole team. Anger because she has been playing through a sore wrist nobody knows about. Frustration because she has been carrying a lot for this group.

In the old version of Jordan, she would have fired back. Said something sarcastic. Slammed the ball. Made the moment about her and the coach.

Today she does something different. She takes one breath. She walks to the sideline. She drinks water. She gives herself fifteen seconds. Her brain catches up to her body. She decides what she actually wants to say.

She walks back into the drill, looks at the assistant coach, and says calmly, "Got it, Coach. I will tighten up." That is it. The drill keeps going.

After practice, she finds the assistant coach in his office. She closes the door. She says, "Coach, when you called me out in front of the team today, it hit hard because I have been playing through some stuff I have not told you about. I should have told you. I will, going forward. I also heard you on the passes. I will fix it."

The coach respects her more after that conversation than he did before practice started. Nothing about the situation changed. Jordan changed.

The coach made his point. The team got its drill done. Jordan kept her seat at the table as a leader. The only reason any of that worked is the fifteen-second pause she took before she opened her mouth.

**Student Action**

- Trigger List — Write down two things that consistently set you off (in sports, school, family, social media). Be specific about the situation, not just the emotion.
- Reaction History — For each trigger, write what your typical reaction looks like, and one outcome it has cost you.
- Pause Plan — Pick one pause move you will use this week (deep breath, count to five, walk to water, step out of the room). Decide in advance, not in the moment.
- Body Check — For 24 hours, notice the physical signs that show up before you react (tight chest, hot face, jaw clench). Write down what your body tells you.
- Share One — Tell one teammate, coach, or family member about one of your triggers and your pause plan. Saying it out loud makes it real.

## Module 3 — Communication Skills

**Objective**

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

- Practice active listening, not just hearing
- Recognize how tone and body language shape every message
- Use I-statements to express frustration without attacking
- Handle teammates who refuse to communicate
- Align your words and your body language so they tell the same story

**Core Concept**

Communication is the system that holds a team together. When it is clear, conflict gets resolved. When it breaks down, every other problem gets worse. The good news is that communication in conflict is a skill, not a personality trait. You can learn it.

Hearing is passive. Sound enters your ears. Active listening is intentional. You focus completely on the speaker, you do not interrupt, you ask clarifying questions, and you reflect back what you heard. In a conflict, active listening can defuse tension fast because the other person feels understood instead of attacked. A simple line that works: "What I am hearing you say is..." followed by their point in your own words.

Tone of voice carries more weight than most athletes realize. The same sentence can sound supportive or aggressive depending on how it is delivered. Research suggests that body language and tone carry the majority of any message. The actual words are a small piece. In a conflict, a calm, even tone signals respect and willingness to resolve. A sharp or sarcastic tone escalates the situation, even when the words look fine on paper.

The I-statement is the single most useful sentence shape in conflict. Instead of attacking the other person, you express how you feel and why. The structure is simple: "I feel ___ when ___ because ___."

Bad: "You always hog the ball."
Better: "I feel frustrated when I do not get passing opportunities because I feel like I cannot contribute to the team."

The first one starts a fight. The second one starts a real conversation. Same situation. Different outcome.

When a teammate refuses to communicate, do not force it. Forcing escalates. Respect their space, then approach them privately at a neutral time, not right after a tough practice or a loss. Use a calm, non-accusatory opener: "I would like to clear things up. Is now a good time?" If they continue to shut down, involve a trusted coach or mediator. Never escalate through third parties or social media.

Body language communicates attitude even when you are silent. Open posture (uncrossed arms, relaxed shoulders, eye contact) signals you are willing to engage. Closed posture (arms crossed, looking away, invading personal space) signals aggression or defensiveness. The most powerful communicators in tense moments make sure their body says the same thing as their words. Both signal respect and openness, or the message falls apart.

**Scenario**

After a loss, two teammates, Tia and Maya, get into it on the bus ride home. Tia thinks Maya gave up in the second half. Maya thinks Tia called her out unfairly during the game.

Neither one has the conversation that night. Instead, Tia posts on Instagram: "Some people need to stop pretending they care about winning."

By morning, the team group chat is on fire. Everyone knows who the post was about. Maya is hurt and angry. She is half a sentence away from posting something back.

The team captain, Alex, sees the chat blow up. Instead of jumping in publicly, she does three things in a row.

First, she sends a single message in the group chat: "Let's keep this off here. Tia and Maya, I will reach out individually. Everyone else, please do not pile on." That is it. No drama, no taking sides.

Second, she calls Tia privately. She listens for ten minutes before saying anything. When Tia finishes, Alex says, "What I am hearing you say is that you felt like Maya checked out, and you have been holding that in for a few weeks. Is that fair?" Tia says yes.

Third, she calls Maya. Same approach. Listens first. Then helps her draft what she actually wants to say to Tia using an I-statement: "I felt disrespected when I saw that post because it affected how the team sees me. Can we talk in person?"

The next day, Tia and Maya sit down in person, not online. The conversation is uncomfortable. It also happens. Both apologize for their part. The team moves on.

The reason this worked is not because anyone was the smartest person in the room. It is because Alex listened first, kept it off social media, and used the right words at the right time. Communication is a skill, not luck.

**Student Action**

- Active Listening Drill — In your next real conversation, practice saying "What I am hearing you say is..." once. Do not skip it. Notice how the other person reacts.
- Tone Test — Pick one sentence ("I am fine.", "That is your call.", "We need to talk."). Say it out loud five different ways. Notice how much the meaning changes when only the tone changes.
- I-Statement Builder — Write three real frustrations you have right now using the structure: "I feel ___ when ___ because ___."
- Body Language Audit — In your next team conversation, check your own posture. Are your arms crossed? Are you making eye contact? Adjust one thing.
- The Cooling Rule — Make one promise to yourself: when you are upset, you will not text, post, or send a DM for at least one hour. Write it down.

## Module 4 — Conflict Resolution Strategies

**Objective**

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

- Use de-escalation techniques to stop conflict from exploding
- Apply a 5-step framework to resolve real conflicts
- Find common ground when two people seem opposed
- Know when to handle a conflict privately and when to involve a coach
- Rebuild a relationship after a resolution

**Core Concept**

Most conflicts do not get worse because the issue is huge. They get worse because nobody knows what to do once it starts. This module gives you a framework you can use anywhere — sport, school, family, future workplace.

De-escalation techniques are actions you take to lower the intensity of a conflict before it becomes destructive. The basics: lower your voice, create physical space, acknowledge the other person's feelings ("I understand you are upset"), avoid accusatory language, suggest a short break before continuing. Conflicts solved at low intensity have far better outcomes than ones that explode and then get patched up.

The 5-step conflict resolution framework is the spine of this whole module. Memorize it.

Step 1: Identify the problem. What is the disagreement actually about? Be specific. "We are not communicating" is too vague. "We did not agree on who runs the rotation in the third quarter" is real.

Step 2: Hear all perspectives. Let each person speak without interruption. Use active listening from Module 3. Ask clarifying questions. Do not jump to solving until everyone has been heard.

Step 3: Find common ground. What do both parties already agree on? In a team conflict, common ground is often "we both want the team to win" or "we both want to be respected." Starting from shared ground makes the rest of the conversation cooperative instead of combative.

Step 4: Brainstorm solutions. Generate multiple options without judging them yet. The first idea is rarely the best one. Keep going until you have at least three real options.

Step 5: Agree on a plan. Pick the best solution together and commit to it specifically. "We will both do better" is not a plan. "Tia will handle the in-game callouts and Maya will focus on positioning, and we will check in after Friday's game" is a plan.

Finding common ground is the step most people skip. They jump from problem to solution and miss the part where both sides see they actually want some of the same things. Without common ground, every solution feels like a loss for someone.

Knowing when to bring in a coach or adult is a sign of maturity, not weakness. Handle it privately when the conflict is between two people, emotions are manageable, and both are willing to talk. Involve a coach when there is a safety concern, the whole team is affected, repeated attempts have failed, or there is bullying or harassment.

Resolution does not end the work. It starts the rebuilding phase. Real rebuilding looks like a genuine apology from anyone who contributed, a clear agreement on what changes going forward, time for trust to grow back through consistent action, and periodic check-ins to make sure the resolution is holding. Real forgiveness shows up in behavior, not in words.

**Scenario**

Coach Davis benches Jordan for the third game in a row with no real explanation. After practice, Jordan is fired up. He walks straight at Coach in the hallway and says loudly, in front of two teammates, "Why do you keep benching me? This is ridiculous. I deserve to play."

Coach is caught off guard. He had a real reason. He was about to talk to Jordan about it after practice. But the way Jordan came at him just shut that conversation down.

Both walk away angry. Nothing is solved.

Now run the 5-step framework on what should happen next.

Step 1 — Identify the problem. Jordan thinks the issue is playing time. The deeper problem is the lack of a conversation about why he is sitting and what he needs to fix.

Step 2 — Hear all perspectives. Jordan needs to come back, calmly, the next day. He requests a private meeting. He says, "Coach, I should not have come at you like that yesterday. I want to understand what you are seeing so I can fix it. I will listen first." Coach explains the decision — Jordan has been late to two film sessions and his defensive rotations have slipped.

Step 3 — Find common ground. They both want Jordan back on the floor. They both want the team to win. They both want this to stop being weird in the locker room.

Step 4 — Brainstorm solutions. Options: Jordan commits to being the first one in the film room, gets a defensive checklist from Coach, asks the assistant coach for extra reps after practice, sets up a weekly five-minute check-in with Coach.

Step 5 — Agree on a plan. They pick three actions: Jordan in the film room 15 minutes early every day, defensive checklist reviewed every Friday, five-minute check-in every Monday. They agree to revisit in two weeks.

After two weeks, Jordan starts. Not because he complained louder. Because he handled the conflict like a leader.

The first interaction in the hallway was a reaction. The second one was a response. That difference is everything.

- De-Escalation Drill — List three de-escalation moves you will use the next time you feel a conflict heating up (lower voice, suggest a break, name the other person's feeling).
- Common Ground Search — For one current disagreement, write down three things both sides actually agree on, even if they do not realize it yet.
- Private vs Public — Make a personal rule for when you handle a conflict alone and when you bring in a coach or adult. Write your two-sentence rule.
- Rebuild Plan — Pick one relationship that has been off lately. Write three specific actions you will take in the next two weeks to rebuild trust. Behavior, not words.

## Module 5 — Leadership in Conflict

**Objective**

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

- Lead through conflict instead of avoiding it
- Take real accountability for your part in any disagreement
- Tell the difference between being a peacemaker and being a pushover
- Step in as a captain or senior teammate without making things worse
- Help build a team culture where conflict makes the team stronger

**Core Concept**

Leadership shows up most clearly in conflict. Anyone can lead when things are easy. The athletes who become real leaders are the ones who stay calm, fair, and accountable when things get tense.

A leader handles conflict differently than someone without leadership skills. A leader stays calm under pressure. They take it seriously without taking it personally. They listen to everyone before deciding. They focus on solutions instead of blame. And they hold themselves accountable, not just others. That last part is what separates real leaders from loud teammates.

Accountability means owning your role in a conflict. It is not blaming others. It is not making excuses. It is not "I was just reacting to what they did." It is naming, out loud, the part you played, and following through on what you said you would do.

Accountability is contagious. When one person takes genuine accountability in a conflict, it almost always pulls the other person to do the same. When nobody takes accountability, the conflict repeats every week, because the root cause is never addressed.

There is a critical difference between a peacemaker and a pushover. A peacemaker pursues a fair, sustainable resolution that respects everyone involved. A pushover avoids conflict by giving in, even when the resolution is unfair. Pushover behavior feels like peace in the short term. Long term, it builds resentment and weakens the team. Real leadership means having the hard conversation with respect and firmness, not skipping it.

Captains and senior athletes have a special role. They can redirect emotional teammates with a calm word, draw attention away from a flashpoint ("Let's get back to the next play"), pull a teammate aside privately when emotions are climbing, and quietly loop in a coach before things escalate. The composure of a captain is contagious. So is the chaos of a captain. Whichever one you bring sets the standard.

A team culture of accountability is one where every member holds themselves and each other to agreed standards, with respect, not punishment. Conflict, handled well, actually strengthens this culture. It shows the team can work through hard things, that difficult conversations are safe, and that the relationships are built on honesty. Teams that avoid every conflict often lack the depth to handle real pressure when it shows up in a championship game or a long season.

**Scenario**

During a team meeting after a four-game losing streak, Coach asks the room one question: "What do we need to do better?"

Two teammates, Sam and Priya, immediately get into it. Sam says Priya has been dragging the team down with bad body language. Priya says Sam has been ducking accountability all season. Voices climb. The whole team goes quiet and watches.

In the corner of the room, the senior captain, Andre, has a choice.

He could stay quiet and hope it passes. He could pick a side. He could try to shut both of them down with authority.

He does none of that. He stands up calmly, walks to the front of the room with Coach's nod, and says three things.

First: "Both of you have a point. I want to hear both of you. But not like this. Not in front of everyone, not at this volume. We are going to handle it like a team."

Second, he sits down between them. He looks at Sam. "Tell me what you have been holding onto, in one sentence." Sam takes a breath and says it. Then Andre looks at Priya. "Same question." Priya answers.

Third, he names the common ground for the room. "Both of you want this team to win. Both of you feel like you are giving more than you are getting back. That is the same problem from different sides."

He suggests they move the rest of the meeting back to football, and that he, Sam, and Priya sit down separately after to actually resolve it. They both agree. The meeting moves forward. Coach watches Andre and nods.

That night, Andre meets with Sam first, then Priya, then both of them together. He uses the 5-step framework from Module 4. He takes accountability for things he has missed as a captain. Both of them follow his lead. By the end of the week, Sam and Priya are back to functioning teammates.

Andre did not solve the conflict by being the loudest. He solved it by being the steadiest. That is leadership in conflict.

**Student Action**

- Peacemaker vs Pushover — List two times in the last six months you backed down from a conflict to keep the peace. For each, decide if it was real peacemaking or pushover behavior. Be honest.
- Captain Move — Pick one captain or senior teammate you respect. Write down two specific things they do in tense moments that you want to copy.
- The Calm Standard — Write your personal rule for how you carry yourself in a tense team moment (volume, body language, tone, words). Make it short enough to remember.
- Team Code — Draft three rules your team could agree to about how conflict gets handled internally (no posting about teammates, no group-text fights, all real issues handled in person).

## Module 6 — Real-World Application

**Objective**

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

- Handle conflict with a coach respectfully, even when the power dynamic is hard
- Respond to social media conflict without making it worse
- Step into a teammate-vs-teammate situation in a real game environment
- Manage post-loss emotion without spiraling into blame
- Stay disciplined when an opponent is trying to provoke you

**Core Concept**

Everything in the first five modules was the foundation. This module is the field. Real conflict almost never happens in a quiet room with a script. It happens after a tough loss, in a group chat at midnight, on the bench during a tight game, or three minutes before tip-off.

Coach conflict is different from any other kind because of the power dynamic. Handle it respectfully and privately. Request a one-on-one meeting. Use a professional tone. Come prepared with specific points, not just emotion. Listen without interrupting. Avoid going over a coach's head until you have exhausted direct, respectful conversation. This approach earns respect even when the outcome is not what you wanted.

Social media conflict is public conflict. The biggest mistake an athlete can make is trying to resolve a conflict in the comments. Do not respond publicly. Take a screenshot for documentation if you need it. Approach the person privately and calmly using an I-statement: "I felt disrespected when I saw that post because it affected how others see me. Can we talk?" If it continues, involve a coach or school administrator. Public escalation almost always damages your reputation more than the original conflict damaged you.

When two teammates are in a serious argument before a big game, your only job in the moment is de-escalation. Insert yourself calmly between them if it is safe. Use a low, firm voice: "Hey, not now. Let's focus on the game." Separate them physically. Speak to each privately to acknowledge their frustration. If it is dangerous or unresolvable before game time, get a coach involved immediately. After the game, encourage them to sit down and work through it with the 5-step framework. Some conflicts need a short pause before resolution. The pause is part of the work.

Losing a big game often triggers conflict because loss creates pain, frustration, and a search for blame. Athletes turn anger inward, on teammates, on coaches, or on officials, instead of processing the loss. Leaders reframe post-loss emotion. They call for reflection ("What can we all do differently?") instead of blame. They model emotional regulation. They give the team space to feel disappointed before pushing into a constructive conversation. Blaming after a loss is normal. Wallowing in blame is destructive.

Conflict with opponents during a game is its own category. Stay within the rules and focus on competing. If an opponent is talking trash or trying to provoke, do not engage verbally or physically. Respond with your performance. If there is a real safety concern (dirty play), communicate to your coach or an official. Retaliation almost always hurts your team more than the opponent. The best response to disrespect is excellence.

This is the module where every framework gets tested. Pause technique. I-statements. 5-step resolution. Accountability. Captain composure. All of them get pulled out of the playbook and used in real time. The athletes who lead in this module are the ones who built the habits in the first five.

**Scenario**

It is the championship game. Down two with 30 seconds left. Time out.

In the huddle, the point guard, Riley, snaps at the freshman, Eli, "If you miss another rotation, we are done. Just stand in the corner."

Eli's head drops. The coach starts drawing up a play. The senior captain, Mia, sees what just happened in two seconds.

She does not call Riley out in the huddle. She does not coddle Eli. She steps to Eli's side, makes eye contact, and says quietly, "You belong here. Run your read. I have your back." Then she looks at Riley and says, "We talk after. Right now we win this play."

The play runs. Eli sets the screen, slips it, and gets fouled. Two free throws. He hits both. Game tied. They win in overtime.

Locker room celebration. Then the work begins.

Ten minutes after the celebration, Mia pulls Riley aside in the hallway. "Riley. In the huddle, you came at Eli in front of everyone. That moment could have ended his confidence for the game. I get the pressure. I felt it too. But that is not how we lead."

Riley pushes back. "I was just being honest." Mia waits. Then says, "I hear you. The honesty was right. The way you delivered it was not. If you have something to say to Eli, say it to him directly, not in front of the team in a high-pressure moment. That is the standard."

Riley sits with it. Eventually nods. Says, "You are right. I will talk to him tonight."

That night, Riley sits with Eli for ten minutes. Owns it. Apologizes. Tells Eli he believes in him. Eli starts the next game stronger because of how that conversation went.

Coach hears about it on Monday. He says nothing publicly. He moves Mia from senior to permanent captain for the rest of the season.

Real-world application is not theory. It is the moments most people never see. The hallway after the win. The text you do not send. The teammate you protect when it would be easier to let them sink. That is where leadership and conflict resolution live.

**Student Action**

- Coach Conversation Plan — Pick one issue you have with a coach right now (real or hypothetical). Write a script for a private one-on-one. Open with respect, name the specific issue, listen for the response, agree on a next step.
- Social Media Rule — Write your personal rule for when you will and will not post about a teammate, coach, opponent, or game. One sentence. Make it strict.
- In-Game Intervention — Visualize the next time two teammates start to go at each other on the bench or in a huddle. Write down exactly what you will say and do in the first ten seconds.
- Post-Loss Protocol — Write a three-line plan for how you will carry yourself for the first hour after your next tough loss (no posting, no blaming, listen first, ask one helpful question).
- Opponent Provocation — Write your one-sentence rule for how you respond when an opponent is trying to bait you. Make it something you would say out loud to your younger self.
