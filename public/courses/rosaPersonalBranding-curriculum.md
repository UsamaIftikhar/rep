## Module 1 — What a Personal Brand Really Is

**Objective**

By the end of this module, you will be able to define a personal brand in your own words, explain why it is more than logos or followers, and audit your current public presence.

**Core Concept**

Your personal brand is the meaning people attach to your name. It lives in their head, not on your phone. When someone hears your name and a coach, a fan, a teammate, a brand, or a recruiter forms a quick picture in their mind, that picture is your brand.

A brand has three parts:

1. Recognition. People know who you are.
2. Associations. People know what you stand for, what you do well, and what you care about.
3. Proof. People have seen something you said or did that backs it up.

If any of these three are missing, your brand is weak in that spot. If you have recognition with no associations, people know your face but cannot describe you. If you have associations with no proof, people doubt you. If you have proof with no recognition, the right people are not seeing it.

Why this matters for student-athletes: You already have a brand. The question is whether you built it on purpose or by accident. Coaches recruit based on it. Brands sign deals based on it. Teammates trust you based on it. Future employers Google you based on it. Even strangers form an opinion of you in less than five seconds. If you do not shape your brand, the highlight reel, the comment section, the bad photo, the one moment your friend filmed at a party, those all shape it for you.

Key ideas:
• Brand is meaning, not decoration. A logo, color, or font are tools. The brand is the meaning behind the tools.
• Recognition without associations is hollow. Going viral once without meaning rarely changes anything long-term.
• The five-second test: When someone says your name, what is the first sentence that follows?
• Brand is built in public, but planned in private. Choose, in private, what you want to be known for. Then let it show up in public on purpose.

**Scenario**

Two athletes are at the same school. Same sport. Same year. Same starting role.

Athlete A posts everything. Game photos, party clips, food, jokes, opinions, drama, more food, a couple of training videos. Their feed has no theme. People know their name but cannot describe them in one sentence.

Athlete B posts less, but everything they post sits inside a clear theme. Their bio says, "First-gen student-athlete. Sports business major. Builder." Their feed shows training, a campus business class, a part-time job, family, and short reels breaking down brand deals in plain language.

A local credit union sees both feeds. They want to sponsor a campus financial literacy campaign aimed at young adults. Who do they call?

They call Athlete B. Not because B is better at sport. Because B has a clear brand the credit union can match to their message.

Practical example: Think about a player like Stephen Curry. Long before the championships, the brand was already there: faith, family, range, work ethic, undersized but undeniable. Every photo, every post-game interview, every training clip backed it up. You could remove the team, the jersey, even the sport, and the meaning behind his name would still hold up. Now think smaller. Think about an athlete you follow with under 10,000 followers. If their brand is clear, you can describe them in one sentence. If it is not, you cannot.

## Module 2 — Identity, Reputation, Perception, Trust, and Consistency

**Objective**

By the end of this module, you will be able to explain the difference between identity, reputation, perception, and trust, and describe how consistency holds all four together.

**Core Concept**

A strong brand sits on five things.

1. Identity. Who you say you are.
2. Reputation. What other people believe about you based on what they have seen.
3. Perception. How people interpret your actions and content in the moment.
4. Trust. The willingness of others to believe what you say and do business with you.
5. Consistency. The thread that holds the other four together over time.

When these line up, your brand feels real. When they do not, your brand feels off, even if you cannot say why.

Why this matters for student-athletes: Athletes live with constant public attention. Your identity is being tested in public almost every day. A bad call, a tough loss, a comment from a stranger, a viral clip of someone else, all of it can pull at your identity if you have not done the work to know who you are. If your identity is strong but your reputation is weak, you need to act on what you say more often. If your reputation is strong but perception is poor in the moment, you may have a content or communication problem. If trust is low, brands and people walk away no matter how talented you are.

Key ideas:
1. Identity is a choice. You decide who you are. Not your followers, not your haters, not even your highlight reel.
2. Reputation is earned in reps. You get it from many small acts repeated for months and years.
3. Perception happens fast. People form opinions in seconds based on what they see.
4. Trust is the most valuable thing you own. Brands pay for trust. It is built slowly and broken quickly.
5. Consistency is the glue. If you say it and do it, again and again, your brand strengthens. Consistency is more important than perfection.

**Scenario**

A starting guard says publicly that she is a leader, a team-first player, and a hard worker. That is her identity.

Her teammates say she shows up early, stays late, and protects younger players. That is her reputation.

After a tough loss, a clip of her snapping at a referee goes around campus. In that moment, perception is "she is hot-headed."

A local sports drink brand is watching all of this before they offer a deal. Their question is, "Can we trust this athlete to represent us for a full season?"

If her identity, reputation, and how she handles the moment are all in the same direction, trust grows. If there is a big gap, trust drops. Consistency over time is what closes the gap. One bad moment does not kill a brand. A pattern of bad moments does.

Practical example: Look at LeBron James. His identity is "kid from Akron, family man, business owner, generational athlete, voice for his community." His reputation lines up with that across two decades. There have been bad moments and bad games, but the pattern is clear. The clip of him visiting the I Promise School lines up with his identity. The clip of him losing his temper lines up less with his identity, so the brand absorbs it, but it does not break it. That is the power of consistency over time.

## Module 3 — Values, Strengths, Story, Personality, and Interests

**Objective**

By the end of this module, you will be able to list your core values, top strengths, key story moments, personality traits, and outside interests. You will turn this into a Values and Strengths Worksheet for your portfolio.

**Core Concept**

You cannot brand what you do not know.

Most weak athlete brands have one thing in common. The athlete never sat down and asked, "Who am I outside of my sport? What do I actually care about? What makes me different from every other athlete on this campus?"

The strongest brands come from five inputs.

1. Values. What you believe is right and important.
2. Strengths. What you are good at, on and off the field.
3. Story. The moments that shaped you.
4. Personality. How you naturally show up.
5. Interests. The things you care about beyond sport.

When these line up with your content, your deals, and your team behavior, the brand feels real. When they are missing, the brand feels fake or copied.

Why this matters for student-athletes: Athletes get reduced to stats and highlights all the time. If you do not define yourself, the world will define you in the smallest way possible, "the guard," "the receiver," "the runner." Knowing your values helps you turn down deals that pay well but do not fit. Knowing your strengths helps you pitch yourself for the right opportunities. Knowing your story helps you create content that actually connects. Knowing your personality keeps your tone consistent. Knowing your interests opens doors after the season ends.

Key ideas:
1. Values are non-negotiable. Common athlete values include faith, family, hard work, loyalty, honesty, education, justice, community, and discipline.
2. Strengths are more than physical. You also have strengths off the field — calm under pressure, funny, good with kids, can explain hard ideas in simple words.
3. Story is what people remember. Use real names, real places, real moments.
4. Personality is your tone. The wrong move is to fake a personality that is not yours. People feel that.
5. Interests are your bridge to life after sport. Music, fashion, business, faith, education, gaming, design, food, real estate, mental health, coaching, finance, art, community work.

**Scenario**

A first-generation college baseball player grew up around his family's small Mexican restaurant in his neighborhood. He works there in the offseason. He is studying business. He cares about food, family, and community.

Two NIL offers come in.

Offer 1: A national energy drink. $5,000 for three posts. He does not drink energy drinks. The brand does not fit his life.

Offer 2: A local family-owned grocery chain that wants to feature small Latino-owned businesses in the city. $2,500 for one campaign over a month, including a video at his family's restaurant.

If he does not know his values, story, and interests, he probably takes Offer 1 because it pays more. If he does, he sees that Offer 2 builds his brand for years. It tells a real story. It connects him to local business. It gives him content for his own platform. It opens doors with other community brands.

The money is short-term. The brand fit is long-term.

Practical example: A college tennis player loves photography. She posts mostly tennis content but every Sunday she shares a photo essay from her week. Campus life. Teammates. A small business in town. A sunset on the drive home. A local camera shop sees the pattern. They offer her a part-time content role for the summer. That role leads to a freelance gig with a regional magazine. By her senior year, her bio reads "tennis player, photographer, storyteller." Her brand is now bigger than her sport.

## Module 4 — Athlete Positioning and Differentiation

**Objective**

By the end of this module, you will be able to write a one-sentence positioning statement that says clearly who you are, who you serve, and what makes you different.

**Core Concept**

Positioning is the work of carving out a clear lane in a crowded space.

There are thousands of college athletes. Many post similar content. Many have similar stats. Many come from similar backgrounds. A great brand cuts through the noise by being specific.

A strong positioning statement has four parts.

1. Who you are. A short label that sticks.
2. Who you serve. The people you want to reach.
3. What you offer. The value you bring, on or off the field.
4. What makes you different. A unique angle that nobody else owns the same way.

Use this format: "I am a [type of athlete or person] who helps or inspires [audience] by [value, skill, mission, or proof]."

Example: "I am a first-generation college soccer player using discipline, culture, and creativity to inspire young athletes who want to win on the field and build something bigger off it."

Why this matters for student-athletes: Without positioning, every post and every pitch sounds like every other athlete. Brands and audiences cannot tell you apart. With positioning, you become the obvious choice for a specific kind of opportunity. A clear position also protects you. When a deal does not fit your lane, you can pass on it without guilt. When a deal does fit, you can charge more for it because you are the right fit, not just any athlete.

Key ideas:
1. Positioning is not bragging. You are claiming a lane that you can actually live in.
2. Be specific, even if it feels narrow. "First-gen Latina softball player who teaches young players how to balance school, sport, and family" beats "athlete who inspires people."
3. Positioning is not permanent. Your position can grow with you.
4. You can position around three things at once: sport plus story plus interest.
5. The "different" part has to be true. Pick something already true about you.

**Scenario**

A soccer player has 18,000 followers. A volleyball player has 12,000 followers. Both want NIL deals.

The soccer player's content is random. Games, food, friends, mood quotes, gym clips. Her bio just says her name and team.

The volleyball player's content is built around one idea: she is a future sports broadcaster. She posts short post-game analysis videos. She breaks down rivals' offenses. She shadows the campus broadcast team. Her bio says "Volleyball. Future sports broadcaster. Watch the breakdowns."

A local sports talk radio station sees this and offers the volleyball player a paid summer internship plus a content collab. They do not call the soccer player.

The volleyball player has 6,000 fewer followers but a much sharper position. Position beat audience size.

Practical example: Two college quarterbacks at the same level. Quarterback A's bio: "QB1. God first. Trust the process." Quarterback B's bio: "QB1. First in family to go to college. Studying engineering. Building a free youth football camp in Birmingham." When a regional bank decides to sponsor a campus financial literacy event, they reach out to Quarterback B. He has the same stats and followers as A but clearer positioning. That single difference can be worth thousands in deals across a college career.

- Step 5 — Draft three positioning statements (Portfolio Piece 3): Use the format from above. Write three different versions. Read them out loud. Pick the one that sounds most like you and save it as Piece 3.

## Module 5 — Audience and Opportunity Mapping

**Objective**

By the end of this module, you will be able to identify the specific groups of people you want to reach, why each one matters, and where to find them.

**Core Concept**

You cannot speak to everyone. If you try, you reach no one.

Audience mapping is the work of naming who you want to reach, why they matter to your brand, and where they actually spend time.

A good audience map has these layers.

1. Primary audience. The group most likely to engage with your brand right now.
2. Secondary audiences. Groups that matter but are not your daily focus.
3. Opportunity audiences. Groups that connect to deals, jobs, or future moves.

Each audience has a "where," which is the place they actually live: Instagram, TikTok, LinkedIn, YouTube, local events, family gatherings, alumni groups, campus orgs, community programs, podcasts, or even church.

Why this matters for student-athletes: Your audience is not just "fans." It is broken into many smaller groups. Coaches. Recruiters. Brands. Local businesses. Alumni. Media. Younger athletes. Your campus community. Future employers. Family. Each of these groups makes decisions about you based on what they see. A small local business may be a more valuable audience than 50,000 distant followers. An alumni network may matter more for your post-sport career than your top 10 posts.

Key ideas:
1. Primary first. You can only have one primary audience at a time.
2. Secondary audiences are useful, not central. You serve them sometimes but you do not build your brand around them.
3. Opportunity audiences sit behind the deal. Brands, employers, and partners are not the audience — they are the buyers of access to your audience.
4. Where matters as much as who. Map carefully where each group spends time.
5. Community is more valuable than crowd. Brands pay more for community engagement than for vanity follower counts.

**Scenario**

A wrestler with 4,500 followers wants more brand deals. He thinks his audience is "wrestling fans." A mentor pushes him to map it out.

When he does, he finds:
• Primary audience: high school wrestlers in his state who want to make a college roster.
• Secondary audience: parents of those wrestlers.
• Opportunity audience: local sports medicine clinics, supplement brands with regional roots, and the strength coach network.

He starts posting weekly tips for high school wrestlers, doing free clinics with a local club, and tagging local clinics in recovery posts. Within a year, he has paid deals with a local chiropractor, a regional supplement brand, and a wrestling shoe seller. None of them care about his total follower count. They care that he owns a clear audience.

Practical example: A women's basketball player at a smaller school positioned herself as a future coach and mentor for young girls in her sport. Her audience map — Primary: middle school and high school girls who play basketball in her city. Secondary: their coaches and parents. Opportunity: local youth basketball camps, a regional shoe brand, a girls' sports nonprofit, and the campus athletic department. She runs a free monthly skills tip series, partners with a city camp, and posts short interview clips with veteran women's coaches. By her junior year, the campus athletic department hires her as a paid student ambassador. She had 7,200 followers. Position plus a clear audience map beat raw reach.

## Module 6 — Storytelling and Message Development

**Objective**

By the end of this module, you will be able to write a clear personal brand story you can use in a bio, a pitch, an interview, or a partnership intro.

**Core Concept**

Stats fade. Stories travel.

People remember the athlete who told the story about their grandmother working two jobs to pay for cleats. They forget the athlete who only listed their stats. Your story is not a sad story or a hype story. It is the truth, told in a way that helps people understand who you are and why you matter.

A strong brand story has four parts.

1. Origin. Where you come from. The people, places, and conditions that shaped you.
2. Struggle. A real challenge you faced and what it taught you.
3. Discipline. What you do, day in and day out, to stay on the path.
4. Future. Where you are heading and why it matters.

You do not have to share every detail. You only share what is true, useful, and ready to be public.

Why this matters for student-athletes: Brands and audiences make decisions with their hearts before their heads. A clear story makes the heart move first. That is what gets you the brand call, the interview, the speaking spot, the role. A clear story also protects you when things go wrong. If people already know your story, they give you more grace in tough moments.

Key ideas:
1. Story is not oversharing. Share what you are ready to share and what helps the brand you want to build.
2. Specific beats general. Use real names, real places, real moments.
3. The "so what" is everything. Every story moment should connect to a "so what" — what it means for who you are today.
4. Discipline is the receipt. Your daily habits are the receipts that prove the story is real.
5. Future is the bridge. The future part of your story is what connects to brands, schools, jobs, and your post-sport life.

**Scenario**

Two football players. Same school. Same position. Same year.

Player A's pitch: "I am a starting linebacker. I have 78 tackles. I want a brand deal."

Player B's pitch: "I grew up in a small town an hour outside Charlotte. My dad was a high school coach who never made more than 38K a year. I lost him during my freshman year of college. I am open to brand partners who want to support youth football in small communities."

Player A had the stats. Player B had the story.

Practical example — a track athlete's brand story: "I grew up in southwest Atlanta. That is origin, struggle, discipline, and future in one paragraph.

## Module 8 — Content Pillars and the 30-Day Content Plan

**Objective**

By the end of this module, you will be able to define three to five content pillars and build a 30-day content plan that matches your brand, audience, and life as an athlete.

**Core Concept**

Random posting is the enemy of a strong brand. Content pillars solve that. Pillars are the three to five core topics you build your content around so every post reinforces who you are.

A strong pillar system has these traits.

1. Three to five pillars only. More is too much to maintain.
2. Each pillar is tied to your positioning, audience, or interests.
3. Each pillar can produce many post ideas.
4. Pillars rotate so you do not burn out or repeat the same idea back to back.

A 30-day content plan is just your pillars filled into a calendar with specific post ideas.

Why this matters for student-athletes: You have a busy life. Practice, games, travel, classes, family, work, and sleep. You do not have time to start every day asking, "What should I post?" Pillars give you a system that saves time and keeps your brand strong even on busy weeks. Brands also pay more for accounts with consistent content themes.

Key ideas:
1. Pick pillars that fit your real life. If you do not lift heavy, do not make a "gym beast" pillar.
2. Rotate pillars across the week. Pillar 1 on Monday, Pillar 2 on Wednesday, Pillar 3 on Friday, Pillar 4 on Saturday.
3. Use the "show, teach, share, repeat" model — show what you did, teach what you learned, share a story, repeat what works.
4. Plan in batches. Use one or two days a week to plan and shoot multiple posts.
5. Track simple signals — most saves/comments, most DMs, followers from your target audience.

**Scenario**

A college tennis player tried to post every day with no plan. She burned out after three weeks. Her engagement dropped. She felt fake.

She rebuilt her content around four pillars: "On the Court," "Off the Court," "Off the Wall" (her humor and personality), and "Off to the Future" (her interest in becoming a sports journalist).

Each week she planned eight posts. Two per pillar. She used Sundays to plan and shoot. By the end of the semester, she had her first paid deal with a local racket brand and a content collab with the campus sports radio show. She did not post more. She posted on purpose.

Practical example — a wrestler's four pillars: Mat Work (training clips, technique breakdowns), Mind Work (books, mental game tips), Mentor Work (free tips for high school wrestlers in his state), Home Work (family, faith, hometown, community). Across 30 days he rotated each pillar weekly so his audience knew exactly who he was and what to expect.

## Module 9 — NIL, Sponsorships, and Brand Deals

**Objective**

By the end of this module, you will be able to explain what NIL is, what kinds of deals exist, how to pitch yourself, and what to expect at different audience sizes.

**Core Concept**

NIL stands for Name, Image, and Likeness. An NIL deal is any agreement where you are paid in money, product, or services in exchange for the use of your name, image, or likeness.

Common NIL deal types include: social media posts; appearances at stores, camps, or events; commercials and video content; photo shoots; autograph signings; camps and clinics you run; merch lines and product collabs; affiliate codes and discount links; speaking engagements.

A clean NIL deal has three traits. There is a real business purpose. There are clearly defined deliverables. The pay is in a reasonable range for what you bring.

A bad deal often has one or more red flags. It pays only for athletic performance with no real promotional work. It locks you up for too long. It uses your image in ways you did not agree to. It asks you to do things that break school or NCAA rules.

Why this matters for student-athletes: NIL is one of the biggest changes in college sport in the last 50 years. Used well, it can pay for groceries, family help, a car, a savings account, even a future business. Used poorly, it can hurt your eligibility, your reputation, and your future.

Key ideas:
1. Know the three legal pieces of NIL — real business purpose, defined promotional activity, reasonable compensation.
2. School compliance comes first. Send your deals to compliance before you sign. Some deals must be reported through the NCAA's NIL Go platform.
3. The pitch is real work. A strong pitch has six things — intro/positioning, audience snapshot, content examples, what you offer, starter price or range, contact info.
4. Pricing principles. Charge more when you are giving up exclusivity, time, or usage rights.
5. Watch the categories. Gambling, alcohol, vape/tobacco/cannabis, adult content, "get rich quick" schemes, and small or new crypto/meme coins all carry extra risk.

**Scenario**

With education, he sees Brand 1's exclusivity could block him from many supplement deals for a full year, Brand 2 is small but flexible and a great local fit, and Brand 3 fits his story (first in his family to go to college, cares about money education), has clear deliverables, and pays well for the time. He chooses Brand 3 first and Brand 2 second. He passes on Brand 1 unless the exclusivity drops.

## Module 10 — Reading the Deal: Deliverables, Pricing, Usage Rights, Exclusivity, and Payment Terms

**Objective**

By the end of this module, you will be able to read a basic brand deal, identify the key terms, and use a checklist to decide whether to sign.

**Core Concept**

Most athletes lose money or freedom because they did not read the contract. A short contract can still have terms that quietly cost you for months.

Five terms control most of the value in a deal.

1. Deliverables. Exactly what you must produce.
2. Pricing. What you get paid and when.
3. Usage rights. Where the brand can use your content, how long, and on which platforms.
4. Exclusivity. Whether you are blocked from working with similar brands and for how long.
5. Payment terms. Net days, kill fees, late penalties, and how you get paid.

Other terms matter too: morality clauses, approvals, revisions, term length, and termination.

Why this matters for student-athletes: A deal can pay $1,000 today and cost you $10,000 in lost deals over the next year if the exclusivity is too wide. A deal can promise a $500 post and turn into 12 hours of unpaid work if revisions and approvals are not capped. A deal can use your face in a commercial for two years for $300 if usage rights are sloppy.

Key ideas:
1. Deliverables: get specific. Not "a few posts" but "Two Instagram in-feed posts, one Instagram reel under 30 seconds, three Stories, all posted between June 1 and June 30."
2. Pricing: line by line. Know what each deliverable is worth and what triggers extra pay.
3. Usage rights: where, how, how long. "Perpetual" or "all media, worldwide" should usually trigger a much higher rate or a shorter term.
4. Exclusivity: scope and time. Limited to direct competitors, not whole industries.
5. Payment terms: Net 30 over Net 90.

The Brand Deal Checklist (use before signing): real business purpose written in; clear deliverables with dates; total pay and per-item pay listed; usage rights limited in place, format, and time; exclusivity limited to direct competitors and a clear time window; payment terms specified, ideally Net 30; revisions capped (1 or 2); approval process and timeline written down; morality clause is mutual and specific; termination clause is fair to both sides; FTC disclosure language matches the law; school compliance has reviewed and approved.

**Scenario**

A college lacrosse player signs a $1,200 deal with a local boutique without reading the deal closely. The contract says: 2 Instagram posts, 3 Stories, usage rights "perpetual, all media, worldwide," exclusivity "all retail clothing brands for 12 months," Net 90 payment.

She does the work. She gets paid 91 days later. She turns down two better clothing deals over the next year. The boutique still uses her face in their store window and on their website two years later, with no extra pay. The "perpetual" usage rights gave them unlimited time. The "all retail clothing" exclusivity blocked her from real deals. Net 90 made cash flow tight.

If she had read each line, she could have asked for changes: limited usage to 6 months, exclusivity only on direct competitors, Net 30 instead of Net 90.

Practical example: A swimmer is offered $900 for two posts and one story for a local health bar brand. The first draft says: usage perpetual/all media; exclusivity 12 months/all snack and bar brands; Net 60. She asks for changes — usage 6 months/organic social only, exclusivity 90 days/direct health bar competitors only, Net 30. The brand agrees to all three changes. The total pay stays the same. Her real value goes up. She is free to work with a competing brand 91 days later.

## Module 11 — FTC Disclosure, NIL Compliance, and Brand Protection Basics

**Objective**

By the end of this module, you will be able to disclose brand deals correctly under FTC rules, understand basic NIL compliance steps, and protect your own brand using simple intellectual property knowledge.

**Core Concept**

You can have the best brand in the world and still get in trouble if you do not handle three things: disclosure, compliance, and protection.

1. FTC disclosure. When you are paid or given anything of value to talk about a product, you must tell your audience clearly.
2. NIL compliance. Your school, conference, and the NCAA have rules about how you handle NIL deals, what you report, and what you cannot do.
3. Brand protection. Your name, your logos, your slogans, your content, and your image have legal value. Trademarks, copyright, and contract clauses help you protect them.

Why this matters for student-athletes: A missed disclosure can mean fines, lost deals, and damage to your reputation. A missed compliance step can cost you eligibility. Weak protection can let other people make money off your name without paying you.

FTC disclosure basics: The FTC says any "material connection" between a brand and an endorser must be disclosed clearly. A material connection includes money, free product, discounts, gifts, family ties, or any other thing of value. Disclose at the top of the post, not buried at the end. Use clear words like "#ad," "#sponsored," or "Paid partnership with [Brand]." Disclose verbally and on-screen in videos and Stories. Do not assume your audience already knows. Disclose even if you would have said the same thing without being paid.

The Disclosure Checklist (use before every brand post): Is there a clear "ad," "sponsored," or "paid partnership" tag at the top? If it is a video, is there a verbal and on-screen disclosure? If it is a Story, is the disclosure visible the whole time? Did I use the language the brand and my compliance office agreed on? Did I avoid words like "free" when I am actually being paid? Did I keep the post true to my real experience?

NIL compliance basics: Each school has a compliance office. Most schools require you to disclose NIL deals before signing, report deals over certain dollar amounts through the school or NIL Go platform, avoid deals in banned categories, avoid pay-for-play, and avoid using school marks unless allowed.

Brand protection basics: Trademark protects brand names, logos, slogans, and identifiers tied to goods or services. Copyright protects original creative works like photos, videos, music, and writing — the person who takes the photo usually owns it, not the person in the photo. Right of publicity protects your name, image, and likeness from being used commercially without your permission. School marks (logos, names, fight songs) are owned by the school.

Do not sign red flags: "perpetual, all media, worldwide" usage with low pay; no FTC disclosure language; vague deliverables and unlimited revisions; exclusivity that covers most of your future career options; categories your school bans; "pay-for-play" language; pressure to sign in less than 24 hours without compliance review.

**Scenario**

A men's basketball player signs an NIL deal with a sneaker brand. He posts a video of him lacing up the shoes with a caption that does not say it is a paid ad. A reporter notices. A complaint reaches the FTC. The brand cuts the deal. The athletic department gets involved. The post stays up online for years as a stain on his brand.

If he had simply added "#ad" or "Paid partnership with [Brand]" clearly at the top of the caption and in the video itself, none of that happens.

Practical example: A college softball player landed a paid partnership with a healthy snack brand. She: 1) Sent the contract to her compliance office. They approved with two small edits. 2) Filed the deal through NIL Go because it crossed the dollar threshold. 3) Added "Paid partnership with [Brand]" at the top of every caption and on-screen in her video. 4) Verbally said, "This is a paid partnership with [Brand]" in the first three seconds of each video. 5) Avoided using her school's logo or fight song in the content. 6) Got a small written license to use the brand's product photo in her Story. She kept the deal clean. The brand re-signed her the next semester.

## Module 12 — Reputation, Crisis Decision-Making, and Life After Sports

**Objective**

By the end of this module, you will be able to handle a public mistake or attack with a clear plan, and you will understand how your brand can support you in life after college sport.

**Core Concept**

Every brand will face a hard moment. A bad post, a viral mistake, an unfair attack, a bad loss, a tough interview, a video taken out of context. The athletes who survive these moments are not the ones who never face them. They are the ones who have a plan.

Your reputation lives in three time zones at once.

1. Before the moment. Built through years of small actions and consistent content.
2. During the moment. Decided in minutes or hours, often under stress.
3. After the moment. Shaped over weeks and months by how you respond and what you do next.

Your brand can also outlive your playing career. Many athletes finish college sport before they turn 23. Most do not become professional athletes. The brand work you do in college becomes the runway for whatever you do next.

Why this matters for student-athletes: A bad moment with no plan can wreck a career in a weekend. A bad moment with a plan often becomes a comeback story that strengthens the brand. The same is true for the years after college sport.

Key ideas:
1. Build a "before the moment" foundation. A strong content history, clear story, and good relationships with mentors give you backup when things go wrong.
2. The 24-hour rule. Unless your life or someone else's is at risk, do not post in the first hour. Take 24 hours when possible.
3. The 3-step crisis response: Pause and check. Decide on a public response. Show, do not argue.
4. The Crisis Response Plan template — first call, second call, compliance contact, hours of silence, statement format (one sentence acknowledgement, one sentence context, one sentence action), 30-day recovery plan.
5. Brand beyond the jersey. Your brand becomes the foundation of life after sport — internships, full-time roles, small business, speaking, media, coaching, broadcasting, training, community work, and continued NIL earning.

**Scenario**

Two athletes at the same school each get caught in a viral moment.

Athlete A is filmed yelling at a referee. The clip goes viral. He responds within an hour with an angry tweet, deletes it, posts a second one blaming the camera person, and goes silent for three weeks. The story drags on. Two brand deals fall through.

Athlete B is filmed during a heated postgame interview. The clip is unfair. She follows her plan: 1) She does not post within the first hour. 2) She talks to a trusted mentor, her family, and her compliance contact. 3) She posts a short, calm statement within 24 hours that takes responsibility for what she can own, explains the context briefly, and shares one specific action she is taking. 4) She does not get into comment-section fights. 5) She returns to her normal content rhythm within a week. 6) She follows up with two pieces of content over the next month that show real action.

A month later, the story is forgotten. Her two pending deals close.

Practical example: A college tight end never went pro. By his senior year, his brand pillars were faith, family, food, and football culture. He started a small content series breaking down college football meals at different schools. After he graduated, a regional sports network hired him as a part-time on-air contributor. He kept his pillars. He kept his content rhythm. He turned his "after sport" career into his main career within two years. A college soccer player who did not get drafted built her brand around women's sports media and coaching. By the time she finished her last season, she had paid speaking spots at three high schools, a part-time job with a girls' youth league, and a podcast with two former teammates.
