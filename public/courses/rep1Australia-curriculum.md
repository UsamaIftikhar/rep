# Rep1 Australian Student-Athlete Academy: Curriculum Content

This course provides information vital to athletes transitioning from Australia to America to make the transition as smooth as possible.

## Section 1: Explore, Choose a Pathway, and Prepare for Recruiting

### Module 1 — Decide Whether the U.S. Football Pathway Fits You

Before anything else, be honest about whether chasing American football in the United States is the right move for you, your family, and your future. This module helps you make that decision with clear eyes rather than highlight-reel hope.

**Why Australian Athletes Consider the U.S. Pathway**

American college football offers something Australia does not: a structured system where sport and tertiary education run together, with professional-standard coaching, facilities, medical support, and competition every week of the season.

For most Australians the real prize is not the NFL. It is a funded or part-funded degree, four years of elite athletic development, and an international network you cannot build at home.

Common motivations worth naming honestly:
• Access to a U.S. degree at reduced cost
• Coaching and strength programs beyond what is locally available
• Genuine competition against thousands of players your age
• A shot (a small one) at professional football
• Living overseas and growing up fast

**Interest, Recruitment, Offer, Admission, Eligibility, and Enrollment: Six Different Things**

Six different things happen on the way from watching a game on TV to actually enrolling at a U.S. school. Coaches, and the internet, use these words loosely. Learn to use them precisely, because confusing one for another is the most common way Australian families get their hopes set on something that hasn't actually happened yet.

Interest: you, or a coach, notice a fit. This can be as small as you emailing a program or a coach liking one of your posts. Nothing has been evaluated yet and nobody has committed to anything.

Recruitment: a coach is actively evaluating you: watching film, checking your grades, maybe attending a game or camp where you're playing. You are being considered. You are not chosen.

An offer: a coach has told you, specifically and ideally in writing, that they want you on the roster, on stated terms. An offer is real, but it is not final until two more things happen.

Admission: the university itself, separately from the football program, has agreed to enrol you as a student. Coaches do not control this. A strong offer means nothing if the admissions office declines you.

Eligibility: the relevant governing body (NCAA, NAIA, or NJCAA) has certified that your academic record and amateur status meet its rules. This is a separate process from admission and can take months.

Enrollment: you have actually registered for classes and shown up. This is the only stage that is truly final.

What this looks like in practice: a DM saying 'love your tape' is interest, not recruitment. A coach saying 'we'd love to have you' in conversation is not an offer. Ask for it in writing. An offer is not admission; you still apply and get accepted like any other student. Being admitted is not the same as being eligible to play; that's a separate certification. None of it is final until you're enrolled and in class.

Treat every stage as provisional until you've reached the next one, and never let anyone, including yourself, describe an earlier stage using the language of a later one.

**What the Pathway Actually Involves**

This is a multi-year project, not a tryout. From first serious decision to first game snap in the U.S. is typically 12 to 30 months.

The rough sequence looks like this:
1. Decide, with your family, that you are committing to it
2. Learn the system and pick realistic target levels
3. Build film, measurables, and an academic record
4. Contact coaches and keep contacting them
5. Meet eligibility and admissions requirements
6. Receive and evaluate offers
7. Secure a visa and depart

Each stage has its own paperwork, deadlines, and cost. Skipping a stage almost always means repeating it later.

**The Time and Money It Really Takes**

Even a fully funded scholarship does not make this free. Budget honestly for the costs that fall on your family regardless of the offer you receive.

Typical out-of-pocket items:
• International flights, often more than once a year
• Visa application and associated fees
• Standardised testing and document certification
• Filming, editing, and profile hosting
• Travel within Australia for combines or testing
• Insurance, phone, and initial living set-up in the U.S.

Time cost is just as real. Expect training most days, film work weekly, and email and admin work that lands on top of Year 11 and 12 study load.

**Physical Readiness and Position Reality**

American football is a specialist sport. Coaches recruit positions, not athletes in general, and the physical profile for each position is well defined.

Be realistic about where you actually sit:
• Compare your height, weight, and speed to published rosters at the level you are targeting, not the level you dream about
• Understand that some positions (punter, kicker, offensive line) have historically been the most accessible entry points for Australians
• Contact and technical skill gaps can be coached; frame size and top-end speed largely cannot

If you are two or three years away physically, that is useful information, not a rejection. It tells you whether to target a prep year, a JUCO route, or a later recruiting cycle.

**Academic Readiness**

Athletic ability gets you looked at. Academics decide whether you are actually admissible and eligible to play.

What matters:
• Consistent results across Years 10 to 12, not one strong semester
• Subject choices that map to U.S. core-course requirements
• English proficiency evidence, even as a native speaker, at some institutions
• A clean, complete, certified transcript

An athlete with average film and strong academics has far more options than the reverse. Grades widen your list of possible schools and unlock academic money that sits on top of any athletic support.

**The Family Conversation** *(callout)*

Sit down with your parents or guardians early. Talk openly about cost, distance, timelines, and what happens if it does not work out. This pathway is a household decision, not an individual one.

**Alternatives Worth Considering**

The U.S. pathway is one option among several, and choosing something else is not a failure.

Worth weighing up:
• Australian university plus local gridiron or another code
• Domestic elite pathways in AFL, rugby league, or rugby union where your athletic profile may transfer
• A gap year of dedicated development in Australia, then re-entering recruiting a cycle later
• U.S. college for academics only, walking on to a team once you are there

Write down what you would do if the U.S. pathway does not eventuate. Having that answer ready removes desperation from every decision that follows, and desperation is what predatory services sell to.

**An Honest Self-Assessment**

Interest is not the same as readiness. Wanting this (watching American football, imagining a scholarship, picturing yourself on a U.S. campus) is a completely normal starting point, and it doesn't mean you're ready to start actively recruiting yet. Readiness is built, not felt. It has five parts: academic, athletic, financial, emotional, and family. You can be genuinely excited about this pathway and still be twelve months away from being ready to pursue it seriously. That's useful information, not a setback.

Two parts of readiness are easy to underestimate because they have nothing to do with football or grades. The first is independence: living away from home means managing your own routine, your own study schedule, your own laundry, your own decisions about what to eat, with no parent down the hall to catch you if you slip. The second is cultural adjustment: American school, football, and social culture run on different norms than Australian ones: more direct in some ways, more formal in others, different expectations around independence and self-advocacy, and a real chance of culture shock in the first few months no matter how prepared you feel. Adjustment takes time, and needing time to adjust is not a sign you made the wrong choice. This academy covers cultural adjustment and mental health in full in a later section. This is just the first flag that it's coming.

Answer the questions below in writing. Vague answers are the warning sign, not a failing grade.

1. Why do I want this, in one sentence, without mentioning the NFL?
2. What are my current height, weight, and tested speed and strength numbers?
3. What level of program do those numbers realistically fit?
4. What are my actual grades, and are they trending up or down?
5. What can my family contribute financially without strain?
6. Who in my life will tell me an uncomfortable truth about my ability?
7. Am I prepared to be far from home, injured, and not playing, for a season?
8. What is my plan if I never receive an offer?

If several of the following are true right now, that's not a reason to give up. It's a signal to spend more time preparing before recruiting starts in earnest:
• Your academic records aren't organised or up to date
• You don't have usable film, or it's out of date
• Your measurements haven't been verified by anyone but you
• You can't explain in one sentence why you want this
• Your family hasn't discussed the financial side yet
• You're relying on someone's promise rather than your own preparation
• You're not ready to write or send an email yourself
• You haven't actually looked into which pathway fits you
• You feel uncomfortable asking an adult for help with any of this
• You're doing this because someone else wants it for you, not because you do

None of these are disqualifying on their own. Several of them together mean: keep building for now, and come back to active recruiting when more of this list is false.

**If It Sounds Too Good to Be True** *(callout)*

Guaranteed scholarships, promises of specific schools, and up-front fees for 'placement' are red flags. Nobody can guarantee you a roster spot.

**Key Takeaways**

The U.S. football pathway rewards athletes who are physically, academically, and financially prepared, and who decide with their family rather than in isolation. Deciding not to pursue it is a legitimate, informed outcome of this module.

**Next Action**

Your first preparation plan, before you contact a single coach:
1. Identify your current stage: using the six-stage ladder above, honestly name where you actually are today.
2. Record your academic and athletic information: pull together your current grades, ATAR trajectory, and any measurements or film you already have, even if incomplete.
3. Identify your gaps: compare what you have against what Module 3 says coaches expect, and write down what's missing.
4. Choose your trusted adults: name the parent or guardian, and the coach, who will be part of this with you from here.
5. Pick your next three actions: specific, not vague. 'Get my 40 time tested' beats 'get faster.'
6. Set an initial timeline: a rough date to revisit this plan, not a deadline to feel pressured by.
7. Know where to go next: once you've done the above, Module 2 will help you work out which pathway actually fits your situation.

Write all seven down and share them with a parent or guardian this week.

### Module 2 — Understand U.S. Football and Education Pathways

The American system is not one pathway. It is several, each with different rules, costs, and outcomes. This module maps them so you know exactly what you are aiming at.

**U.S. High School Football**

American high school football is the base of the pyramid and the main place college coaches recruit from. Players typically have three to four seasons of tape by the time they are recruited.

As an Australian this matters for one reason: you are competing against athletes with far more football-specific experience than you. Some Australians close that gap by attending a U.S. high school for a year, usually as an exchange or international student.

Be aware that transferring into a U.S. high school carries its own eligibility rules, residency questions, and cost, and that a year of high school football does not guarantee college interest.

**Prep Schools and Post-Graduate Years**

A prep school or post-graduate ('PG') year is an extra year of schooling after finishing Year 12, taken at a U.S. private school with a strong football program.

Why athletes use it:
• Adds a year of physical development and football-specific coaching
• Produces U.S.-based film that college coaches trust
• Gives time to lift academic results or standardised test scores
• Puts you physically in front of recruiters

The trade-off is cost. Prep schools are usually fee-paying and partial scholarships are competitive. Treat it as an investment decision with a defined budget and an exit point.

**Football Academies as a Distinct Pathway**

A football academy is a training-first operation built around the sport, not a traditional school with a football team attached. That distinction matters more than it sounds.

A prep school is an accredited school where football is one part of a normal academic institution. A football academy is often a standalone program, sometimes attached loosely to a school for academic credit, sometimes running its own academic component of variable quality, built primarily around specialised coaching, strength and conditioning, and exposure to recruiters.

What they typically offer:
• Position-specific coaching at a level most Australian environments can't match
• A concentrated training environment with athletes from multiple countries chasing the same pathway
• Organised exposure to recruiters through showcases and connections

What to check before committing to one:
• Whether its academic component is properly accredited: this can directly affect NCAA or NAIA eligibility later, not just admission
• Who actually owns and runs it, and how long it's operated
• Whether the athletes it claims to have placed can be verified on official college rosters
• The full cost, including accommodation, and what happens if you leave early

Academies range from genuinely excellent to unregulated businesses selling access. Treat verifying one the same way you'd verify any paid recruiting service (covered in full in Module 5).

**NCAA Division I, II, and III**

The NCAA is the largest governing body for U.S. college sport and is split into three divisions.

• Division I: the highest level, largest budgets, most visibility. Includes the FBS and FCS subdivisions in football. Athletic scholarships are available.
• Division II: strong competition with a broader academic-athletic balance. Athletic scholarships are available and often partial.
• Division III: no athletic scholarships. Financial support comes through academic and need-based aid, which can still be substantial.

Division III being scholarship-free does not make it a lesser destination. Many Australians end up better supported at a D-III or D-II school that genuinely wants them than as a fringe D-I roster addition.

**NAIA Programs**

The NAIA is a separate governing body from the NCAA, made up largely of smaller private colleges.

What to know:
• Athletic scholarships are available
• Eligibility rules and initial certification differ from the NCAA and are often more straightforward for international students
• Programs are smaller, which can mean earlier playing time and closer coach relationships
• Academic quality varies widely, so research the institution as carefully as the football program

The NAIA is frequently overlooked by Australians and is one of the more realistic entry points.

**Junior College (JUCO)**

Junior colleges are two-year institutions. In football they function as a development and second-chance route into four-year programs.

Why it is used:
• Lower academic entry requirements than most four-year schools
• Two years to develop, produce film, and lift grades
• A well-worn path into Division I and II via transfer

The realities: facilities and support are usually modest, international scholarship availability is limited, and the transfer is not automatic. Going JUCO means committing to two demanding years with the second half of the plan still unwritten.

**How Scholarships Actually Work**

A 'full ride' is the exception, not the norm. Understanding the components protects you from misreading an offer.

An athletic scholarship may cover some or all of:
• Tuition
• Fees
• Room (accommodation)
• Board (meals)
• Books or a book stipend

What it usually does not cover:
• International flights
• Visa and immigration costs
• Personal expenses, phone, and travel home
• Health cover gaps

Scholarships are also typically awarded year by year rather than guaranteed for four years. Always ask, in writing, what is covered, for how long, and under what conditions it can be reduced.

**Preferred Walk-On and Walk-On Roles**

A walk-on joins the team without an athletic scholarship. A preferred walk-on has a guaranteed roster spot but no athletic money.

This is a legitimate route, especially for Australians whose film is thin. Many walk-ons earn scholarships in later years.

Before accepting, get clarity on:
• Whether the roster spot is actually guaranteed
• Whether you receive the same medical, academic, and training support as scholarship players
• What specifically would need to happen for you to be placed on scholarship
• Whether your family can fund the full cost for at least one year

**Redshirting Explained**

Redshirting means sitting out a season of competition (no games) while still practising, training, and studying full-time, without it counting against your four years of playing eligibility. It is common, not unusual, and not a judgement on your ability.

Why programs use it: to let a young player develop physically and technically for a year before facing older, bigger competition; to manage the roster across multiple graduating classes; or to allow recovery from an injury (a medical redshirt, which is a separate process with its own rules).

Advantages: an extra year of strength and conditioning before competing, more time to learn the playbook and earn a real role, and an extra year on campus to work toward your degree.

Tradeoffs: a longer total time away from home before your playing career is done, and (if a professional career is part of your thinking) a delayed timeline. It does not, on its own, affect your scholarship.

Common misconceptions to drop: redshirting is not a punishment, it does not mean a coach has lost interest in you, and it is not automatic. Some players never redshirt at all.

When to raise it with a coach: during recruiting, directly ask whether they expect you to redshirt in year one. Get their honest expectation before you commit, not after you arrive. A coach who won't answer the question plainly is itself worth noting.

**The U.S. Recruiting and Academic Calendar**

The American year runs on a different rhythm to the Australian one, and missing the rhythm costs you a cycle.

Broad shape of the year:
• Northern autumn (Aug–Dec): season, coaches are busy and hardest to reach
• Winter (Dec–Feb): signing periods and the most intense recruiting activity
• Spring (Mar–May): spring practice, evaluation, and camp planning
• Summer (Jun–Aug): camps, official visits, and pre-season

The critical mismatch for Australians: the U.S. academic year starts in August, while ours finishes in November or December. Plan on a gap of six to eight months between finishing Year 12 and starting college, and plan what you will do with it.

**How Australian Schooling Lines Up**

U.S. institutions read your Australian record through their own framework, so know the translation.

• Year 12 completion is generally treated as equivalent to U.S. high school graduation
• Your ATAR or equivalent state certificate is the primary academic evidence
• Subject-by-subject transcripts, not just the final ranking, are usually required
• Some institutions require certified translations or credential evaluations even for English-language documents
• Standardised testing may still be required regardless of your Australian results

Order certified copies of everything early. Document delays are one of the most common and most avoidable reasons an athlete misses an intake.

**Get Your Australian Coach Involved** *(callout)*

Your local coach can verify your position, measurables, and film honestly. U.S. programs weigh third-party verification heavily.

**Key Takeaways**

There is no single U.S. pathway. Knowing the difference between NCAA divisions, NAIA, JUCO, and prep schools lets you target programs realistically, and the calendar mismatch with the Australian school year needs planning for, not discovering.

**Next Action**

Shortlist the two pathway types that best match your age, academics, and current level.

### Module 3 — Build Your Recruiting Profile

Your recruiting profile is the package a coach sees before they ever meet you: film, measurables, academics, and contact details. This module shows you how to build one that survives a 30-second review.

**What Coaches Actually Look For**

A recruiting coordinator may open hundreds of profiles a week. Yours gets seconds, not minutes.

In rough priority order they want:
1. Position and current measurables, immediately visible
2. Film that shows the traits that position requires
3. Evidence you are academically admissible
4. Confirmation you are eligible and available for a specific year
5. A way to contact you and someone who can verify you

Everything else (logos, music, motivational text) is noise. If a coach has to hunt for your height, weight, position, and graduation year, you have already lost them.

**Coachability, Character, Reliability, Leadership, and Work Ethic**

Coaches can measure your speed with a stopwatch. They can't measure your character the same way, but they still try, from a distance, using the evidence available to them.

Coachability is whether you take instruction and adjust, rather than defending every mistake. It shows up in film: does a player who gets beaten on a rep change technique next play, or repeat the same error? It shows up in references too: a coach asked 'does this kid actually listen?' is answering a coachability question.

Character covers how you carry yourself under pressure, after a dropped pass, a bad call, a loss. Full-game film, not just highlights, is where this actually gets evaluated, because it's the only place a coach sees you fail and respond.

Reliability is about consistency: do you show up, do you follow through on commitments, does your effort look the same in the fourth quarter of a blowout as in a close game. Coaches ask your school and club coaches directly about this.

Leadership doesn't require a captaincy. It shows in how you talk to teammates on film, whether younger players are drawn to you, and how your references describe your role on a team even if it isn't formally titled.

Work ethic is inferred from your training history, your improvement year over year, and, again, what the people who've actually coached you say when asked directly.

None of these can be faked convincingly for long. The honest move is to actually be coachable, reliable, and a good teammate, and let your references and film reflect it, not to try to perform these traits for an audience that isn't watching yet.

**Measurables and Testing Numbers**

Publish real numbers, measured recently, in units Americans use.

Standard set to have ready:
• Height and weight (feet/inches and pounds, with metric alongside)
• 40-yard dash
• Vertical jump and broad jump
• Shuttle and three-cone times
• Bench press, squat, and power clean where relevant
• Position-specific numbers (for example, punt hang time and net distance)

Have them measured by a coach or at a recognised testing event rather than self-reported. Inflated numbers are found out at the first camp, and the reputational damage is permanent.

**Choosing and Proving Your Position**

Coaches recruit specialists. 'Athlete' is not a position, and listing three positions signals you have not committed to one.

How to choose:
• Match your frame, speed, and skill set to published rosters at your target level
• Ask an experienced coach where you project, not where you enjoy playing
• Pick one primary position and, at most, one secondary

Then prove it. Your film, your testing numbers, and your training should all point at the same position. Consistency across the whole profile is what makes it credible.

**Filming Your Games and Training**

Film quality is often the limiting factor for Australian athletes, and it is entirely within your control.

Practical rules:
• Film wide enough to show the play developing, not zoomed onto the ball
• Elevated and side-on beats ground level and end-on
• Steady camera, stable mount, no shaky handheld
• Film every game you can: you cannot edit tape you never captured
• Note the date, opponent, and your jersey number for every session

Phone footage from a tripod at height is perfectly acceptable. Poorly framed footage from an expensive camera is not.

**Building a Highlight Reel**

The highlight reel exists to earn a second look, nothing more.

What works:
• Three to five minutes maximum, best plays first
• Your jersey number and a clear marker on you at the start of each clip
• Name, position, height, weight, graduation year, and email on the opening frame
• Full-speed plays, then a slow-motion repeat only where genuinely useful
• No music, no long intro, no highlight of you celebrating

What kills it: a 40-second title sequence, clips where nobody can find you, and plays that show effort rather than ability.

**Why Full Game Film Still Matters**

Highlights show your ceiling. Full games show your floor, and serious coaches want both.

Full game film reveals what a reel hides: your stance, your assignment discipline, whether you finish plays, how you respond after a mistake, and how you carry yourself on the sideline.

Keep two or three complete games available and linked from your profile. Being willing to share them signals confidence; refusing to share them signals the opposite.

**Presenting Your Academic Record**

Make your academics as easy to assess as your film.

Have ready:
• A current transcript covering Years 10 to 12
• Your predicted or achieved ATAR or state equivalent
• Subject list, with mathematics, English, and science clearly identified
• Any standardised test scores
• A brief plain-English note explaining the Australian system

That last item matters more than athletes expect. A coach who cannot interpret your results will move on to a profile they can read in ten seconds.

**Coach and Teacher References**

A reference is someone a coach can call who has actually watched you compete or work, and will tell the truth about it. It is one of the few parts of your profile a coach trusts more than anything you say about yourself.

Who to line up:
• Your current head coach or position coach: the most valuable reference, since they can speak to on-field ability and coachability directly
• A strength and conditioning coach, if you have one, for training history and work ethic
• A teacher, where appropriate: particularly useful for confirming character and reliability outside football, and for backing up your academic record

What makes a reference useful rather than generic:
• They've actually seen you compete or work recently, not years ago
• They can speak in specifics (a game, a habit, a moment), not just 'he's a good kid'
• They're willing to be contacted directly by a U.S. coach, by phone or email
• You've told them in advance that they may be contacted, and roughly what for

How to set it up: ask directly whether they're comfortable being listed, give them your one-page profile so they know what you're presenting, and include their name, role, and contact details, with permission, in your recruiting profile and your first email where relevant.

A reference who takes a week to reply, or seems surprised to be contacted, undermines you more than having no reference at all. Choose people who will actually pick up the phone.

**Recruiting Platforms and Profiles**

Online recruiting platforms host your film and details in a format coaches recognise. Some are free, some charge subscriptions.

Before you commit to a paid platform, check:
• Whether coaches at your target level actually use it
• Whether the free tier does what you need
• Whether you retain ownership of your film and can export it
• Whether you can cancel without penalty

An unlisted video link, a one-page PDF profile, and a well-written email will get you just as far as a paid platform if the content is strong.

**Your Social Media Footprint**

Assume every coach searches your name. Many programs formally review recruits' accounts.

What to do:
• Clean up anything involving alcohol, drugs, violence, or abusive language
• Remove or make private anything that mocks opponents, coaches, or officials
• Use a consistent handle and a clear profile photo of you in uniform
• Post training, results, academics, and community involvement
• Keep your position, graduation year, and email in your bio

One post can cost you an offer. No post has ever won you one on its own.

**Protecting Yourself Online** *(callout)*

Never post your home address, school timetable, or travel plans publicly. Keep an adult copied on any account used for recruiting contact.

**Recruiting Timeline by Australian School Year**

There's no single correct age to start, but there is a realistic window, and Australians frequently misjudge it in both directions.

Roughly, by Australian school stage:
• Year 9–10: too early for direct coach contact in almost all cases. This is a building phase: get into good training habits, start keeping film, and get familiar with the pathway and terminology.
• Year 10–11: start building your recruiting profile in earnest: consistent film, verified measurables, a one-page summary. Light research contact with programs is reasonable; don't expect responses yet.
• Year 11–12: the active recruiting window for most Australians. Profile should be complete and current, direct coach outreach underway, and camps/combines become worth considering.
• Post-Year 12: still very viable, especially via JUCO, prep-year, or walk-on routes. Being a year or two 'behind' the typical U.S. recruiting class is normal for international athletes and not a disadvantage on its own.

Recruiting too early looks like: contacting coaches with no film, no measurables, and no real profile: this uses up a first impression before you have anything to show. Recruiting too late looks like: waiting until the final months of Year 12 to start building a profile, when most rosters for the following year are already largely set.

The single most reliable marker of correct timing isn't your age. It's whether Module 3's profile-building work is actually done. If it is, you're not too early. If it isn't, you're not ready yet, whatever year you're in.

**Camps, Combines, and Showcases**

These events fall into two very different categories, and mixing them up costs families real money.

School- or program-run camps: held directly by a college's own coaching staff, on their campus. These carry real weight: the coaches evaluating you are the ones who might actually offer you. Attending one is one of the strongest ways to get seen by a specific program you're genuinely interested in.

Independent combines and showcases: run by third-party companies, not by any single college. Some are genuinely well-attended by real recruiters and produce credible, independently verified testing numbers. Many are attended by few or no college coaches at all, despite the marketing.

How to tell which is which before you pay or travel:
• Ask directly which specific colleges and coaches attended last year, by name, not 'top programs nationwide'
• Check whether the numbers produced are independently timed and verified, or self-reported
• Search for verifiable outcomes: athletes who attended and can confirm what actually happened afterward
• Be sceptical of any event that guarantees exposure, evaluation, or a scholarship outcome. That's the same red flag as Module 5's recruiting-scam warning signs, just wearing a football uniform

For an Australian family, the cost of international travel raises the stakes considerably. A single college's own camp, attended because a specific coach has shown real interest, is usually a better use of a travel budget than a large generic combine with an uncertain coach turnout.

**Keeping Your Profile Current**

A stale profile reads as an athlete who stopped trying.

Set a monthly routine:
• Update measurables whenever they are re-tested
• Add new film within a week of the game
• Refresh your academic results each semester
• Recheck that every link opens on a phone, in a browser, without a login
• Re-send an updated one-line note to coaches you have already contacted

That last step is one of the highest-value habits in this entire handbook. A short 'here is my updated film and new 40 time' email gives you a legitimate reason to appear in a coach's inbox again.

**Your Student Recruiting Checklist**

Come back to this list throughout your recruiting process. It's meant to be reviewed, not completed once and filed away.

Academic
□ Current transcript, Years 10–12, up to date
□ ATAR or state-equivalent result or trajectory
□ Standardised test scores, if applicable

Athletic profile
□ Verified measurables, tested in the last few months
□ Current highlight film (3–5 minutes)
□ At least two full games available on request
□ Athletic résumé pulling the above into one document

References
□ Coach reference lined up and briefed
□ Teacher reference lined up, if relevant
□ Both told they may be contacted directly

Presence and research
□ Social media reviewed and cleaned up
□ Target schools researched: pathway type, division, coaching staff, scheme fit
□ Staff contact details pulled from official directories

Outreach

### Module 4 — Communicate With Coaches

Most Australian athletes lose opportunities not because of talent but because of how they communicate. This module is the full handbook on reaching, answering, and building relationships with U.S. coaches.

**Researching a Program Before You Reach Out**

A generic email announces itself as generic. Ten minutes of research is the difference between an email that gets read and one that gets deleted.

Before you write anything, find out:
• What scheme they run on offense and defense, and whether your skill set and position actually fit it
• Who's currently on the roster at your position, and what year they're in. A program with three senior offensive tackles has more immediate need than one that just graduated its whole line
• The coaching staff's background: where they've coached before, whether they've recruited internationally or had Australian players before
• Recent results and any staff changes, since a new coaching staff usually means recruiting priorities are actively shifting

Where to find this: the program's official athletics website (roster, staff bios), recent local sports media coverage, and the team's own social media for recent results and depth chart hints.

You don't need to become an expert on the program. You need enough to write one accurate, specific sentence about why you're contacting this program instead of a copy-pasted one.

**Who to Contact at a Program**

Sending everything to the head coach is the most common mistake. Head coaches at larger programs rarely read unsolicited recruiting email.

Who actually reads it:
• Recruiting coordinator: usually the correct first contact
• Position coach for your specific position: the highest-value contact
• Director of player personnel, at larger programs
• Special teams coordinator, if you are a kicker or punter

At small programs the head coach may genuinely handle recruiting themselves. Check the staff directory before assuming.

**Finding Accurate Contact Details**

Every program publishes a staff directory on its official athletics website. That is your source of truth.

Process:
1. Go to the official athletics site, not a third-party list
2. Open the football staff directory
3. Record name, exact title, and published email address
4. Note the correct spelling of the name: get it wrong and you look careless
5. Recheck details each season, because staff turnover is constant

Never pay for a 'coach contact database'. This information is free and public, and paid lists go out of date faster than the official directories.

**Writing Your First Email**

One short email, properly built, outperforms ten long ones.

Structure:
1. Greeting using the coach's correct title and surname
2. One sentence: who you are, position, graduation year, location
3. One sentence: your key measurables
4. One sentence: why their program specifically
5. Your film link, on its own line
6. Your academic snapshot in one line
7. A clear closing line and full signature

Keep it under 200 words. A coach should be able to read it on a phone without scrolling twice.

**Subject Lines That Get Opened**

The subject line decides whether the email is opened at all. Make it a summary, not a hook.

Work:
• '2027 OL | 6'5" 285lb | Melbourne, Australia | Film'
• 'Australian P | 4.2s hang | 2026 grad | Highlight link'

Do not work:
• 'Hello Coach'
• 'Please read this'
• 'Future star ready to commit'

Include position, graduation year, and the fact you are Australian. Being international is a genuine differentiator. Lead with it rather than hiding it.

**What to Include Every Time**

Every message, first or fiftieth, should contain enough for a coach to act without replying to ask for basics.

The non-negotiables:
• Full name
• Position
• Graduation year
• Height and weight
• Key measurables
• Working film link
• Academic summary
• Email and phone with country code
• Your Australian coach's name and contact, with their permission

Put them in the same order every time. Coaches skim, and predictable formatting makes you easy to process.

**What to Leave Out**

Leave out anything a coach cannot verify or act on.

Cut:
• Long life stories and childhood background
• Claims about your character: show it, do not assert it
• Attachments; use links instead, since attachments trip spam filters
• Any mention of other schools' interest unless it is factual and asked for
• Emotional appeals or pressure about deadlines
• Excessive punctuation, emoji, and all-caps

If a line does not help the coach evaluate you, delete it.

**Sharing Film the Right Way**

Film that a coach can't open in ten seconds might as well not exist.

Rules that actually matter:
• Use an unlisted link (YouTube unlisted, Hudl, or similar), never a link that requires a login the coach doesn't already have
• Test the link yourself, on a phone, in a private/incognito browser, before you send it
• Put the link on its own line in the email, not buried inside a sentence
• Label the file or video title with your name, position, and graduation year, so it's identifiable if downloaded or saved
• Keep a backup copy and the same link stable: don't take down or move film that coaches may already have bookmarked

What to avoid: password-protected files, links inside attachments, film hosted somewhere that requires an account to be created, or a link that expires. Every one of these gets your email deleted by a coach with hundreds of others to get through.

**Personalising Without Flattery**

Generic mass email is obvious and gets deleted. Empty flattery is equally obvious.

Good personalisation is specific and factual: a scheme they run that suits your skill set, their academic program in your intended field, a recent result, or a graduate at your position.

One accurate sentence is enough. 'I noticed you develop specialists and have signed international players before' beats a paragraph about their proud tradition of excellence.

**Timing and Time Zones**

Australia is 14 to 19 hours ahead of the U.S. mainland depending on the zone and daylight saving. Send email so it lands during their working morning, midweek.

Practical guidance:
• Aim for Tuesday to Thursday, U.S. morning
• Avoid game days and the day after
• Avoid the busiest period of their season for first contact
• For calls, propose two or three specific times in their local time zone, and state the zone

Always write times in the coach's time zone, not yours. Making them do the conversion is a small friction that costs replies.

**How and When to Follow Up**

Persistence works. Pestering does not. The difference is whether each message carries new information.

A workable rhythm:
• First follow-up about two weeks after initial contact
• Then roughly monthly, each time with something new: updated film, a new test number, a semester result, an upcoming trip
• Reference your previous email briefly so they have context
• Keep every follow-up shorter than the one before

Never send the identical message twice, and never send three in a week.

**When You Get No Reply**

Silence is the normal outcome, not a personal judgement. Coaches receive enormous volumes of email and can only reply to a fraction.

What to do:
• Assume it was not read, not that you were rejected
• Try a different staff member at the same program
• Improve the weakest part of your package (usually the film) and try again
• Widen your list; contact many more programs than feels necessary
• Keep going for at least two full cycles before concluding a program is not interested

Do not send an email asking why they have not replied. It never helps.

**Handling an Explicit Rejection**

A clear 'no' from a coach is more useful than silence, even though it doesn't feel that way. Respond to it well, because recruiting classes and staff both change.

What to do when a coach tells you no:
• Reply within a day or two: don't ghost, and don't argue with the decision
• Thank them for their time and the clear answer
• Ask, briefly, if it's appropriate to stay in touch in case your profile changes or their needs shift
• Move your attention to the next program on your list rather than dwelling on this one

What not to do: don't ask them to reconsider on the spot, don't take it personally in your reply, and don't burn the relationship: coaching staffs move between programs, and a professional response now can matter again years later in a different context.

The reverse situation matters just as much: at some point you may need to decline an opportunity or a roster spot yourself, whether because you've committed elsewhere or the fit isn't right.

Do it well:
• Respond promptly once you've decided: don't leave a coach hanging while you wait on other options
• Thank them specifically for their time and the opportunity
• State your decision clearly, without over-explaining or leaving room for back-and-forth
• Never criticise the program, coach, or school in your message, even if your experience with them was frustrating
• Don't just stop replying: disappearing is worse for your reputation than a direct decline
• If a recruiter or representative was involved, make sure they're informed at the same time, not after
• Confirm, in writing, any formal withdrawal or release steps the school requires, if you'd taken a step like signing an interest form

A rejection is information about fit at this moment, not a verdict on your ability. And how you decline is often remembered longer than how you were recruited. Coaching networks are small, and a gracious decline costs you nothing later.

**Phone and Video Calls**

A call is a big step forward. Prepare for it like a job interview.

Before:
• Confirm the time in both zones in writing
• Test your connection, camera, and audio
• Sit somewhere quiet, well lit, with a plain background
• Have your questions written down

During:
• Wear a collared shirt or team kit
• Speak slightly slower than feels natural
• Answer the question asked, then stop talking
• Have a parent or guardian nearby, especially if you are under 18

After: send a short thank-you email the same day, restating anything you agreed.

**Questions You Should Ask Coaches**

Asking good questions marks you as serious and protects you from bad fits.

Worth asking:
1. Where do you see me on the depth chart in year one?
2. What exactly does the scholarship or offer cover, and for how long?
3. How is it renewed, and under what circumstances could it be reduced?
4. What academic support exists for international student-athletes?
5. How many international players are currently on the roster?
6. What does a typical week look like in and out of season?
7. What happens to my support if I am injured?
8. What is your expected timeline for a decision?

Write the answers down during the call.

Understanding what a coach's answer actually means matters as much as asking the question. Coaches are often positive without making a formal commitment, and the gap between encouragement and commitment is where Australian families most often get their hopes ahead of reality.

What different phrases usually mean:
• 'We're interested': you're on their radar, nothing more
• 'We're evaluating you': active consideration, still no decision
• 'Stay in touch': keep them updated, but treat it as open rather than promising
• 'You're on our board': you're being tracked internally, not offered
• 'We want you to apply': this is about admission, not a roster guarantee
• 'We have a roster opportunity': likely a walk-on conversation, confirm which
• 'We have a scholarship offer': this is the real thing, but still ask for it in writing
• 'This is contingent on admission or eligibility': the offer is conditional; find out exactly on what

If a phrase is ambiguous, ask directly: 'Just to be clear, is this a scholarship offer, or are we still in the evaluation stage?' A coach who values you will answer that plainly.

**Questions They Will Ask You**

Have honest, prepared answers ready.

Expect:
• What are your current measurables and when were they last tested?
• What is your graduation year and academic standing?
• Have you taken any standardised tests?
• Why do you want to play in the United States?
• What other programs are you speaking with?
• Who coaches you, and can we contact them?
• Are you willing to redshirt or take a developmental year?
• How is your family placed to support this financially?

Never exaggerate a number. Everything you say can be checked, and often is.

**Tone, Slang, and Australian English**

Write in plain, direct English. Australian slang, abbreviations, and understated humour do not travel well in a recruiting inbox.

Adjustments worth making:
• Give both metric and imperial measurements
• Spell out Australian competitions and levels rather than assuming knowledge
• Avoid 'reckon', 'heaps', 'keen as', 'arvo', and similar
• Skip self-deprecation: it reads as a lack of confidence, not modesty
• Use full sentences and proper punctuation, never text abbreviations

Be respectful and direct. 'Coach' as a form of address is standard and appropriate.

**Texting and Direct Messages**

Once a coach gives you their number or replies on a messaging platform, different norms apply.

Guidelines:
• Only text if invited to; email first, always
• Keep messages brief and during their waking hours
• Stay professional: no memes, no voice notes unless asked
• Do not double-message if there is no reply
• Social media DMs are acceptable at smaller programs but treat them as a supplement to email, never a replacement

Keep the same identity everywhere: same name, same photo, same position.

**Tracking Every Conversation**

By month three you will not remember who you contacted or what you said. Build a simple spreadsheet from day one.

Columns to keep:
• School and division
• Coach name, title, email
• Date of first contact
• Date and summary of every follow-up
• Their reply and any commitments made
• Next scheduled action and date
• Status: no response, interested, evaluating, offered, closed

Review it weekly. This one document is the difference between a campaign and a scattering of hopeful emails.

**Keep a Parent in the Loop** *(callout)*

Copy a parent or guardian on coach correspondence, especially anything involving money, travel, or documents.

**Common Mistakes to Avoid**

The errors that cost Australian athletes the most opportunities:

• Mass emails with the wrong school or coach name left in
• Film links that require a login or do not work on mobile
• Missing graduation year, so the coach cannot slot you into a class
• Inflated measurables that collapse at a camp
• Contacting only Division I programs
• Giving up after one round of silence
• Letting a parent or agent write and send everything for you
• Never following up after a positive reply

Every one of these is preventable with a checklist and ten extra minutes.

**Key Takeaways**

Clear, short, verifiable, and consistent communication beats volume. Contact the right staff member, lead with position and measurables, follow up with new information rather than repetition, and track every conversation in one place.

### Module 5 — Evaluate Recruiters and Protect Yourself

There is a paid industry around this pathway. Some of it is genuinely useful, some of it is worthless, and some of it is predatory. This module teaches you to tell the difference.

**The Recruiting Services Landscape**

The businesses operating around Australian athletes fall into a few broad types:

• Profile and film platforms that host your material
• Combine and testing operators who produce verified numbers
• Training academies focused on football-specific skills
• Placement or 'agency' services that claim to connect you with programs
• Individual consultants and former players offering advice

Each can be legitimate. The risk rises sharply as you move down the list, because placement outcomes are the hardest thing to verify and the easiest thing to promise.

**What Legitimate Services Actually Provide**

A service worth paying for delivers something concrete that you cannot easily do yourself.

Genuine value looks like:
• Professionally measured, independently verified testing numbers
• High-quality filming and editing
• Specific football coaching from credentialed coaches
• Administrative help with transcripts, testing, and eligibility paperwork
• Introductions that the service can name and evidence

Notice that none of these are outcomes. A good service sells you work, materials, or expertise, never a result.

**Red Flags and Warning Signs**

Walk away if you encounter any of these:

• A guaranteed scholarship, offer, or named school
• Large up-front fees before any service is delivered
• Pressure to sign immediately or lose a 'limited spot'
• Refusal to provide a written contract
• No verifiable list of past athletes you can contact directly
• Vague claims like 'connections with hundreds of colleges'
• Asking you to keep the arrangement from your parents or school coach
• Requesting exclusive rights to represent you across all programs
• Taking a percentage of a future scholarship

One red flag is enough to stop. You do not need to be certain it is a scam to decline.

**Conflicts of Interest and Who's Really Paying Whom**

A conflict of interest exists when a person or service advising you has a financial relationship with the school or program they're steering you toward, while presenting themselves as working in your interest alone.

This can look like:
• A 'recruiting consultant' who receives a fee or commission from a school for every athlete they refer
• A camp or combine operator who is paid to promote specific programs to attendees
• An 'independent' rating or ranking service that is actually funded by certain schools or conferences

Why it matters: advice that's meant to be about fit for you can quietly become advice about who's paying the advisor. You can't always see this relationship from the outside. It's rarely advertised.

Ask directly: 'Do you receive any payment, commission, or benefit from any school or program you might recommend to me?' A legitimate advisor answers plainly. Reluctance to answer, or a vague non-answer, is itself the red flag. Treat it the same way you'd treat any other item on this module's red-flag list.

**Fake Coaches, Fake Schools, and Fake Recruiting Organizations**

Impersonation is a real and growing risk in recruiting, because so much of the process happens over email and social media with no in-person verification.

How to verify a coach is who they claim to be:
• Cross-check their name and title against the program's official staff directory on the athletics website, not a link they sent you
• Confirm their email address matches the school's actual domain, not a generic email provider
• If contacted by phone or social media first, ask for a follow-up email from that same official domain before sharing anything personal

How to verify a school or organization is real:
• Search for the institution independently: official accreditation, an actual physical campus, a real athletics department
• Be wary of organizations that only exist on social media, with no verifiable institutional presence
• A legitimate school or program never asks you to keep the relationship secret from your parents or coach

If something feels off (a rushed timeline, pressure to respond immediately, requests for personal information before any real conversation has happened), stop and verify independently before replying further. Confirming a coach's or school's identity takes minutes; recovering from impersonation can take much longer.

**Questions to Ask Before You Pay Anything**

Ask these in writing and keep the answers:

1. Exactly what services do I receive, and by when?
2. What is the total cost, including anything not in the headline price?
3. What is your refund policy if the service is not delivered?
4. Can you provide three past athletes I may contact independently?
5. Which specific programs have you placed athletes with in the last two years?
6. Who owns my film and profile data?
7. Are you affiliated with, or paid by, any college program?
8. What happens if I stop using your service midway?

A legitimate operator answers all eight without hesitation.

**Reading Contracts and Fee Structures**

Never sign anything without an adult reading it, and never sign on the spot.

Check specifically for:
• Total fees, payment schedule, and any automatic renewal
• Exclusivity clauses preventing you from talking to coaches yourself
• Any claim on future earnings, scholarship value, or NIL income
• Termination terms and notice periods
• Which country's law governs the agreement and where disputes are heard
• Ownership and licensing of your film, photos, and personal data

If you are under 18, a parent or guardian must be party to the agreement. Take a copy away and review it over at least 48 hours.

**Verifying Claims and Track Records**

Claims are easy to make and easy to check.

How to verify:
• Search named athletes on official college roster pages: if they are not listed, the placement did not happen
• Contact past clients yourself, using details you find, not details the service supplies
• Check the business is registered and how long it has traded
• Look for reviews and complaints outside the service's own website
• Ask your school coach and your state gridiron body whether they know the operator
• Confirm any claimed college affiliation directly with the college

Do the verification before you pay, not after.

**No One Can Guarantee a Scholarship** *(callout)*

Any service promising a guaranteed offer, roster spot, or specific school in exchange for a fee is misrepresenting how recruiting works.

**Your Data and Your Film Rights**

Your film, testing data, transcripts, and personal details have commercial value to these businesses.

Protect them:
• Keep original, unedited copies of all your film yourself
• Do not grant exclusive or perpetual licences over your footage
• Confirm you can export your profile and film if you leave the service
• Never hand over passport scans, bank details, or full identity documents without a clearly stated, necessary reason
• Ask how your data is stored and whether it is sold or shared

If a service becomes the only holder of your material, you have lost your leverage.

**Protecting Your Identity, Passport, and Transcripts**

Your passport, transcripts, and other identity documents are exactly the kind of material identity thieves and scammers want, and recruiting is a context where people can plausibly ask for them.

Protecting yourself:
• Never send a full passport scan, driver's licence, or other government ID unless you know exactly why it's needed and who's asking. Official school admissions offices sometimes need this later in the process, but a recruiter or 'agent' almost never does at this stage
• Send transcripts and academic records only to verified institutional email addresses or official application portals, not to a personal or generic email
• Redact or withhold sensitive numbers (passport number, any national ID number) unless you've confirmed the request is genuinely necessary
• Keep your own secure digital copies, and know exactly who you've sent originals or scans to and when
• If a document is requested urgently, with pressure to send it immediately, treat the urgency itself as a warning sign: genuine institutional requests come with reasonable timeframes

If you ever suspect a document has been misused, tell a parent or trusted adult immediately and follow this module's reporting guidance.

**Safeguarding and Adult Involvement** *(callout)*

No adult in this process should ask you to keep conversations secret from your parents or coach. If that happens, stop and tell a trusted adult immediately.

**Travel and Camp Safety for Minors**

Travelling to a camp, combine, or visit as a minor carries real safety considerations that are easy to overlook in the excitement of the opportunity.

Before booking any travel:
• Confirm exactly who will be responsible for you at every stage: arrival, accommodation, transport to and from the event, and departure
• Get accommodation details in writing, including who else is staying there
• Share your full itinerary with a parent or guardian, including flight details, accommodation address, and event schedule
• Confirm emergency contact arrangements at the destination, not just at home

Parent involvement during travel: a parent or guardian should be directly involved in booking and confirming arrangements, not simply informed after the fact. If a service or individual is arranging travel on your behalf and discourages parent involvement in these logistics, treat that as a serious warning sign.

At the event itself: know who the event organisers are and how to reach them, keep a parent reachable at all times, and trust your instincts if an arrangement (accommodation, supervision, or a request from an adult) feels wrong. Leaving a situation that feels unsafe is always the right call, regardless of any cost already paid.

**Identifying Your Trusted Adults**

Before anything goes wrong is the right time to work this out, not in the middle of a situation that already feels uncomfortable.

Name, specifically, right now:
• The parent or guardian you'd go to first with any concern, big or small
• A coach or school staff member you trust, separate from your family, in case a concern involves recruiting specifically
• A backup adult, in case your first choice isn't reachable

Write these names down. Make sure each of them knows they're on this list, so they're not caught off guard if you come to them.

A trusted adult isn't just for emergencies. Use them for the ordinary, uncomfortable moments too: an email that feels off, a request you're not sure about, a conversation that made you uneasy even if you can't say exactly why. You don't need certainty that something is wrong to bring it to someone. That instinct, on its own, is reason enough.

**Doing It Yourself Without a Service**

Every essential step in this pathway can be done without paying an agency.

The self-directed version:
1. Film your own games from an elevated, side-on position
2. Edit a three-to-five-minute reel with free software
3. Get measurables taken by your school or club coach
4. Build a one-page PDF profile and an unlisted video link
5. Pull coach details from official staff directories
6. Email programs directly and track every contact
7. Handle transcripts and testing through your school

Australians have completed this route on their own many times. Paying for help is a convenience decision, not a requirement.

**Where to Report a Problem**

If something goes wrong, report it. It protects the next athlete as much as you.

Where to go:
• Your school, club, or state gridiron association first
• The Australian Competition and Consumer Commission for misleading conduct or scams
• Scamwatch for fraudulent operators
• Your state fair trading or consumer affairs body for contract disputes
• Police, if there is any suggestion of grooming, coercion, or exploitation of a minor
• The college directly, if a person claimed to represent them

Keep every email, contract, receipt, and message. Documentation is what makes a complaint actionable.

**How Rep1 Supports Your Journey**

A recruiting service or consultancy, including this one, sits somewhere in the landscape covered by this module. Whatever service you use, the same standards apply: clear scope, no guaranteed outcomes, verifiable claims, and full parent involvement.

What a legitimate recruiting support service should do:
• State clearly what's included, for what fee, and for how long
• Never guarantee a scholarship, offer, or specific school
• Be transparent about how it makes money, including any relationships with schools or programs (see Conflicts of Interest, above)
• Support your own outreach and profile-building rather than replacing your voice entirely
• Put every commitment in writing

**Key Takeaways**

You can complete this pathway without paying anyone. If you do pay, pay for a defined service with a written scope, never for a promise. Verify every claim independently, keep an adult involved in every agreement, and treat guaranteed outcomes as proof the operator is not credible.

## Section 2: Academics, Eligibility, Offers, and Enrollment

### Module 6 — Plan Your Academic Path From Australia

What U.S. schools and eligibility centers need from your Australian academic record, and how to make sure it arrives complete, consistent, and on time.

**How Your Australian School Stage Maps to the U.S. Process**

U.S. institutions think in terms of grades nine through twelve and a single graduation date. Your Australian school record needs to translate cleanly into that frame.

Roughly, the mapping is:
• Year 9 and Year 10: equivalent to U.S. freshman and sophomore year. This is early for anything admissions-related, but it's when your subject choices start affecting later options.
• Year 11: equivalent to U.S. junior year. This is when U.S. schools expect to start seeing a real academic record and predicted results.
• Year 12: equivalent to U.S. senior year. Your final results and graduation evidence come from this year.
• Recently graduated: you've finished Year 12 but haven't started a U.S. program yet. This is common for Australians because of the calendar gap covered in Module 2, and it doesn't disadvantage you academically.
• Gap year or school leaver: you've taken time between finishing school and pursuing this pathway. Be ready to explain what you did with that time. Continued training and study both count as reasonable answers.

Whichever stage you're at, admissions offices want a graduation date, a final or predicted result, and a way to interpret your grading scale. The next sections cover exactly how to give them that.

**Building Your Academic Record Package**

Your academic record package is the set of documents that lets a U.S. institution and, separately, an eligibility center understand what you've actually achieved. Both need slightly different things, so build the full set once and use it everywhere.

What the package should include:
• Official, subject-by-subject transcripts covering Years 10 to 12, not just a final summary
• Predicted results if you haven't finished Year 12 yet, and final results once you have
• Your ATAR or state equivalent (or the calculation method, if your state doesn't issue an ATAR)
• Graduation evidence: a certificate or official letter confirming you completed Year 12
• A school profile: a short document, often prepared by your school, explaining its grading scale, curriculum, and any subject weighting, so an American reader can interpret your results correctly
• A counselor or teacher report where the school or program requests one

Ask your school's registrar or front office for these as a set early, not one at a time as each is requested. Schools can take weeks to produce certified copies, and that delay is one of the most common causes of missed deadlines.

**Choosing Subjects That Support Eligibility**

Subject choice matters more for this pathway than it does for a typical Australian university application, because NCAA Division I and II eligibility is partly built around a specific list of academic subjects called core courses.

What this means in practice: NCAA eligibility counts a defined number of years of English, mathematics, natural or physical science, social science, and additional academic subjects. Your school's specific subjects have to be matched against the NCAA's approved list for your school, which the NCAA Eligibility Center manages through your school's own core-course submission, not something you calculate yourself.

What you can control now: keep a broad, consistent academic spread across Years 10 to 12 rather than narrowing early. English every year, ongoing mathematics, and at least one science are the safest foundation regardless of which pathway you end up choosing, since NAIA and NJCAA lean more on overall GPA and graduation than a specific approved-course list, and a strong general record satisfies all three.

Don't assume a subject qualifies just because it sounds academic. Confirm with your school's NCAA Eligibility Center liaison, or directly with the Eligibility Center, once your school has an approved course list on file.

**Standardised Testing: What You May Need**

Standardised testing requirements are one of the most volatile parts of this whole process right now, so treat anything below as a starting point for your own confirmation, not a fixed rule.

What's generally true: the SAT, administered by the College Board, and the ACT are the two main standardised tests U.S. institutions use. Many universities have moved to test-optional admissions policies in recent years, meaning a strong application can be considered without a score, but policies vary school by school and can differ from what an athletics program or eligibility center separately requires.

What to do:
• Check the specific university's current testing policy directly on its admissions website, not through a third party
• Check separately with the relevant eligibility center (NCAA, NAIA, or NJCAA) about whether a test score is currently part of academic certification for your target division
• If you do test, register early. International test dates and centres are more limited in Australia than in the U.S.
• Keep score reports on file even if a school says they're optional. Requirements can change during your recruiting window.

**Credential Evaluation and Certified Translations**

Credential evaluation is the formal process of converting your Australian academic record into terms a U.S. institution or eligibility center can directly compare against its own standards. Whether you need one depends entirely on who's asking.

When it commonly comes up:
• Some universities request a third-party credential evaluation as part of international admissions, particularly for grading-scale conversion
• The NAIA Eligibility Center uses a specific credential evaluation partner for international students as part of its own registration process
• The NCAA Eligibility Center generally works from your school's own submitted records rather than requiring a separate paid evaluation, but individual institutions may still ask for one as part of admissions

Certified translations are a related but separate requirement: they're only relevant if any of your documents aren't in English, which is uncommon for Australian records but can apply to supporting documents from elsewhere.

Before paying for any evaluation service, confirm with the specific school or eligibility center that it's actually required, which service they accept, and what it costs. Don't assume one evaluation satisfies every destination; some accept only specific, approved providers.

**Sending Records and Keeping Your Details Consistent**

How you send your records matters almost as much as what's in them, and small inconsistencies cause real delays.

Sending records:
• Ask your school's registrar to send official transcripts directly to the receiving institution or eligibility center, electronically where supported, or as a sealed certified copy
• Keep your own certified copies as well, since you'll likely need to send the same records to more than one place
• Track exactly what was sent, where, and when, the same way you'd track a coach contact

Keeping details consistent:
• Your name should appear identically, in the same order, across your passport, transcripts, eligibility center account, and any test registrations
• Your date of birth should be formatted and recorded the same way everywhere
• Minor spelling or order differences between documents are a common, avoidable reason a file gets flagged or delayed for manual review

If you've ever gone by a different name, spelling, or order on an older document, sort that out with your school before it becomes a problem in someone else's process.

**Questions to Ask the School or Admissions Office**

Ask these directly, in writing, and keep the answers:

1. What documents do you require from an Australian Year 12 record, specifically?
2. Do you require a credential evaluation, and if so, from which providers?
3. Is standardised testing required, optional, or not considered for international applicants this cycle?
4. What is your application deadline for the term I'm targeting, and does that differ for international students?
5. Who should I contact if my records are delayed by my school?
6. How will my results be interpreted if my school doesn't issue an ATAR?
7. Is there a difference between what admissions requires and what athletics or the eligibility center requires?

Ask the admissions office and the athletics program these questions separately. Their answers don't always match, and knowing that early avoids the conflicting-instructions problem covered later in this section.

**Your Academic Document Checklist**

Track this alongside Module 3's recruiting checklist. This one covers your academic side specifically.

Records
□ Official subject-by-subject transcript, Years 10 to 12
□ Predicted results (if still at school) or final results (if graduated)
□ ATAR or state-equivalent result, or an explanation of your state's method
□ Graduation certificate or official completion letter
□ School profile explaining your grading scale and curriculum

Testing and evaluation
□ Confirmed whether SAT or ACT is required, optional, or not considered, for each school
□ Confirmed whether a credential evaluation is required, and by which provider
□ Certified translations arranged, if any document isn't in English

Consistency
□ Name spelling and order matches across passport, transcripts, and every account
□ Date of birth recorded identically everywhere

Sending
□ Official transcript request submitted to your school's registrar
□ Confirmed delivery method (electronic or sealed copy) for each destination
□ Personal certified copies kept on file

Questions
□ Admissions office questions asked and answered in writing
□ Any conflict between admissions and athletics requirements identified

If a box is unchecked with less than a month before a deadline, that's the next thing to action, not a reason to worry yet.

**Key Takeaways**

Your academic record is read by at least two separate offices, admissions and the eligibility center, and they don't always want the same thing. Build one complete, consistent document package early, confirm testing and evaluation requirements directly rather than assuming them, and keep your name and date of birth identical across every document and account.

### Module 7 — Understand Athletic Eligibility and Amateurism

Admission gets you into the school. Eligibility gets you onto the field. This module explains how the two are decided separately, and what could put your eligibility at risk before you even arrive.

**Admission Is Not Eligibility**

These are two separate approvals, decided by two separate offices, and mixing them up is the single most common misunderstanding in this whole process.

Admission is the university's own decision that you meet its academic standards and may enrol as a student. The admissions office makes this call, and it has nothing to do with sport.

Eligibility is a separate certification, made by the relevant governing body (NCAA, NAIA, or NJCAA), that your academic record and amateur status meet its rules to practise and compete.

You need both, and one does not guarantee the other. A student can be admitted and not eligible (common when academic certification is incomplete or amateurism status is unresolved), or in rarer cases be on track for eligibility but not admitted (the school's own academic bar can sit above the governing body's minimum). Confirm each one separately, with the office that actually owns it, and don't treat a confirmation from one as covering the other.

**The NCAA Eligibility Center and Certification**

The NCAA Eligibility Center is the body that certifies whether you're academically and athletically eligible to compete at a Division I or Division II school, or as an international student-athlete at a Division III school.

The basic process:
• Register at eligibilitycenter.org and choose the right account type. A Certification Account is for students planning to compete at Division I or II. A Profile Page account suits students still exploring options or heading to Division III.
• Your school submits your academic records through its own registrar portal.
• You complete an amateurism questionnaire covering your sports participation history.
• Once your records and amateurism responses are in, you request your final certification yourself. The Eligibility Center does not finalise it automatically, even if every other task is complete.

International students go through the same Eligibility Center but are assessed against country-specific academic standards guidance, and registration carries its own fee structure separate from domestic students.

Start this well before your final year of school. Certification can take months, and it is entirely independent of the admissions timeline covered in Module 6.

**NAIA Eligibility Certification**

The NAIA runs its own certification process, separate from the NCAA, through the NAIA Eligibility Center at PlayNAIA.org.

How it differs from the NCAA process:
• Eligibility is generally assessed through a simpler set of academic criteria: typically graduation with a qualifying GPA, or meeting a combination of GPA, standardised test score, and class rank benchmarks.
• International students complete a credential evaluation through the NAIA's designated partner service as part of registration, which is a required step, not optional.
• Your prospective coach usually needs to place you on the school's 'short list' for your file to be prioritised for review.

What to do: create your PlayNAIA profile, have your school send records through the NAIA's High School Portal, and ask your coach to add you to their short list once you're seriously talking with a program.

The NAIA is a genuinely realistic entry point for Australians and its process is often less complex than the NCAA's, but it is still a real certification, not a formality.

**NJCAA Eligibility Requirements**

The NJCAA governs two-year college athletics and has its own, generally more accessible, eligibility framework.

Core requirements typically include:
• Being a graduate of high school or holding an accepted equivalent
• Maintaining amateur status, which starts applying once you turn 19 or enrol full-time, whichever comes first
• Meeting continuing academic standards once enrolled, generally around a minimum GPA and credit-hour load each term

A feature specific to the NJCAA: if you sign an NJCAA Letter of Intent with a member college, you become unrecruitable by other NJCAA programs and must disclose that signing to anyone who contacts you afterward.

Because NJCAA eligibility rules are applied by each member college's own athletic staff working from the national rulebook, the most reliable answer to any specific eligibility question is the athletic department at the specific college you're considering, not a general summary.

**Core-Course Requirements Explained**

Core courses are the specific set of academic subjects the NCAA counts toward initial eligibility for Division I and II. This is distinct from your general Year 12 results.

The rough shape: a required number of years each of English, mathematics, natural or physical science, and social science, plus additional years from any of those areas or another approved academic subject. A commonly cited target is a total of sixteen core courses across your high school years.

The catch for Australians: the NCAA doesn't accept your subjects at face value. Your school has to submit its own list of courses to the NCAA, which reviews and approves which of your school's specific subjects count as core courses. This is a school-level process, not something you can calculate yourself from a subject name.

What to do: ask your school whether it already has an approved core-course list on file with the NCAA (many international schools don't, and it can take time to establish one). If it doesn't, this needs to start early, ideally by Year 10 or 11, not discovered in Year 12.

**Amateurism: What Can Put Your Eligibility at Risk**

Amateurism rules exist to keep you eligible for college sport by limiting certain kinds of payment and professional involvement before you enrol. For international athletes, this area causes more genuine problems than any other part of eligibility, because sport is organised differently outside the U.S.

Activities that can put amateurism status at risk include:
• Accepting payment or benefits for playing that exceed your actual and necessary expenses
• Signing with, or being represented by, an agent for the purpose of marketing your athletic ability
• Playing on a professional team, or in competitions where some competitors are paid, depending on the sport and specifics
• Accepting prize money above actual expenses in individual competitions

What generally does not put you at risk:
• Approved name, image, and likeness (NIL) activity, which is treated separately from amateurism under current rules
• Using an NCAA-compliant recruiting service to help find a program
• Normal school, club, or representative football participation in Australia, which is exactly the kind of history the Eligibility Center's amateurism questionnaire is built to review

Because other countries structure junior and semi-professional sport differently than the U.S. does, an activity that looks completely normal in an Australian pathway (a paid junior representative program, for instance) can raise a genuine question under NCAA rules that a domestic U.S. athlete would never encounter. Answer the amateurism questionnaire completely and honestly, and if anything in your sporting history is unusual, raise it with the Eligibility Center or a compliance office proactively rather than hoping it goes unnoticed.

**Gap Years, Delayed Enrollment, and Continued Competition**

Taking time between finishing school and enrolling in the U.S. is common for Australians because of the calendar mismatch covered in Module 2, and it does not automatically cost you eligibility. But it does need to be handled correctly, not just assumed to be fine.

What matters:
• The period between your expected graduation and your actual full-time college enrollment is generally treated as a defined 'grace period' by the NCAA, during which certain amateurism protections continue to apply
• Continuing to train and compete in Australia during a gap year is normal and expected, but the nature of that competition (paid or unpaid, representative level) still needs to be disclosed as part of your amateurism certification
• Delayed enrollment does not reset or extend your eligibility clock in your favour. Your window is still measured from your original expected enrollment timeline in most cases
• Continued competition after what would have been your graduating class's enrollment date is one of the specific situations the Eligibility Center reviews individually

What to do: keep your Eligibility Center account updated with accurate dates as your plans firm up, and don't assume a gap year is invisible to the certification process. It isn't. It's simply one of the standard situations the process is built to handle, as long as it's disclosed accurately.

**Redshirting and Eligibility Are Not the Same Thing**

These get confused constantly, and they're not the same axis at all.

Eligibility is a certification: has the governing body confirmed your academic record and amateur status meet its rules to practise and compete, at all. This is decided once, before you can do anything on the team.

Redshirting, covered in Module 2, is a participation decision made after you're already certified eligible: whether you compete in games during a given season, or train and study without playing while preserving a year of competition.

The relationship between them: you must be eligibility-certified before you can be on the roster at all, redshirt or not. Redshirting doesn't affect your eligibility certification, and it doesn't use up or protect eligibility in the certification sense; it only affects how many seasons of actual competition you have left within your total eligibility window.

If a coach talks about you 'redshirting' in your first year, that's a roster and development conversation. It has nothing to do with whether your academic and amateur status has been approved. That approval has to happen regardless.

**Common Eligibility Mistakes**

The mistakes below cost Australian athletes real time, almost always because of when something was started, not what was done.

• Registering with an eligibility center in Year 12, when the process realistically needs to start a year or more earlier
• Assuming Australian subjects automatically count as NCAA core courses without the school's approved list actually being on file
• Filling in the amateurism questionnaire vaguely or incompletely, which slows down certification rather than avoiding scrutiny
• Never actually requesting final certification, assuming it happens automatically once other tasks are done
• Not realising NAIA, NCAA, and NJCAA are three separate systems, and registering for only one when your options span more than one pathway
• Treating a gap year as something to hide rather than something to accurately disclose
• Waiting for a coach or recruiter to handle eligibility registration, instead of owning it yourself

Every one of these is a timing or communication problem, not an ability problem. They're avoidable with a calendar and a checklist, which is exactly what the rest of this module gives you.

**Questions for a Compliance Office**

An athletic compliance office is the university staff responsible for making sure the athletic program follows governing-body rules, including your eligibility status. Once you're talking seriously with a program, these are worth asking directly:

1. Has my eligibility certification actually been received and reviewed by your office, not just submitted to the Eligibility Center?
2. Is there anything in my academic record or amateurism history that needs further documentation?
3. What is the current status of my core-course approval, if applicable?
4. Are there any pending items on my file that could delay my certification?
5. Who is my specific point of contact if a question comes up after I arrive?
6. If I take a gap year or delay enrollment, who do I need to notify and when?

Asking these questions doesn't signal a problem. It signals that you understand the process is real and separate from recruiting, which compliance staff generally respect.

**Your Eligibility Document Checklist**

Keep this alongside Module 6's academic checklist and Module 3's recruiting checklist. This one is specifically about certification.

Registration
□ Correct eligibility center account created (NCAA, NAIA, and/or NJCAA, depending on your target pathway)
□ Registration fee paid or fee waiver confirmed, where applicable

Academic certification
□ School's core-course list confirmed as filed, if pursuing NCAA Division I or II
□ Transcripts submitted directly by your school's registrar to the relevant eligibility center
□ Graduation date and enrollment date entered accurately

Amateurism
□ Amateurism questionnaire completed fully and honestly
□ Any unusual competition history (paid representative programs, prize money, prior professional-adjacent participation) disclosed proactively

Final steps
□ Final certification actually requested, not just assumed
□ Confirmation received from the specific college's compliance office, not only the eligibility center

Ongoing
□ Any change in plans (gap year, delayed enrollment, change of target division) reported and re-certified

Eligibility certification and school admission are tracked on two separate lists. Don't let progress on one make you assume the other is handled.

**Key Takeaways**

Admission and eligibility are decided by two completely separate offices, and neither one implies the other. Start eligibility center registration well before your final year, disclose your full sporting history honestly on the amateurism questionnaire rather than hoping something goes unnoticed, and remember that redshirting is a roster decision that happens after eligibility certification, not a substitute for it.

### Module 8 — Compare Scholarships, Offers, and Total Costs

A scholarship offer is a starting point for a negotiation and a budget conversation, not a finished deal. This module shows what an offer actually includes, what it doesn't, and how to compare two offers honestly.

**From Interest to a Real Offer**

Recruiting language moves through several distinct stages, and treating any of them as more solid than they are is how families end up disappointed.

Recruiting interest: a coach has noticed you and is watching. Nothing has been offered.

A roster opportunity: a coach has indicated a spot on the team, but without athletic financial aid attached. This is a walk-on conversation, not a scholarship offer, however it's phrased.

A verbal offer: a coach has told you, out loud or informally in writing, that they intend to offer you athletic aid. It is a strong signal of intent, but it is not a binding commitment from the institution, and it can change if circumstances at the program change.

A written athletic-aid offer: a formal, binding document from the institution itself, not just the coach, setting out the aid you'll receive. Exactly what this document is has changed recently, and it's covered in full in Module 9.

Treat every stage before the written, institution-issued offer as provisional. A verbal offer from a coach is a real and meaningful signal, but it is not the same category of commitment as a signed, written agreement from the school.

**Types of Financial Support Explained**

Four different kinds of money can make up a package, and they come from different sources with different rules.

Athletic scholarship: aid tied to your participation in the sport, awarded by the athletic department, generally on a year-to-year basis.

Academic scholarship: merit-based aid tied to your academic record, awarded independently of athletics. This can stack on top of an athletic scholarship at many schools, and is worth pursuing on its own merits.

Institutional grants: money the university itself provides, which can be merit-based, need-based, or a mix, set entirely by that specific school's own policy.

Loans: borrowed money that must be repaid, usually with interest, regardless of whether you finish your degree or your sporting career. Treat any loan as debt, not aid, when comparing offers.

An important distinction for Australian families specifically: U.S. federal need-based aid, the kind administered through FAFSA, is generally not available to international students. Some individual universities do offer their own need-based institutional aid to international students, but this is entirely a per-school policy, not a guarantee, and needs to be confirmed with that school's financial aid office directly rather than assumed from how need-based aid works for U.S. citizens.

**How Scholarships Are Renewed (and Lost)**

Athletic scholarships are typically awarded one year at a time, not guaranteed for four years, and several things can affect whether yours is renewed.

Factors that can affect renewal:
• Athletic performance and roster fit, within the limits of what NCAA rules allow a program to consider
• Injury, though some conferences and schools have specific medical-scholarship protections. Whether one applies to you is a question for that school's compliance office, not something to assume
• A change of head coach, which can sometimes affect how existing scholarship commitments are honoured in practice, even where they remain contractually binding
• Conduct and academic standing, same as any student

What to actually ask before accepting: is this scholarship renewable each year, under what specific conditions could it be reduced or not renewed, and is there anything in writing that protects you in the event of injury or a coaching change. Get the answer in writing, not as a verbal reassurance.

**The Full Cost of Attendance**

Cost of attendance (COA) is the official, complete figure a university's financial aid office calculates for what it actually costs to attend for a year. It's a wider number than most families expect, and it's the number to compare against, not the headline scholarship amount.

COA typically includes:
• Tuition and fees
• Room and board
• Books and required course materials
• Personal expenses and transportation
• In some cases, an additional stipend some conferences now permit on top of traditional scholarship coverage

A 'full' athletic scholarship historically covered tuition, fees, room, board, and books, which can still be meaningfully less than the full COA figure once personal expenses and travel are included. Ask the financial aid office for the actual published cost of attendance for your specific circumstances (in-state, out-of-state, and international status can all change this figure), then compare your offer against that number, not against the word 'full'.

**Comparing Offers Side by Side**

Build one simple table before comparing anything else. For every school, record:

• Full published cost of attendance for your circumstances
• What your athletic aid actually covers, itemised (tuition, fees, room, board, books, other)
• Any academic or institutional aid stacked on top
• The gap: cost of attendance minus total aid, which is what your family actually pays
• Renewal terms and conditions
• Travel cost home, since this varies enormously and is rarely included in any offer

The school with the biggest scholarship number isn't automatically the cheapest option once you've filled in this table. A smaller athletic scholarship at a lower cost-of-attendance school can leave your family paying less overall than a bigger-sounding award somewhere more expensive.

**Questions to Ask Before You Accept**

Ask these, get the answers in writing, and don't accept based on a verbal answer alone:

1. Exactly what does this offer cover, itemised against the full cost of attendance?
2. Is it renewable each year, and under what specific conditions could it be reduced or not renewed?
3. What happens to my aid if I'm injured?
4. What happens to my aid if the head coach leaves or is replaced?
5. Is there any need-based or academic aid available on top of this, and have I applied for it?
6. What is the actual signing document, and what does it legally bind me to?
7. What happens if my eligibility certification isn't finalised by the time I'm meant to enrol?
8. Who do I contact if the answers I get from the coach and the answers I get from financial aid or compliance don't match?

**Red Flags When Reviewing an Offer** *(callout)*

Be cautious of: pressure to accept immediately with no time to review anything in writing, a coach who won't put scholarship terms in writing, vague answers about renewal, or being told a document is 'just a formality' when you're asked to sign it.

**Bring a Parent or Guardian Into Every Offer Conversation** *(callout)*

Every offer conversation should include a parent or guardian, especially the numbers. A financial commitment this size deserves the same scrutiny as any major household decision.

**Key Takeaways**

A scholarship offer is not final until it's written and signed by the institution, and even then it's typically renewed year to year rather than guaranteed for four. Compare offers against the full published cost of attendance, not the headline scholarship figure, and get renewal, injury, and coaching-change terms in writing before accepting anything.

**Next Action**

Build your offer-comparison table for every school you're seriously considering, using the cost-of-attendance framework above, before you make any verbal commitment.

### Module 9 — Commit, Apply, and Complete Enrollment

The gap between agreeing to go somewhere and actually being enrolled there is full of paperwork, deadlines, and two separate offices that don't always talk to each other. This module is the checklist for closing that gap.

**Choosing Between Offers: Fit Beyond Football**

Once you have real, comparable offers in hand, weigh them on more than the football program.

Academic fit: is the degree you actually want offered, and is it strong in that field, not just generally reputable?

Athletic fit: where do you realistically sit on the depth chart, and does the scheme suit your skill set, covered in detail back in Module 4?

Financial fit: your actual net cost from Module 8's comparison, not the headline number.

Personal fit: location, climate, distance from a major airport for the trip home, and campus size and culture.

Coaching stability: how long has the current staff been there, and what happens to your situation if they leave?

International-student support: does the school have a genuinely resourced international student office, or are you likely to be figuring things out largely alone?

No single factor should override all the others. A team that's a great athletic fit at a school that's a poor personal or financial fit for four years is a real tradeoff, not an easy decision.

**Making It Official: Commitments and Signing**

Getting from a good conversation with a coach to actually being cleared to practise involves more separate approvals than most families expect, and a coach's enthusiasm only ever covers one of them.

1. Recruiting commitment: a verbal, often public statement (sometimes announced on social media) that you intend to attend a particular school. It is not binding on either side and can change.
2. Athletic agreement: the actual binding document. This is where recent changes matter, covered below.
3. Application: submitted to the university's own admissions office, separately from anything athletics has told you.
4. Admission: the university's own decision, made by admissions, that you're accepted as a student. Athletics does not control this.
5. Eligibility certification: covered in full in Module 7, a separate governing-body process running in parallel, not replaced by admission or by signing an athletic agreement.
6. Enrollment confirmation: your formal acceptance of your place, usually tied to a deposit.
7. Deposit payment: money paid to secure your spot, deadline-driven and often non-refundable.
8. Housing: a separate application and deadline, sometimes due before you've even been admitted.
9. Course registration: happens once you're enrolled, usually coordinated around an orientation date.
10. International-student processing: your school's international student office confirms your documentation separately from admissions, athletics, and eligibility. Your visa itself is covered in a later section.
11. Final permission to begin participation: the athletic department's own confirmation, once every one of the above is actually complete, that you're cleared to practise and compete.

On the athletic agreement specifically: for decades, Division I and II athletes signed a National Letter of Intent (NLI) to make their commitment official. In October 2024, the NCAA Division I Council eliminated the NLI for Division I. It has been replaced by a written offer of athletic aid, a binding financial aid agreement signed directly between the athlete and the institution, tied to the same non-contact protections the NLI used to provide: once you sign it, other schools are prohibited from recruiting communications with you. Division II continues to use the traditional NLI. NAIA and NJCAA each run their own separate commitment and signing documents, unrelated to the NCAA's process.

One practical detail worth knowing: signing a written athletic-aid agreement does not require your eligibility certification to already be finalised. But if certification isn't complete by the time you're meant to enrol, the agreement becomes void and the aid is lost. Signing the agreement is not a substitute for finishing eligibility certification; the two run on parallel tracks.

A coach's approval touches exactly one of the eleven steps above. The other ten are handled by people your coach doesn't manage and, in some cases, has never met.

**Submitting Your Application**

Once you've chosen where to apply, the application itself runs through the university's admissions office, separately from anything athletics has told you.

What's typically involved:
• An application submitted through the school's own admissions portal or a common application platform
• Supporting documents: your academic record package from Module 6, and any required test scores
• Any credential evaluation the school requires
• An application fee, though some schools waive this for recruited athletes; confirm rather than assume

Submit well before the stated deadline, not on the day it closes, and keep a copy or screenshot of everything you send, with the date. International applications sometimes take longer to process, and a late or incomplete submission is one of the most avoidable ways to lose momentum you've spent years building.

**Understanding Your Admission Decision**

Universities issue a few different kinds of decisions, and the language matters.

Unconditional admission: you've been accepted outright, meeting all academic requirements as submitted.

Conditional admission: you've been accepted contingent on something specific, often final Year 12 results, a final transcript, or a test score still to come in. Ask exactly what the condition is and the deadline to satisfy it.

Deferral: a decision has been postponed, often to a later admission round, and is not a rejection.

Denial: the application was not successful. This can happen independently of a strong athletic offer, since admissions and athletics are separate offices, as covered in Module 7.

Whatever the decision, get it confirmed in writing and understand exactly what, if anything, still needs to happen before it becomes final.

**Confirming Enrollment: Housing, Health, and Paperwork**

Being admitted is not the same as being enrolled. A series of separate confirmations still has to happen.

Typically required:
• A deposit, often non-refundable, that reserves your spot and is usually due well before the semester starts
• A housing application, sometimes due before you've even accepted admission, on its own separate deadline
• Health forms and immunisation records, often required by the university's health service regardless of athletic status
• Athletic physicals and medical clearance, required separately by the athletic department
• Evidence of health insurance meeting the university's requirements

Miss a deposit or housing deadline and you can lose your place even after being admitted. Track every one of these on the same calendar you've been using for admissions and athletics.

**Getting Ready for Orientation and Classes**

Once enrollment is confirmed, a final set of administrative steps gets you into an actual classroom.

• Orientation registration, usually mandatory and often the first time you'll meet academic advisors in person
• Course registration, typically guided by an academic advisor and sometimes coordinated with your athletic department around training schedules
• Setting up your student account for billing and communications
• Confirming payment deadlines for tuition and fees not covered by your aid

Ask your academic advisor and your position coach separately about how course registration and training schedules are coordinated. Assuming they already talk to each other is the same mistake as assuming admissions and athletics talk to each other.

**International Student and Compliance Requirements**

Beyond admissions and athletics, a university's international student office has its own separate requirements, focused on your status as an international student specifically.

What this typically covers:
• Confirming your academic documents meet the university's own international-admission standards, which can be a different check to what admissions or athletics already reviewed
• Health insurance requirements specific to international students, which are sometimes stricter than the general student requirement
• Check-in requirements once you arrive on campus

The visa process itself (F-1 or J-1 documentation, the SEVIS fee, and your embassy interview) is a separate, larger topic covered in full in a later section. This section is only about the international student office's role once you're already admitted and enrolling. Don't conflate international-student office sign-off with visa approval; they're related but not the same process.

**When Admissions and Athletics Don't Agree**

At some point, admissions, athletics, financial aid, and the international student office will likely give you instructions that don't perfectly line up. This is common, not a sign something has gone wrong.

What to do:
• Get every instruction in writing, from each office, with the name of who gave it
• When two instructions conflict, go back to both offices with the other's instruction in writing and ask them to resolve it between themselves rather than asking you to guess
• Keep a single point of contact where possible, often your recruiting coordinator or an assigned academic advisor, and route conflicting information through them
• Don't assume the most recent instruction overrides an earlier one; confirm which one is actually correct

This is one of the most common sources of genuine stress in the enrollment process, and it is a coordination problem between university departments, not something you did wrong.

**Tracking Every Deadline**

By this stage you're tracking deadlines across several separate systems: recruiting, admissions, financial aid, athletics, and soon your visa. Put them in one place.

Build a single tracker with columns for:
• Deadline and what it's for
• Which office owns it (admissions, financial aid, athletics, international student office)
• Status
• Confirmation received, and from whom

Review it weekly from the point you accept an offer through to departure. A missed housing or deposit deadline can undo months of recruiting work in a way that's entirely avoidable with a shared calendar.

**Your Pre-Visa Enrollment Checklist**

Everything below should be complete before you move into the visa process covered in the next section.

Commitment and application
□ Written athletic-aid offer (or NLI, if Division II) received and reviewed with a parent or guardian
□ Application submitted to admissions, with confirmation of receipt
□ Admission decision received and any conditions satisfied

Eligibility
□ Eligibility certification finalised with the relevant governing body, not just submitted

Enrollment
□ Deposit paid and confirmed
□ Housing application submitted and confirmed
□ Health forms, immunisation records, and athletic physical completed
□ Health insurance meeting university requirements confirmed

Academics
□ Orientation registration completed
□ Course registration guidance received from an academic advisor

International student office
□ International-student-specific requirements confirmed directly with that office

Coordination
□ Any conflicting instructions between offices identified and resolved in writing

Once every box here is checked, you're ready to move into visa and departure preparation, covered in the next section.

**Key Takeaways**

Getting from an offer to actually starting classes runs through several separate approvals: recruiting commitment, a written and signed athletic agreement, admissions, eligibility certification, enrollment confirmation, and international-student sign-off. A coach's approval only ever covers one of these. Track every deadline in one place, and when offices give you conflicting instructions, get both in writing and make them resolve it, rather than guessing yourself.

**Next Action**

Confirm which document your target school actually uses now for a binding commitment (a written athletic-aid agreement, or, if Division II, a National Letter of Intent), and ask directly what happens to that agreement if your eligibility certification isn't finalised in time.

## Section 3: Visa, Departure, Travel, and Arrival

### Module 10 — Understand the U.S. Student Visa Process

Being admitted to a school does not mean you have permission to enter the United States. This module maps the whole visa process and introduces a major regulatory change that affects nearly every part of it.

**Admission Is Not Visa Eligibility**

Being admitted to a school, even with a scholarship and full eligibility certification, does not give you permission to enter the United States. That permission runs through an entirely separate government process, decided by different authorities than the ones covered in Section 2.

The full sequence, once you're admitted:
1. Your school issues a Form I-20, tied to your academic program
2. You pay the SEVIS I-901 fee
3. You apply for an F-1 visa through the U.S. Department of State
4. If approved, a visa is placed in your passport
5. You travel to a U.S. port of entry and request admission from U.S. Customs and Border Protection
6. CBP decides, that day, whether and for how long to admit you

Every one of these six steps is a separate decision by a separate authority. A scholarship offer has no bearing on any of them. Treat each one as its own approval, the same way Section 2 taught you to treat recruiting, admission, and eligibility as separate approvals.

**Your Immigration Timeline at a Glance** *(timeline)*

Every step here is a separate decision by a separate authority. The detailed explanation of each one follows in this module and the next three.

Steps:
1. Admission Offer
2. I-20 Issued

5. Visa Interview
6. Visa Issued
7. Travel to the U.S.
8. CBP Admission

10. School Check-In

15. Departure Period

**The Role of Your International Student Office and DSO**

Your school's international student office, and specifically your Designated School Official (DSO), is your primary point of contact for everything in this section. Not admissions, not athletics.

A DSO is a school official specifically authorised by the U.S. government to issue and manage your immigration records. Larger schools have a Principal DSO (PDSO) overseeing several DSOs. Your DSO:
• Issues and updates your Form I-20
• Manages your record in SEVIS, the government's student tracking system
• Advises you on maintaining your status once you arrive
• Is who you contact first for almost any visa or status question, including anything covered later in this section

Get your DSO's name and direct contact details as soon as you're admitted, separately from whatever contact you have with admissions or the athletic department. This relationship matters more, for longer, than either of those.

Before moving on:
✓ Save your school's international student office and DSO contact information somewhere you won't lose it.

**SEVP-Certified Schools and Your Form I-20**

Only schools certified by the Student and Exchange Visitor Program (SEVP), part of the Department of Homeland Security, may issue the Form I-20, officially the 'Certificate of Eligibility for Nonimmigrant Student Status.' Confirm your school is SEVP-certified for the specific program you're joining; certification applies at the program level, not just the institution.

What the I-20 contains: your identifying information, your program of study, expected start and end dates, estimated costs, and how you'll fund them. You and your DSO must both sign it. If you're under 18, a parent must also sign.

What's changed and matters immediately: under a major rule taking effect September 15, 2026, your I-20's program end date is no longer just a school record; it becomes the anchor for your actual, fixed permitted stay in the United States, covered in full in Module 13. Check every detail on your I-20 the moment you receive it: name, date of birth, and program dates all need to match your passport and your later visa application exactly.

Before moving on:
✓ Check every detail on your I-20 against your passport.
✓ Print two copies of your I-20 and keep one separate from the original.
✓ Add your program end date to your calendar now.

**SEVIS and Your Form I-94: Related but Not Interchangeable**

These two records get confused constantly, and the confusion has gotten more consequential under the new rule.

Your Form I-20 is issued by your school. It's essentially your school's certification of your program and your funding, tracked in SEVIS, the government's Student and Exchange Visitor Information System. It exists before you travel and gets updated by your DSO as your program changes.

Your Form I-94 is issued by U.S. Customs and Border Protection, not your school, the moment you're actually admitted at the border. It's the government's own record of your permitted stay, and it's what you legally rely on to know how long you may remain.

The two are related, since your I-94 admission period is now tied to the program end date on your I-20, but they are not interchangeable and are not automatically kept in sync. A DSO updating your I-20 does not update your I-94. Only CBP, or USCIS through an Extension of Stay, can change your I-94. Get in the habit now of thinking of these as two separate documents from two separate authorities, because Module 13 depends on you keeping that distinction straight.

**The SEVIS I-901 Fee**

Before you can apply for your visa, you must pay the SEVIS I-901 fee, a one-time payment tied to your specific SEVIS record.

What to know:
• The fee funds the system that tracks international students, separate from any school or visa application fee
• Your SEVIS ID number on the payment receipt must exactly match the one on your Form I-20; a mismatch causes delays
• Keep the payment receipt; you must present it at your visa interview
• Pay it only through the official government payment site, never through a third party claiming to process it for you

Do not assume the fee amount from memory or from a recruiting company's website. Confirm the current fee directly on the official SEVP payment page before paying, since this figure changes periodically.

**The DS-160 and Applying for Your Visa**

The DS-160 is the U.S. Department of State's Online Nonimmigrant Visa Application, and it's the foundation of your entire visa application, submitted through the official government site, not a third-party service.

What it covers: your personal background, education history, travel history, family details, and a set of security and background questions. You'll also upload a photo meeting specific requirements.

Why accuracy matters intensely: a consular officer reviews your DS-160 before and during your interview, and any inconsistency between what you wrote and what you say in person is treated as a serious red flag, not a minor slip. Answer every question carefully and honestly.

After submitting, print the confirmation page with its barcode; you'll need it at your interview. Confirm current session time limits and validity periods directly on the official site rather than relying on a guide, since these details are administered by the government and can change.

**The U.S. Embassy or Consulate's Role**

Your visa interview and the actual decision to issue your visa happen at a U.S. embassy or consulate, run by the U.S. Department of State, a completely different part of government from the school, from SEVP, and from the CBP officers you'll meet at the border later.

What this means practically: a consular officer, not your school and not your coach, decides whether to issue your visa, based on U.S. law and the evidence you present. You generally interview at the embassy or consulate in the country where you live, though exceptions exist. Each embassy or consulate can have its own specific procedures, appointment systems, and wait times, so check the specific location's official page directly rather than assuming a generic process.

**The F-1 Visa, and Whether Another Status Might Apply**

F-1 is the standard visa category for international students pursuing a full academic program, like a bachelor's degree, at an SEVP-certified school, and it's what almost every student using this academy will apply for.

A couple of related categories exist and are worth knowing about even if they don't apply to you: M-1 is for vocational or non-academic study, and J-1 covers exchange visitor programs, sometimes used for specific structured exchange arrangements rather than standard degree study. The name printed on your Form I-20 (Academic and Language Student versus Vocational Student) determines which visa category actually applies to you; it isn't a choice you make yourself.

If you're unsure which category fits your specific program, particularly if it involves a prep year, an exchange arrangement, or a non-standard enrollment, ask your DSO directly before assuming F-1 applies.

**Visa Issuance Is Not Permission to Enter**

A visa in your passport is permission to travel to the United States and request admission. It is not, on its own, permission to actually enter.

That second decision, made at the port of entry, belongs entirely to U.S. Customs and Border Protection, a different agency again from the Department of State that issued your visa. A CBP officer can, in principle, deny entry even to someone holding a valid visa, though this is uncommon when documents are in order and answers are truthful and consistent.

What's changed under the rule taking effect September 15, 2026: CBP's decision at the border now also sets your fixed admission period, tied to your I-20 program end date, rather than the old open-ended 'duration of status.' Your visa gets you to the border. CBP's decision at the border is what actually determines how long you may stay, and that decision is covered in full in Modules 12 and 13.

**Student and Parent Responsibilities Through the Process**

This process runs on your own accuracy and follow-through, with a clearly defined role for a parent or guardian alongside you.

Your responsibilities: submitting accurate, honest information on every form, meeting every deadline yourself rather than assuming someone else is tracking them, keeping every document safe, and reporting changes to your DSO promptly once you're studying.

A parent or guardian's role: helping demonstrate financial support, being present for interview preparation, signing your I-20 if you're under 18, and being someone you can talk through the process with, particularly the interview. A parent cannot attend the visa interview itself or answer for you, but their involvement in preparation is expected and valuable.

Neither a coach nor a recruiting service can substitute for either of these roles. This is a government process involving you and your family directly.

**Your Overall Timeline, Realistically**

Budget months, not weeks, and start as soon as your I-20 arrives.

Roughly, in order:
1. Receive your I-20 from your school
2. Pay the SEVIS I-901 fee
3. Complete and submit the DS-160
4. Schedule and attend your visa interview
5. If approved, your visa is issued in your passport
6. Travel to the United States, generally no earlier than 30 days before your program start date
7. Enter through a port of entry and receive your electronic Form I-94
8. Check in with your school and your DSO

Interview appointment availability varies significantly by embassy and by season, and visas are generally only issued within a set window before your program start date. Start the process the moment your I-20 arrives rather than waiting until you feel ready; the process has its own timeline, independent of yours.

**Common Misunderstandings About This Process**

Clear up these before you start, because each one causes real problems if assumed rather than checked.

• 'I'm admitted, so my visa is basically a formality.' No. Admission and visa approval are entirely separate decisions by entirely separate authorities.
• 'My coach can help sort out my visa.' No. A coach has no role in, or authority over, any part of this process.
• 'A scholarship guarantees my visa.' No. Financial support is one factor a consular officer considers, not a guarantee of anything.
• 'Getting my visa means I'm guaranteed to enter.' No. CBP makes an independent decision at the border.
• 'My I-20 end date is automatically how long I can stay.' Under the rule taking effect September 15, 2026, your actual permitted stay is set by CBP at entry and recorded on your I-94, connected to but not automatically identical to your I-20 date. Module 13 covers this in full.
• 'This process works the way it did for students a few years ago.' A major rule change affects almost everything about how long you're admitted for. Don't rely on older guides, forum posts, or even this year's version of this handbook without checking it's still current.

### Module 11 — Prepare and Apply for the Student Visa

Your visa application succeeds or fails on preparation and accuracy, not persuasion. This module covers exactly what to gather and how the interview actually works.

**Reviewing Your Form I-20 Before You Apply**

Before you touch the DS-160, confirm every detail on your I-20 is exactly right. This is the single most preventable cause of visa delays.

Check specifically:
• Your name matches your passport exactly, same spelling, same order
• Your date of birth is correct
• Your school's name and SEVIS school code are correct
• Your program start and end dates make sense against your actual enrollment plan
• The financial information matches what you were actually awarded and what your family is actually contributing

If anything is wrong, go back to your DSO for a correction before you apply for your visa, not after. A mismatch between your I-20 and your passport, caught by a consular officer instead of by you, causes exactly the kind of delay this section exists to prevent.

**Proving You Can Afford It**

A consular officer needs to see that the gap between your scholarship and your full cost of attendance, covered in Section 2, is actually covered by someone.

What to bring:
• Your signed athletic-aid or scholarship award letter
• Documentation of any sponsor's ability to pay, typically a parent: bank statements, proof of income or employment
• Bank records showing funds available for costs your aid doesn't cover
• Anything showing your total cost of attendance, so the officer can see your numbers actually add up

The goal isn't to prove you're wealthy. It's to show a credible, specific plan for covering the actual gap, with documents, not just an assurance that 'it'll work out.'

**Passport Validity and Photo Requirements**

Handle your passport early. It's the one document nothing else in this process can substitute for.

Validity: most countries, including the United States for this purpose, expect your passport to remain valid for at least six months beyond your intended stay. Check the expiry date now, not when you're ready to apply.

Renewing: the Australian Passport Office handles renewals; start well before you need it, since processing takes time and this pathway runs on tight windows already.

Photo: your DS-160 photo has specific requirements, a recent colour photo against a white background, meeting the exact size and pose specifications on the official site. Use the test-photo tool on the application itself before you rely on it.

Before moving on:
✓ Locate your passport's expiry date right now, and renew if it's anywhere near the six-month threshold.

**Completing the DS-160 Accurately**

The DS-160 is submitted through the official Consular Electronic Application Center at ceac.state.gov, not through any third-party website, however official-looking.

What it covers: personal background, education history, travel history, family details, and a set of security questions, all of which must match your other documents exactly.

Practical points: save your application ID immediately in case your session times out, upload your photo directly in the form, and once submitted you cannot edit it; if you spot an error afterward, you generally start a fresh application rather than correcting the old one. Print the confirmation page with its barcode; you'll need it at your interview.

**Paying Fees and Scheduling Your Interview**

Two separate fees are involved, both paid before you're actually sitting in front of a consular officer: the SEVIS I-901 fee covered in Module 10, and a separate nonimmigrant visa application fee.

Don't rely on a remembered figure for either fee; confirm the current amount directly on the official site before paying, since both change periodically.

Scheduling: appointment systems are run separately by each embassy or consulate, and availability varies significantly by location and season. Visas are also generally only issued within a defined window before your program start date, not indefinitely far in advance, so time your application accordingly rather than applying the moment you're admitted and then waiting for months.

**Choosing the Right Embassy or Consulate**

You'll generally interview at the U.S. embassy or consulate in the country where you legally reside, which for most students following this academy means an Australian post.

Some flexibility exists to interview elsewhere, but applying outside your home country can mean slower processing or a higher chance of refusal for administrative reasons unrelated to your actual application. Check the specific location's official page directly for its own procedures, current wait times, and any location-specific requirements before assuming a generic process applies everywhere.

**Preparing for the Interview: What You'll Be Asked**

Expect direct questions, and prepare direct, honest, specific answers rather than rehearsed-sounding ones.

Common areas:
• What you're going to study and why this specific school
• How your studies are being funded
• Your ties to Australia and your plans after finishing your program
• Your football participation, which is part of your story, not a substitute for your academic purpose

Be ready to discuss football honestly. You're not hiding that it's part of why you're going. But your answers about study plans and funding should stand on their own, not depend on the officer being impressed by the athletic side of your file.

**Demonstrating Study Plans, Financial Readiness, and Ties Home**

A student visa is a nonimmigrant visa, meaning you're expected to show a genuine, temporary purpose and an intention to return home once that purpose is complete.

What this looks like in practice: family, property, or career prospects in Australia, a clear and specific study plan tied to your actual program, and financial arrangements that make sense for a temporary stay rather than a permanent move.

This doesn't mean pretending you have no interest in what might come after graduation, including a professional football opportunity. It means your current, stated purpose for this visa is temporary study, and your answers should reflect that honestly rather than overstating certainty about a future that hasn't happened yet.

**Documents to Carry to Your Interview**

Bring physical copies of everything, even documents you've already submitted electronically.

Standard set:
• Valid passport
• DS-160 confirmation page with barcode
• Form I-20
• Your school's admission letter
• SEVIS I-901 fee receipt
• Financial evidence covered earlier in this module
• Academic records from Module 6
• A passport photo meeting requirements, as a backup even if uploaded

Organise these in one folder, in a logical order, rather than searching through a bag during your interview.

**Interview Conduct and Truthful Answers** *(callout)*

Answer every question truthfully and concisely. This is not a performance to win, it's a factual review, and treating it as anything else creates real risk.

What matters:
• Answer the question actually asked, without over-explaining or volunteering unrelated information
• Never provide false information, false documents, or omit a material fact. Misrepresentation to a consular officer can affect your ability to obtain a visa not just now but for years afterward, and is a completely different category of problem than a weak answer
• If you don't understand a question, ask for it to be repeated or clarified rather than guessing
• Dress neatly and arrive early

This handbook will never suggest a way to phrase, soften, or omit something to make an answer more favourable. If something about your history feels complicated, the right move is honesty plus, where appropriate, advice from a qualified immigration professional beforehand, not a more careful version of the same story.

**Administrative Processing, Refusal, and Reapplication**

Three outcomes are possible, and each has a different meaning.

Approved: your visa is issued and your passport is typically returned to you, sometimes the same day, sometimes after further processing.

Administrative processing: your case needs additional review before a decision. This is not a denial, and timelines vary considerably, from days to months. Avoid booking non-changeable travel until this resolves.

Refused: generally because the officer wasn't satisfied you demonstrated the temporary, nonimmigrant intent covered earlier in this module, or because required information or documents were missing. A refusal on one of these grounds doesn't necessarily bar you from reapplying, but reapplying with the same weaknesses in your case produces the same outcome. If you're refused, get a clear understanding of the stated reason before deciding whether or how to reapply, and consider qualified immigration advice if the reason is unclear or if there's more to your history than a simple documentation gap.

**Planning When Your Program May Run Longer Than Your Initial Admission**

Your visa interview does not set the length of time you'll actually be admitted for. That decision belongs to CBP, at the border, covered in Module 12, and it now follows the fixed-admission-period rule covered throughout this section.

What this means if your program might run longer than a standard timeline, for example if a redshirt year or an academic change extends things: don't assume your visa's validity period (how long you can use it to request entry) is the same thing as your admission period (how long you're actually allowed to stay once admitted). These are different concepts, decided by different agencies. If your program is likely to run past your initial admission period, start that conversation with your DSO well in advance; Module 13 covers the Extension of Stay process you may need.

**Visa Scams and Unofficial Services** *(callout)*

Be alert for: agents guaranteeing a visa outcome, unofficial websites that look like embassy or government pages but ask for payment through unusual channels, anyone asking you to submit false bank records or false documents, and pressure to pay extra for a 'guaranteed' interview slot.

No one can guarantee a visa outcome, and no legitimate service asks you to falsify anything. Always use the official government sites directly for payments and scheduling, never a link from an email or a third party.

Financial and document prep
□ I-20 checked against passport, corrected with DSO if needed
□ Financial evidence gathered and organised
□ Passport valid at least six months beyond intended stay

Application
□ DS-160 completed accurately and submitted
□ Confirmation page printed
□ SEVIS fee and visa application fee paid, current amounts confirmed

Interview
□ Interview scheduled at the correct embassy or consulate
□ Documents organised in one folder
□ Interview answers prepared honestly, not rehearsed

After

□ If issued, visa details checked for accuracy
□ If administrative processing, no non-changeable travel booked yet

**What to Do Once Your Visa Is Issued**

Getting the visa is genuinely good news, but two things still need checking before you relax.

First, check the visa itself: your name, visa category, and validity dates should all be correct. Report any error to the issuing embassy or consulate immediately.

Second, understand what it does and doesn't tell you: your visa's validity period is about how long you can use it to request entry, generally allowing multiple entries within that window. It is not the same as your admission period, which CBP decides when you actually arrive, covered fully in Module 12. Don't assume the years printed on your visa are how long you'll be allowed to stay.

You may generally travel to the United States no earlier than 30 days before your program start date, covered next.

**Common Mistakes**

Watch for these specifically at this stage:

• Submitting a DS-160 with answers that don't match the I-20 or other documents
• Bringing incomplete or vague financial evidence instead of specific, documented figures
• Treating the interview as if it's only about football, and under-preparing the study-plan and funding answers
• Assuming visa issuance guarantees entry, covered fully in Module 12
• Paying for a 'guaranteed' visa outcome from anyone other than the official government process

None of these are about ability or worthiness. They're about preparation and accuracy, both fully within your control.

**Key Takeaways**

Your visa application succeeds on accuracy and honest preparation, not persuasion. Check your I-20 against your passport before you start, bring specific and complete financial evidence, and answer every interview question truthfully. Your visa's validity period is not the same as your eventual admission period, which is decided separately at the border.

**Next Action**

Pull together your financial evidence documents now, even before scheduling your interview, so you know what gaps still need to be filled.

### Module 12 — Prepare for Travel and Enter the United States

A visa lets you request entry. It does not guarantee it. This module covers travel logistics and exactly what happens at the U.S. border.

**When You May Travel**

You may generally enter the United States no earlier than 30 days before the program start date on your Form I-20. That window is your actual lawful entry point, not a suggestion.

A mistake worth naming directly: Australians can normally visit the U.S. as tourists through the Visa Waiver Program (ESTA) without a visa. That is not the correct way to enter if your actual purpose is to study and compete. Entering on a tourist basis and hoping to sort out your student status afterward does not work reliably, and a CBP officer who identifies a mismatch between your stated purpose and your travel authorization can refuse entry on the spot. Travel specifically on your F-1 visa, for its actual purpose.

Your school may also have its own required arrival date, sometimes earlier than the bare minimum, for training camp, orientation, or team activities. Confirm both dates before booking anything.

Before booking:
✓ Confirm your program start date and your earliest lawful entry date (30 days prior).
✓ Confirm your school's own required arrival date, which may be earlier.

**Booking Flights and Planning Connections**

Book only once your entry window and your school's required arrival date are both confirmed, not before.

What to consider:
• A flexible or refundable ticket is worth the extra cost given how visa timing can shift
• Connecting flights mean your CBP inspection generally happens at your first U.S. port of entry, even if your final destination is another domestic flight; build in a realistic connection time for that
• Confirm housing availability and airport pickup arrangements before you lock in your arrival date, not after

Before booking:
✓ Confirm housing availability.
✓ Confirm airport pickup instructions with your school.

**Travelling as a Minor: Consent and Airline Rules**

If you're under 18, several extra layers apply, and they exist for real safety reasons, not as red tape.

Before you fly:
• Parent or guardian consent documentation may be required by your airline; check the specific airline's policy, since these vary and are airline-specific, not universal
• Understand your airline's unaccompanied-minor policy if travelling without a parent, including any age thresholds and fees
• Have a school-approved pickup plan in writing, with the full name and verifiable identity of whoever is meeting you
• Carry emergency contacts for both your family and your school, and know the backup plan if your flight is delayed and your pickup timing changes

Non-negotiable safeguarding points:
• Never surrender your passport to a recruiter, driver, teammate, or any adult who isn't performing an official function like a CBP officer or your DSO. No one else has a legitimate reason to hold it
• Never accept an unofficial transportation arrangement that wasn't confirmed in writing with your school in advance
• Keep your parents and your school informed at every connection, not just your final arrival
• Carry medication in original packaging with supporting documentation, and know your housing contact details before you land

A school's official pickup and check-in process is not the same thing as an immigration document, but treat it with the same seriousness: confirm it in writing beforehand, and don't improvise on the day.

**Your Carry-On Document Pack**

Everything in this list travels in your carry-on. None of it goes in checked luggage, ever, regardless of how full your carry-on gets.

The pack:
• Passport
• Visa
• Form I-20, signed and current
• SEVIS I-901 fee receipt
• Admission and enrollment records
• Financial documents covered in Module 11
• Your school's contact details and your DSO's direct contact information
• Your U.S. accommodation address
• Medication, in original packaging, with supporting documentation

Checked luggage can be delayed, misrouted, or lost. Every document on this list is something you cannot simply replace at the airport if it goes missing.

Before leaving Australia:
✓ Place every original immigration document in carry-on luggage, not checked.
✓ Save electronic copies of everything securely, separate from the originals.
✓ Print your school's and DSO's contact information on paper as a backup.
✓ Confirm your U.S. accommodation address in writing.

**Packing, Customs, and Restricted Items**

Every traveller entering the United States must declare specific categories of items, and getting this wrong causes real, avoidable penalties.

What must be declared: meats, fruits, vegetables, plants, seeds, soil, and other agricultural or animal products, even packaged or processed ones, and even a piece of fruit from the plane. Declare on the customs form or app; a CBP agriculture specialist decides whether it's admissible. Undeclared items risk fines, while declared items that turn out to be prohibited are simply given up at the border with no penalty for declaring honestly.

Money: carrying large amounts of currency across the border generally requires a declaration above a set threshold; confirm the current threshold on the official CBP site rather than assuming a figure.

Medication: keep prescription medication in its original packaging, and bring supporting documentation, particularly for anything that could be questioned as a controlled substance.

When in doubt about any item, declare it. Declaring something that turns out to be fine costs you nothing. Not declaring something that turns out to be restricted can cost real money and unwanted scrutiny.

**Money, Payment Access, and Phone Access on Arrival**

Arrive with a plan for both, not an assumption that you'll sort it out once you land.

Money: bring a mix of a small amount of U.S. cash and a card that works internationally without excessive fees; check your bank's foreign transaction fee policy before you leave. Setting up a U.S. bank account is covered in a later section, so budget for a few weeks of using Australian-linked access first.

Phone: arrange either an international roaming plan for your first days or a plan to get a U.S. SIM or eSIM immediately on arrival. Your school and your family both need a working number for you as soon as possible, not after a few days of being unreachable.

**Travel and Health Insurance Before You Fly**

You need coverage that starts on your travel date, not on your first day of class.

Travel insurance covers the trip itself: lost luggage, flight disruption, and medical issues during travel. Health insurance, covered in full in Section 2, needs to be active before you arrive, since an injury or illness in your first days on U.S. soil is not covered by a policy that only starts at orientation. Confirm your specific start date for both with your provider and your school before you fly, not after something's already gone wrong.

**What Happens at the Port of Entry**

At the port of entry, a CBP officer examines your passport and visa and decides whether to admit you. Expect a short, direct conversation, not an interrogation.

What to expect: you may be asked why you're travelling to the United States. Be ready to say clearly that you're arriving to study at a specific school, the same honest, concise standard covered in Module 11's interview guidance applies here too.

Most inspections are routine and brief. Some travellers are referred to secondary inspection for additional review, covered in its own section below; this is a normal part of the system, not a sign you did something wrong.

**The Role of U.S. Customs and Border Protection**

U.S. Customs and Border Protection is a completely separate agency from the U.S. Department of State, which issued your visa, and from your school, which issued your I-20.

CBP's role is narrow but final: at the actual moment you arrive, a CBP officer decides whether to admit you, and for how long, under the fixed-admission-period rule covered throughout this section. Holding a valid visa means you may request admission. It does not bind CBP's decision. This is the same distinction covered in Module 10, now playing out in person, at the border.

**Checking Your Form I-94 After Entry**

This is the single most important administrative task of your entire arrival, and it's also the most commonly skipped.

When it's created: the moment a CBP officer admits you, an electronic Form I-94 is generated. You are not handed a paper copy at most airports.

Where to get it: retrieve it yourself at i94.cbp.dhs.gov (or the CBP One app) using your passport details, as soon as possible after you arrive.

What to check, in detail:
• Your name, spelled exactly as on your passport
• Your class of admission, which should read F-1
• Your admit-until date

Do not assume it says 'D/S.' Under the rule covered throughout this section, your I-94 now carries a specific fixed date rather than the old open-ended duration of status, and that date, not a general sense of 'as long as I'm a student,' is your actual legal permission to remain. It should relate to your I-20 program end date, but confirm the two actually match; they are not automatically identical, as covered in Module 10.

If anything is wrong: contact your DSO immediately, the same day if possible. Depending on the error, correction may go through your DSO, direct contact with CBP, or in some cases a formal CBP correction process. Don't wait to see if it 'sorts itself out.'

Save both a digital copy and a printed copy the day you retrieve it. You'll likely need to produce it again later, for a job on campus, a re-entry after travel, or an Extension of Stay application covered in Module 13.

After entry:
✓ Retrieve your Form I-94 as soon as possible.
✓ Check your name, class of admission, and admit-until date.
✓ Save a digital copy and print one.
✓ Contact your DSO immediately if anything looks wrong.

**Secondary Inspection: What It Means**

Secondary inspection means you've been sent for a closer, more thorough review, in a separate area away from the main inspection line.

This happens for many routine reasons and is a normal, built-in part of the system, not an accusation. Stay calm, answer questions honestly and directly, and have your documents organised and ready to hand over. Most secondary inspections resolve within a reasonably short time and end in normal admission.

**Delays, Missed Connections, and Entry Problems**

A few things can go wrong, and each has a specific right response.

Delayed or missed connections: contact your school and your pre-arranged pickup as soon as you know your arrival timing has changed. Don't let anyone find out you're late by simply not showing up.

Lost documents in transit: report a lost passport to the nearest Australian embassy or consulate immediately, and tell your DSO the same day.

Denied entry or deferred inspection: rare, but serious. If it happens, contact your DSO and, given the legal stakes, qualified immigration counsel as soon as possible. This is not something to try to resolve alone or based on general advice, including this handbook.

**First-Arrival Checklist**

Airport to campus, in order:

□ CBP inspection completed, admitted into the U.S.
□ Form I-94 retrieved and checked (name, class of admission, admit-until date)
□ Digital and printed copy of I-94 saved
□ Pickup or transport to accommodation confirmed
□ School and family notified of safe arrival
□ Phone access sorted (roaming or local SIM)
□ Medication and essential documents accounted for
□ Housing check-in scheduled

Module 13 picks up from here: what to do once you've actually arrived and settled in.

**Common Mistakes**

Watch for these specifically around travel and entry:

• Booking travel before confirming the actual permitted entry window
• Arriving on a tourist or ESTA basis when F-1 entry is what's actually required
• Packing the passport, visa, or Form I-20 in checked baggage
• Carrying an outdated or unsigned Form I-20
• Not knowing the school's address or the DSO's contact information
• Giving vague or inconsistent answers to a CBP officer
• Never retrieving or checking the Form I-94 after arrival
• Assuming 'D/S' will appear on the I-94, when the current rule sets a fixed date instead
• Ignoring an admit-until date that looks wrong instead of raising it immediately
• Bringing undeclared agricultural or food products
• Relying on an unofficial airport pickup arrangement
• Failing to tell the school about a delayed arrival

Every one of these is a preparation or communication failure, not bad luck. All of them are avoidable with the checklists in this module.

**Key Takeaways**

A visa lets you request entry. CBP decides admission and, under the current rule, sets a fixed admission date on your Form I-94, so retrieving and checking that document the moment you arrive is not optional. Keep every immigration document in your carry-on, never checked luggage, and if you're under 18, treat pickup and consent arrangements with the same seriousness as the documents themselves.

### Module 13 — Manage Your Immigration Timeline

Passport, visa, I-20, SEVIS, I-94, program dates, and a possible Extension of Stay are different documents with different owners, different expiration rules, and different consequences if you get them confused. This module makes sure you never mix them up.

**Your Immigration Timeline at a Glance** *(timeline)*

Everything in this module maps onto this cycle. Your Form I-20 does not, by itself, determine how long you're authorised to remain in the United States, the detail behind every step below does.

Steps:
1. I-94 Admission Period Begins
2. Monitor I-94 vs. I-20 Dates
3. Maintain Full-Time Enrollment
4. Approaching Program End Date
5. File Extension of Stay (if needed)
6. USCIS Receipt Notice
7. Decision: Approved, Denied, or Still Pending
8. Program Completion
9. 30-Day Departure Period
10. Departure or Next Authorised Step

**Form I-94 versus Form I-20 Dates**

You already know these are different documents. This is about living with that distinction day to day, not just understanding it once.

Your Form I-20's program end date is set by your school and can be updated by your DSO as your academic plan changes: a subject change, a delay, a redshirt year, anything that shifts your expected completion. Your Form I-94 admit-until date is the actual government record of how long you may remain, generally set to reflect your program length at the time you were admitted, but it is not automatically kept in sync afterward.

A DSO extending your I-20's program end date does not, by itself, extend your I-94. That generally requires a separate USCIS action, an Extension of Stay, covered later in this module. Get in the habit of comparing both dates regularly, not just when something feels wrong.

**The Fixed Admission Period**

Under the rule effective September 15, 2026, F students are generally admitted for a fixed period of time rather than duration of status. That period is tied to your academic program as shown on your Form I-20, subject to the maximum covered in the next section.

This is not automatically four years for everyone. A shorter program generally results in a shorter admission period. Your Form I-94 is the controlling record of your actual admission period unless it's corrected or superseded by an official government action, not your own assumption about how long a student visa 'usually' lasts.

**The Four-Year Maximum Admission Period**

Four years is a ceiling, not a promise. Keep these distinctions straight:

• Your authorised admission period may not exceed four years
• A program shorter than four years generally results in a correspondingly shorter admission period
• A program that naturally runs longer than four years, which can genuinely happen with a redshirt year or an academic delay, may require an Extension of Stay
• If you're delayed by academic, medical, athletic, or personal circumstances, don't assume your DSO can fix it by simply updating your Form I-20. That may not, by itself, extend your I-94
• Compare your I-94 admit-until date against your realistically expected completion date, not your original best-case plan

Start planning well before your admission period ends. There isn't a single legally fixed lead time this handbook can state as a rule for every student, so treat 'well before' as planning guidance to confirm with your DSO, not a statutory deadline.

**When You Need an Extension of Stay**

This is one of the most consequential sections in this entire academy, and it deserves to be read slowly.

An Extension of Stay (EOS) is a request, generally filed with U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services, for additional authorised time beyond your current Form I-94 admission period. USCIS, not your school, decides it.

Why a Form I-20 update and a USCIS extension are different actions: your DSO can update your program information in SEVIS and issue a new I-20 reflecting a later program end date. That is a school-side academic record change. It is generally not, by itself, an extension of your authorised stay. Getting the extra time you actually need generally requires both the updated school paperwork and a separate USCIS filing.

What's typically involved: evidence of continued enrollment, your DSO's supporting recommendation, and documentation of your circumstances. Filing on time matters enormously, covered in the next section. A USCIS receipt notice confirms your request was received; it is not an approval. Approval extends your authorised stay on the approved terms. A denial can create a serious status problem and needs immediate attention, from your DSO and, given the stakes, qualified immigration counsel.

This handbook cannot tell you your application will be approved, and it will not pretend a timely filing guarantees a good outcome. Individual circumstances genuinely vary, and this is exactly the kind of decision that needs your DSO's direct involvement, not general guidance from any handbook.

**Filing Before Your Authorized Period Expires**

Several distinct things happen in sequence here, and blending them together is where real risk creeps in.

Preparing early is not the same as filing. Filing before your Form I-94 expiration date is not the same as receiving a USCIS receipt. Receiving a receipt is not the same as an approval. And remaining in the United States while a qualifying, timely-filed request is pending is not automatically the same as maintaining every other F-1 requirement without effort: you generally still need to keep meeting your normal F-1 obligations throughout.

Where a filing was genuinely timely and otherwise valid, current rules may generally permit you to remain while USCIS decides, subject to the final rule and USCIS's own guidance at the time. That protection is not unconditional, and it is not something to assume applies to your specific situation without confirming it with your DSO and, where the stakes are high, qualified counsel.

**The Roles of You, Your DSO, SEVIS, and USCIS**

Four different parties, four different jobs. Confusing them is one of the most common ways students lose track of their own status.

YOU are responsible for: monitoring your Form I-94, monitoring your Form I-20, maintaining full-time enrollment, reporting changes, preparing and filing any required applications, keeping your own records, responding to USCIS requests by their deadlines, and asking for help before a deadline arrives, not after.

YOUR DSO is responsible for: maintaining your school's SEVIS records, updating your program information where they're authorised to, issuing or updating your Form I-20, advising you on school and SEVIS procedures, and confirming your school's own institutional requirements. Your DSO does not guarantee USCIS approval, does not grant immigration status, does not replace USCIS or CBP, and does not provide legal representation unless they're separately qualified to do so.

SEVIS is the government's information system for tracking student and school records. It is not itself a visa, a passport, a Form I-94, or an Extension of Stay approval, it's the database behind several of those things, not a substitute for any of them.

USCIS adjudicates the immigration applications that actually matter here, including Extension of Stay requests where one is needed. USCIS's decision, not your DSO's paperwork, is what ultimately extends your authorised stay.

**What Happens While Your Extension of Stay Is Pending**

A pending application is a specific, limited state, not a green light to relax.

What generally applies: a timely filed request may permit you to remain in the United States while USCIS decides, subject to the governing rules current at the time. You must keep complying with your normal F-1 requirements throughout. Employment permission must never be assumed just because an application is pending. International travel may affect or complicate a pending request, covered in full in the travel section below. A pending request is not an approval, and treating it as one is a real risk.

If USCIS sends a Request for Evidence or any other notice, respond by the stated deadline, every time. Keep your receipt notice and copies of everything you submitted. If your request is denied, if you filed late, if you're planning travel while something is pending, or if you suspect any kind of status violation, that's a moment for qualified legal advice, not a general assumption that things will work out.

**Program Extension Is Not the Same as Immigration Extension**

Three different actions, three different owners, three different effects. Keep them separate in your own head, because the consequences of confusing them fall on you, not on whoever explained it wrong.

UPDATING YOUR PROGRAM END DATE
Who controls it: your DSO or school, through SEVIS
What it changes: your academic and SEVIS program information
What it does not automatically change: it may not, by itself, extend your Form I-94 admission period

FILING AN EXTENSION OF STAY
Who controls it: you, through the applicable USCIS process
What it changes: requests additional authorised stay
What it does not automatically change: it does not guarantee approval

RECEIVING USCIS APPROVAL
Who controls it: USCIS
What it changes: extends or changes your authorised period as approved
What it does not automatically change: it does not replace your continued obligation to comply with school and F-1 requirements

A longer program on paper and a longer authorised stay in fact are two different things. Only the third row actually gives you more time in the United States.

**Finishing Early**

Finishing sooner than planned sounds like good news, and it can be, but it changes several things you need to actively manage rather than ignore.

What's affected: your program end date, your SEVIS record, your departure period timing, employment planning if you had any authorised work, any travel you'd planned, and any future program plans like a graduate pathway.

Report early completion to your DSO as soon as it's clear you'll finish ahead of schedule. Don't assume you can simply remain in the United States until your original Form I-20 or I-94 date without confirming the actual consequences with your DSO first: the departure clock generally starts from your actual completion date, not your originally planned one.

**Needing More Time to Finish**

Plenty of legitimate reasons can push your completion date later, and none of them are a personal failure.

Common reasons: academic difficulty, an approved program change, medical issues, injury, a properly authorised reduced course load, transfer complications, program restructuring, or delayed required coursework.

One thing this handbook will state plainly: athletic participation, an injury, or a coaching decision does not, on its own, justify an immigration extension. Those may be real, understandable reasons your academic timeline shifted, but the immigration consequence still has to go through the proper process covered in this module, not an assumption that a football-related reason gets automatic accommodation.

Contact your DSO the moment a delay becomes likely, not after your Form I-94 deadline has already passed. The earlier this conversation happens, the more options are generally available.

**The New 30-Day Departure Period**

Under the rule effective September 15, 2026, the standard departure period after your authorised stay ends is generally 30 days, shorter than the 60-day period that applied under the old duration-of-status system.

Before it ends, generally complete:
• Any final travel planning
• Closing out housing arrangements
• Wrapping up banking and phone arrangements
• Retaining copies of every relevant record, covered in full later in this module
• Keeping evidence of your actual departure date
• Confirming any transfer or authorised next step, if applicable, before you assume you're simply done

Do not assume every student gets the same departure period. Students transitioning from the previous duration-of-status system may have different treatment, covered in the next section. Don't apply this 30-day figure to your own situation without confirming which category you fall into.

**If You Were Already Admitted Under Duration of Status**

If you're already in valid F-1 duration-of-status on September 15, 2026, specific transition rules apply to you, and they are not as simple as 'you just stay under D/S.' That statement is inaccurate, and this handbook won't repeat it.

Generally, your authorised period becomes tied to whichever comes first: the program end date on the Form I-20 that was valid on the effective date, or four years from September 15, 2026, subject to the full transition provisions and any applicable exceptions.

Other transition details that matter:
• Some students in this transition group may retain the former 60-day departure period rather than the new 30-day period
• If you travel abroad and are readmitted after the effective date, you will generally be placed onto the new fixed-period system on that readmission, even if you started under D/S
• OPT and STEM OPT applicants or participants may have their own specific transition treatment, separate from the general rule above

Your own transition position depends on your specific dates and circumstances. Confirm it directly with your DSO and, where the details are unclear, qualified immigration counsel, rather than applying a general rule to your own case without checking.

**How Travel and Readmission Affect Your Fixed Admission Date**

Leaving the country, even briefly, can reset the clock this whole module is about. Review before every trip, not just your first one:

• Passport validity
• Visa validity
• Your current Form I-20, including whether a travel signature is needed from your DSO
• Your most recent Form I-94
• Your current SEVIS status
• Any pending USCIS application
• Employment documentation, if relevant
• Realistic re-entry timing against your school calendar
• The possibility that you'll receive a new, fixed-period admission on your return, even if your prior admission worked differently

If you have a pending Extension of Stay or any unresolved status issue, do not treat this section as general reassurance that travel will be fine. That combination is exactly the kind of student-specific, potentially legal question that needs your DSO's and, where appropriate, qualified counsel's direct input before you book anything.

**OPT, STEM OPT, and This Rule**

This is an introduction, not a complete guide. Full post-graduation career and work-authorisation content belongs in a later section.

What's worth knowing now: Optional Practical Training (OPT) and STEM OPT have their own separate eligibility rules and application processes, distinct from everything else in this module. Your employment-authorisation dates, if you eventually have them, are their own separate dates, distinct from your visa, your Form I-20, and your Form I-94.

The fixed-period rule covered throughout this module may interact with your authorised stay and, if you're in the transition population, your transition status, in ways that depend on your specific timeline. Having a pending or approved employment authorisation does not remove any of your other compliance obligations covered elsewhere in this module.

Some students may need an Extension of Stay or another action depending on exactly how their OPT timeline lines up with their admission period. Confirm current procedures directly with your DSO and official USCIS guidance when this becomes relevant to you, rather than relying on this introduction alone.

**Employment Restrictions, Including While an EOS Is Pending**

Do not assume any income-generating activity is permitted just because:

• The NCAA allows it
• Your school allows it
• A coach approves it
• A sponsor offers it
• The activity happens online
• The payment is deposited into an Australian account
• You or someone else calls it NIL rather than employment
• It's framed as a gift, a prize, reimbursement, or sponsorship rather than payment
• It happens during a school break

None of those framings changes what your immigration status actually permits. Athletic rules, immigration rules, tax rules, school rules, and any relevant state rules can all apply separately to the same activity, and satisfying one does not mean you've satisfied the others.

Before accepting any compensation of any kind, consult your DSO, your athletic compliance office, qualified immigration counsel, and a qualified tax professional where relevant. This handbook will not tell you a specific activity is fine. That determination is genuinely individual, and getting it wrong can affect your immigration status, not just your bank account.

**Attendance and Full-Time Course-Load Requirements**

Maintaining status depends on more than watching your I-94 date; it depends on what you actually do academically day to day.

What generally applies: you're expected to maintain full-time enrollment and attend as required. Dropping a course needs proper authorisation beforehand, not a decision made and reported after the fact. A reduced course load is sometimes available for specific medical or academic reasons, but it generally has to go through your DSO and be properly authorised, not simply decided on your own.

If you're struggling academically, having a medical issue, considering a program change, or thinking about a transfer, raise it with your DSO early. Athletic participation does not excuse academic or immigration noncompliance; a demanding training or travel schedule is a real scheduling challenge to solve with your school, not a reason your enrollment requirements are waived.

Institution-specific procedures for all of the above vary by school. Confirm your school's exact process rather than assuming a generic one applies.

**Reporting Changes: Records and Protection**

Report changes to your DSO promptly, not when it's convenient. This list covers what generally needs reporting: address, phone number, email, program, major, school, funding source, sponsor, legal name, passport, visa, Form I-20 details, employment, travel plans, graduation date, and any early or delayed completion. Lost or stolen documents belong on this list too, and belong there immediately, not after you've tried to solve it yourself.

Keep your own records, independent of what your school keeps:
• A secure cloud copy of everything
• A printed backup
• Copies sent to a trusted parent or guardian, so a second person has them too
• Every USCIS notice you ever receive
• Every prior Form I-20, not just your current one
• Every prior Form I-94
• Your visa records
• Your SEVIS fee receipt
• Relevant school correspondence
• Travel records
• Employment documents, if any

The pattern behind this whole list: nothing here should exist in only one place, and nothing here should exist only in your own memory of what happened.

## Section 4: Arrive, Check In, and Set Up Your Life in the United States

### Module 14 — Complete Your Required Arrival and Check-In Steps

Arriving in the United States is not the finish line. Leaving the airport or your port of entry means your visa and CBP admission are behind you, but your school, immigration, housing, and athletics processes are still ahead of you, and several of them run on their own deadlines. This module walks through your school's check-in process, what your DSO needs from you, orientation, and what to do if your arrival doesn't go to plan.

**Your Arrival Checklist** *(timeline)*

Arriving in the United States is not the finish line, it's the start of a short, busy stretch where a handful of specific actions matter. This checklist orients you to what needs to happen in your first 24 hours, your first week, and the days immediately after, without repeating the detail covered in the sections that follow.

First 24 hours:
• Reach your school or approved housing safely, using the transport arrangement you confirmed before you travelled
• Confirm your safe arrival with a parent or guardian
• Save your school's key contacts (DSO, housing, coach) and local emergency numbers in your phone

First week:
• Confirm your school's own check-in deadline, which may be earlier than any federal timeframe
• Contact your DSO or international student office as your school instructs
• Attend required orientation sessions
• Meet your academic advisor, coach, and any other relevant school contacts

Ongoing from day one:
• Keep your passport, visa, Form I-20, and any arrival paperwork somewhere secure and easy to find
• If your plans change (a delay, a different arrival date, a housing issue) tell your school as early as possible rather than waiting

This checklist only covers arrival and check-in. Housing and campus life, health and medical clearance, emergency contacts, and banking and everyday setup each get their own module later in this section.

Steps:
1. Reach Your Housing or Accommodation Safely
2. Confirm Arrival With Your Parents or Guardian
3. Save Your School and Emergency Contacts
4. Confirm Your School's Check-In Deadline
5. Contact Your DSO or International Office
6. Attend Required Orientation
7. Meet Your Academic, Housing, and Athletics Contacts
8. Secure Your Passport, Visa, I-20, and Arrival Records
9. Tell Your School Early About Any Changes

**What Parents Should Confirm**

The days right after your student arrives are when a short, specific check-in matters more than ongoing monitoring. This section gives parents and guardians a practical list to work through with their student, not a reason to take over.

Worth confirming together:
• Your student has arrived safely and reached their expected housing
• Your student has a working phone number and a reliable way to stay in touch
• Your student knows their school's check-in deadline and what it involves
• Your student knows how to contact their DSO, housing office, coach, athletic trainer, and campus safety
• Your student has access to their passport, visa, and Form I-20, and knows where they're kept
• Your student has a plan for orientation and any early appointments
• You have the school's own emergency and international-office contact details on hand, not just your student's number

This is a conversation, not a checklist to run without your student. The goal is that your student feels supported, not supervised, and that you both know who to call if something doesn't go to plan.

**Confirm Your School's Own Arrival Deadline**

'Arrival deadline' can mean several different things, set by several different offices, and assuming they're all the same date is a common and avoidable mistake.

A single school might have separate deadlines for:
• Housing move-in
• International student check-in
• Orientation
• Program or semester start
• Team reporting date
• Athletic medical clearance

These can come from different offices entirely: international student services, housing, the registrar, and athletics don't always coordinate their dates, and meeting one doesn't automatically mean you've met another.

For each deadline that applies to you, confirm directly with the office that owns it:
• The exact date and time
• The physical location, or whether it can be completed online
• What documents you need to bring
• Who to contact if you have a question or a problem

Do not assume a single 'arrival day' covers everything. Treat each deadline as its own requirement until you've confirmed otherwise.

**Complete Your School Check-In So Your DSO Can Register You**

Your school's check-in process and your SEVIS record are connected, but they are not the same thing, and understanding the difference helps you take the right action at the right time.

Your DSO, or Designated School Official, is the staff member at your school authorised to manage your immigration record. Federal rules require your DSO to register eligible students in SEVIS, the government's system for tracking international student status, generally within 30 days of your program's start date. That is your DSO's own compliance obligation to the government, not a personal grace period for you.

Your school sets its own student-facing check-in process, and that process is what you actually need to follow. It exists so your DSO has the information and documents required to complete your registration, and your school's own deadline for it can fall well before any federal timeframe. Complete it as soon as your school allows rather than treating any later date as safe to wait for.

In short: you follow your school's check-in instructions and deadline; your DSO then uses what you provide to meet their own separate SEVIS registration obligation. Confirm your specific school's process directly, since the exact steps vary by institution. This is separate from the broader immigration timeline covered in Section 3, which explains how your status is maintained over the rest of your time in the United States.

Separately, federal regulation also sets a reporting deadline that applies to you directly, not just to your DSO: initial F-1 and M-1 students should report to their DSO as soon as possible after being admitted to the United States, and no later than the Initial Session Start Date listed in SEVIS (8 CFR 214.3(g)(iii)(C)). If a DSO cannot confirm that you reported, SEVIS can automatically terminate your record as a no-show. Your school's own check-in deadline may fall earlier than this federal baseline, but it cannot fall later, so follow whichever deadline comes first.

**What Your DSO Needs From You**

Your DSO can only complete your check-in and registration with accurate, current information from you. Schools vary in exactly how they collect it, but the categories below are commonly requested.

Your school may ask for:
• Your passport
• Your F-1 visa, where applicable
• Your Form I-20
• Details of your travel and entry to the United States
• Your current U.S. address
• Up-to-date contact information
• Confirmation of your enrollment
• Any school-specific check-in form

This list is a general guide, not a controlling checklist. Use your school's own official check-in instructions as the final word on exactly what's required and in what format, and ask your DSO directly if anything on their list is unclear.

**Attending International Student Orientation**

International student orientation exists to cover the practical and compliance information you need before classes and training begin, and attending it is usually treated as part of your check-in, not an optional extra.

Typical topics include:
• Maintaining your immigration status
• Campus resources and how to use them
• Academic expectations and registration
• Health, safety, and emergency information
• Introductions to key offices and staff

International student orientation, general new-student orientation, and athletics orientation are usually three separate sessions run by three separate offices. Attending one does not satisfy the requirement to attend the others, even if the content overlaps.

Before you go, confirm what to bring (often your passport, visa, and Form I-20), and come with a short list of questions about anything you're unsure of, from check-in steps to campus logistics. If your orientation schedule conflicts with a team or academic commitment, raise it with your international student office and your coach as early as possible rather than choosing one and hoping the other works itself out. The exact format, length, and content of orientation is set entirely by your school.

**What to Do If You Arrive Late**

Travel plans change. What matters most if yours does is acting early and communicating clearly, not the delay itself.

If you're going to arrive later than planned:
1. Contact your DSO or international student office as soon as you know
2. Contact your housing office and athletics staff if the delay affects either
3. Explain the reason and your expected arrival date accurately, don't guess or minimise
4. Ask what documentation or approval, if any, your school needs from you
5. Follow your school's written instructions exactly
6. Keep a record of every email, message, or call about the change

A delay caused by an airline, a family circumstance, or something outside your control does not automatically resolve any school or immigration question on its own. Your DSO is generally required to have authorised any change to your travel plans in advance where your immigration record is concerned, so contacting them before the fact, not after, gives you the best chance of a smooth outcome.

**If You Miss Your Required Check-In**

Missing your school's required check-in is serious and needs an immediate response, not because every missed check-in ends the same way, but because the possible consequences can include your immigration status.

If this happens to you:
• Contact your DSO immediately, in person or by whatever method your school specifies
• Do not rely on advice from friends, teammates, coaches, online forums, or anyone other than your DSO or qualified immigration counsel
• Follow your school's written instructions exactly
• If your DSO advises that your status may be affected, or if you're unsure, contact a qualified U.S. immigration attorney
• Keep every document and message related to what happened

This handbook cannot tell you what a missed check-in means for your specific situation, and no one, including your DSO, a coach, a teammate, or Rep1, can promise a particular outcome or that a missed check-in will be resolved a certain way. Your DSO is the right first contact, and qualified immigration counsel is the right source for anything touching your legal status. For how your status is maintained more broadly after check-in, see Section 3's coverage of your immigration timeline.

**Common Mistakes**

Watch for these, they account for most of the avoidable problems Australian student-athletes run into during arrival and check-in.

• Assuming your visa or CBP entry stamp completes your school check-in; it doesn't, they're separate processes
• Waiting until a federal deadline feels close, instead of following your school's own, usually earlier, deadline
• Following only your athletics schedule and missing an international-office requirement
• Assuming your coach or athletics department has handled your international student check-in for you; they generally haven't
• Missing an email or student portal message with check-in instructions because you weren't checking it yet
• Giving your school an old address or phone number instead of updating it on arrival
• Not reporting a travel delay or changed arrival date to your DSO
• Arriving after your authorised or school-approved date without contacting your DSO first
• Giving your parents an incomplete or outdated set of contact details
• Acting on informal advice instead of your school's official written instructions

The common thread: treat your school's official instructions as the source of truth, and contact the right office early whenever something doesn't go to plan. If something about a school contact or situation feels wrong beyond a simple compliance question, the trusted-adult approach from Section 1 still applies here, in a new country.

**Key Takeaways**

Follow your school's own check-in and arrival deadlines rather than any single federal timeframe, since your school's dates are often earlier and are what actually governs your day-to-day requirements. Your DSO registers you in SEVIS using the information you provide at check-in, a separate administrative step from anything you personally need to track. If your plans change or you arrive late, contact your DSO immediately rather than waiting. If you miss a required check-in, contact your DSO and, where advised, qualified immigration counsel, without assuming a particular outcome.

**Next Action**

Locate your school's official arrival and check-in instructions, generally available from your international student office or DSO, and save your DSO's name and contact details before you need them.

### Module 15 — Move Into Housing and Start Campus Life

Getting settled means more than moving your bags into a room. Before the semester properly starts, you'll need to understand your housing terms, meet the people you'll rely on all year, from your advisor to your coach, and know how to get help if something goes wrong. This module walks through housing, your first meetings on campus, and campus safety basics.

**Housing Options Overview**

Most schools offer more than one type of student housing, and the categories below cover what you're likely to encounter, though not every school offers every option.

Common categories:
• Residence hall or dormitory, run by the general housing office
• Athletics-associated housing, which is often still managed by the general housing office rather than the athletics department
• Other school-arranged housing, such as apartment-style residence communities
• Off-campus apartments or shared housing, arranged independently
• Host-family or temporary housing, at some schools, particularly early in a program

Before assuming 'athletics housing' means the athletics department runs it, or that a certain option is automatically available to you, check directly with your housing office. Cost, meal plans, contract length, transportation, furnishings, and eligibility all vary by school and by housing type.

When comparing options, weigh:
• Location relative to classes, training, and facilities
• Contract length and what happens at the end of it
• Total cost, including anything not obviously included
• Meal access
• Transportation to and from campus
• What's furnished versus what you need to bring
• Roommate arrangements
• Access during school breaks
• Move-in date

No housing type is universally the best choice. Your official housing offer and contract from your school are what actually apply to you, not any general description here.

**Confirm Whether Housing Is Required**

Whether you're required to live in campus housing isn't a single, simple rule, it depends on several things that vary by school.

Factors that can affect this include:
• Whether you're a first-year student
• Your age
• Any scholarship or athletics housing arrangement
• The school's general campus residency policy
• Your specific program type
• Availability at the time you enrol
• Whether the school approves individual exceptions

Don't assume that because a rule applies at one school, or to students generally, it applies to you. Confirm directly with your housing office:
• Whether campus housing is mandatory for you specifically
• Whether an exemption exists, and what qualifies for one
• Who has the authority to approve an exemption
• Any relevant deadlines
• Deposit amounts and cancellation terms
• What happens, financially and otherwise, if you sign a contract and then need to change your plans

A housing contract is a binding agreement, and this handbook isn't the place for legal advice about one. If a contract term is unclear or feels like it doesn't fit your situation, ask your housing office directly before signing, not after.

**The Housing Move-In Process**

Move-in day goes more smoothly when you know what to expect, even though the exact sequence differs from school to school.

Before move-in:
• Confirm your move-in date, time, location, and whether an appointment or time slot is required
• Confirm what identification and documents you need to bring
• Confirm your room assignment and any roommate information you've been given
• Confirm what's supplied in the room and what you need to bring yourself
• Save your housing office's contact details, including an after-hours number if one exists

During move-in:
• Collect your keys or access credentials
• Obtain or confirm your campus ID card, if that happens at the same time
• Inspect your room before unpacking
• Document any existing damage, ideally with photos, before you move your things in
• Learn the building's fire, evacuation, guest, and access rules
• Locate your resident assistant or housing contact for the building

After move-in:
• Test locks, internet, and essential utilities and facilities
• Report any damage or missing items promptly, using your school's official process
• Confirm how you'll receive mail and packages at your new address
• Review emergency procedures for your building
• Keep copies of anything you sign or submit

Not every school follows this exact sequence, and some steps may happen in a different order or be combined. Use your school's own move-in instructions as the final word.

**Roommates and Dorm Life**

Sharing a room or a living space for the first time, in a new country, is an adjustment, and a short conversation early on prevents most of the problems that come up later.

Worth agreeing on early:
• Quiet hours and sleep schedules, including around early training sessions
• Cleaning and shared chores
• Guests, and how much notice matters
• Food and shared property, including the basics of what's off-limits without asking
• Privacy expectations
• How training and competition schedules affect the room

Cultural differences in what's considered normal are common between international and domestic students, and between students from different backgrounds generally. A habit that feels perfectly normal to you might genuinely surprise your roommate, and the reverse is just as true. Raise it directly and early, calmly, rather than letting it build into resentment.

A simple early conversation checklist:
• What time do you usually sleep and wake?
• How do you feel about guests, and with how much notice?
• Who's responsible for what when it comes to cleaning?
• Is there anything from home you'd like me to know about?

If something isn't working and a direct conversation hasn't resolved it, your resident assistant or housing office has a process for this. Use it before a small issue becomes a serious one. The trusted-adult approach from Section 1 still applies here: if something feels more serious than an ordinary roommate disagreement, you don't have to sort it out alone.

**Meeting Your Advisor and DSO**

Your academic advisor, your DSO, and any athletics academic support you have access to are different roles, and while they can overlap at some schools, don't assume they do at yours.

Your academic advisor typically helps with:
• Degree and program requirements
• Course planning
• Registration
• Academic resources on campus

Your DSO, or a school's broader international office, typically helps with:
• F-1 and SEVIS-related school processes
• Updating your address and contact information in your student record
• Travel-signature guidance on your Form I-20
• Enrollment and program questions that fall within their role

Athletics academic support, where your school has it, typically helps with:
• Balancing coursework with training and competition travel
• Athletics-specific study support and tutoring
• Referrals to your compliance office or your general academic advisor when something falls outside their role

None of these people can resolve an immigration-status issue on their own beyond their role. Your DSO handles your school's part of the process; anything touching your actual legal status, as covered in Module 14, still goes through your DSO and, where advised, qualified immigration counsel.

Useful questions for your first meeting with each:
• What should I come to you for, and what should I take somewhere else?
• How do I reach you, and how quickly should I expect a response?
• Is there anything specific to international or athlete students I should know?
• Who do you work with when something falls outside your role?

**Meeting Your Coaches and Team**

Your first meeting with your coaching staff and team sets the tone for the semester, and knowing what's typically covered helps you show up prepared.

Common first-meeting topics:
• Reporting location and time
• Training schedule
• Team communication tools and expectations
• Equipment issue
• Facility access
• General team expectations and rules
• Who handles medical clearance
• Who handles eligibility and compliance questions
• How to raise an academic schedule conflict
• Transportation to practice, training, and competition

Your coaching staff sets team direction, training expectations, and team culture. What they are not is the final authority on immigration, medical clearance, insurance, or eligibility decisions, even when they're personally helpful and well-intentioned. Those areas belong to specific offices:
• Your athletic trainer or sports medicine staff handle medical clearance, covered fully in Module 16
• Your athletics compliance office handles governing-body and institutional compliance
• Your DSO handles school immigration-process questions, covered in Module 14

If a coach offers to help with one of these, that's often genuinely useful as a connector, but confirm the actual requirement with the office that owns it, not just with your coach.

**Campus Safety Orientation**

Knowing how to reach help before you need it matters more than knowing after, and a few minutes early in the semester covers most of what you'll need.

Locate and save:
• 911, for immediate danger or a life-threatening emergency
• Your campus safety or campus police office
• Your residence life after-hours contact number
• Your school's emergency alert system
• A safe-ride or escort service, if your school offers one
• Evacuation information for your building
• Severe-weather instructions for your campus
• How to report a concern, safety or otherwise

U.S. colleges and universities that receive federal funding are generally required to maintain a campus emergency notification system and issue timely warnings about significant safety threats, under the Clery Act. What that looks like in practice, text alerts, an app, email, siren systems, varies significantly by school, so confirm how yours works and enrol in it directly rather than assuming you're automatically included.

Practical actions:
• Enrol in your school's official emergency alert system
• Save your campus address and campus safety number in your phone
• Learn your nearest safe location or building
• Know how to request a welfare check or a safety escort if your school offers one
• Use 911 for immediate danger, always

This section is your starting point. Module 17 covers emergency and support contacts in full, including medical emergencies and who to contact for different kinds of problems; this section won't repeat that detail.

**Common Mistakes**

Watch for these, they cover most of the avoidable problems that come up around housing and campus arrival.

• Assuming housing is automatically included with admission or with an athletics offer
• Signing a housing contract without checking the dates, total cost, and cancellation terms
• Missing a deposit deadline or a move-in appointment
• Arriving without knowing where or how to collect your keys
• Not documenting existing room damage before moving in
• Assuming your coach controls your housing arrangement
• Letting a roommate concern build for weeks instead of raising it early
• Missing orientation because it overlapped with another school or team event, without telling anyone in advance
• Mixing up your academic advisor, DSO, coach, athletic trainer, and compliance office, and going to the wrong one
• Not saving after-hours contact details until you actually need them
• Assuming every campus offers the same safety, transport, or support services as another school you've heard about

The common thread: confirm directly with the office that owns each process, save contacts before you need them, and raise problems early rather than waiting for them to grow.

**Key Takeaways**

Confirm your housing terms, whether it's required, the cost, and the contract details, directly with your housing office rather than assuming. Document your room and save your school's key contacts as soon as you move in. Understand that your academic advisor, DSO, coach, athletic trainer, and compliance office each have a different role, and use the right one for the right problem. Learn your campus safety procedures immediately, don't wait until you need them.

**Next Action**

Save the official contact details for your housing office, your DSO or international office, your academic advisor, your athletics contact, and campus safety, before your first week is over.

### Module 16 — Set Up Your Health Coverage and Medical Records

Getting healthcare, insurance, and athletic medical clearance sorted is connected work, but it's not one single task. General health coverage, athletic injury coverage, and medical clearance to practice are three separate processes that happen to overlap, and each has its own office, its own paperwork, and its own deadline. This module walks through all three, plus the everyday basics of finding care and looking after your mental health.

**Why Health Setup Can't Wait**

Health preparation is easy to postpone because nothing feels urgent yet, but several pieces genuinely need to be in place before you need them, not after.

A few realities worth planning around:
• Medical care in the United States can be expensive, and cost is a real factor in decisions you might otherwise put off.
• Insurance generally has to be active before it applies. It isn't something you can activate retroactively once you're already unwell or injured.
• Athletic participation usually requires a separate medical clearance process, on top of any general health insurance you have.
• Ongoing prescriptions may need planning ahead of time, since not every medication or brand transfers directly to a U.S. pharmacy.
• Immunization records may be required before you can move into housing, register for classes, or start training.

These are four genuinely separate things, easy to blur together:
• General healthcare access: being able to see a doctor or go to a pharmacy at all.
• Student health insurance: the general medical coverage most schools require or offer.
• Athletic injury coverage: coverage specific to injuries from training or competition, on top of your general insurance.
• Athletic medical clearance: the school's or governing body's process for confirming you're medically fit to practice or compete.

Waiting until you're actually sick, injured, or in crisis to work out how any of this functions is the single most avoidable problem in this module. None of it takes long to set up properly if you start early.

**How U.S. Healthcare Differs From Medicare**

The U.S. healthcare system works differently to Medicare, and a few core terms will come up constantly once you arrive.

Key terms:
• Premium: what you or your school pays regularly to keep an insurance plan active, whether or not you use it.
• Deductible: the amount you pay out of pocket for covered care each year before your plan starts paying its share.
• Copay: a fixed amount you pay for a specific service, such as a doctor's visit.
• Coinsurance: a percentage of a covered service's cost that you pay after you've met your deductible.
• Network: the group of doctors, clinics, and hospitals your insurance plan has an agreement with. Using an in-network provider is normally far cheaper than an out-of-network one.
• Primary-care provider: the doctor or clinic you'd normally see first for non-emergency care.
• Urgent care: a clinic for problems that need same-day attention but aren't life-threatening.
• Emergency room: for genuine emergencies. Emergency treatment and how it gets paid for are two separate questions, covered below.
• Pharmacy: where prescriptions are filled. Not every medication or brand name is the same as in Australia.
• Claim: the request your provider or insurer processes to pay for care you received.
• Explanation of benefits: the statement your insurer sends explaining what was billed, what it paid, and what you may owe. It usually isn't a bill itself.

A few things worth being clear on early:
• Medicare Australia generally does not operate as your health insurance in the United States. Don't assume it covers anything once you're enrolled at a U.S. school.
• Reciprocal healthcare arrangements should not be assumed to apply. Confirm your actual coverage rather than relying on what might apply in a different country.
• School insurance plans vary significantly from one institution to another.
• Having insurance does not mean every service is free. Deductibles, copays, and coinsurance still apply depending on your plan.
• Whether the emergency room will treat you and whether your insurance will pay for it are related but separate questions. Always seek emergency care for a genuine emergency regardless of insurance status, and sort out payment afterward.

Confirm exact terms, costs, and coverage with your school's student insurance office. This section explains the vocabulary, not your specific plan.

**Gather Your Health Records**

Bring your health information with you rather than trying to reconstruct it from Australia later.

Worth gathering before you leave:
• Vaccination and immunization history
• A general medical history summary
• A current prescription list, including generic (non-brand) medication names
• Known allergies
• Previous injuries relevant to your sport
• Surgery history
• Relevant imaging or specialist reports
• Dental and vision prescriptions
• Emergency contact details
• Insurance information, if arranged in advance
• Any athletic medical forms your school or governing body has already sent you
• English translations of any records not already in English

Make copies, digital and physical, and don't carry the only copy of anything important. Leave a set with a parent or trusted adult who can send it again if something is lost or left behind.

If you take prescription medication, check the applicable travel and customs rules for bringing it into the United States before you pack it. Module 12 covers medication and travel document preparation in more detail; this section is about the records themselves, not the trip.

**Activate Your Student Health Coverage**

Most U.S. schools require some form of student health insurance, but the exact rules vary enough that you need to confirm them directly rather than assume.

Confirm with your school's student insurance office:
• Whether the school requires you to be on its student health plan
• Whether you can waive the school plan using outside coverage, and what that coverage must include to qualify
• The plan's effective date and expiration date
• Which providers and facilities are in-network
• Deductible and copay amounts
• Whether prescriptions are covered, and how
• Whether mental-health care is covered
• How emergency care is handled
• Whether sports-related injuries are excluded or handled separately
• How to get your insurance card
• How to contact the insurer directly

A few things to keep in mind:
• Waiver approval isn't automatic. If you want to use outside coverage instead of the school plan, expect a process, a deadline, and specific documentation requirements.
• Travel insurance is generally not the same as a school-approved health plan and often won't satisfy the school's requirement, even if it was useful for your trip over.
• Athletic injury coverage is a separate topic, covered next. General student health insurance and athletic injury coverage don't automatically cover the same things.

This handbook doesn't recommend a specific insurer. Your school's student insurance office can tell you exactly what applies to you.

**Confirm Your Athletic Injury Coverage**

Athletic injury coverage is separate from your general student health insurance, and understanding how the two interact matters before you're actually injured.

Confirm with your athletics compliance office, athletic trainer, or sports medicine staff:
• Which insurance responds first if you're injured, your own or your parents' insurance, the school plan, or an athletics-specific policy
• Whether athletics-related injuries are covered at all under your general plan
• Whether the school carries secondary, excess, or catastrophic coverage on top of primary insurance
• Who remains responsible for deductibles or costs not covered
• Who actually submits claims, you or the school
• Whether pre-authorization or network rules apply to athletic injury treatment
• What documentation is required
• Whether coverage applies during training, competition, travel, and off-season activity, or only some of these

What's confirmed about governing-body frameworks, current as of this research:
• NCAA member institutions must certify that student-athletes have coverage for athletically related medical expenses within the NCAA Catastrophic Injury Insurance Program deductible, currently $90,000, which must be met within 24 months of the date of injury. That deductible can be satisfied through a student's or parent's personal insurance, a basic accident policy the institution maintains, or the institution's own self-insurance arrangement. It is not automatically paid by the NCAA itself below that threshold.
• NAIA membership requires participation in NAIA's catastrophic injury insurance program as a condition of membership, covering varsity and junior varsity athletes; specific deductible and premium arrangements are administered through the NAIA's insurance program and vary by year and sport.
• NJCAA requires member schools to carry catastrophic injury coverage for student-athletes. The specific current deductible figure varies by source and wasn't confirmed directly from njcaa.org in this research pass, so no specific dollar figure is stated here. Confirm the current NJCAA catastrophic insurance deductible and how it applies directly with your school's athletics department.

None of these figures are something you or your family are expected to track independently. Ask your athletic trainer or compliance office to walk you through exactly what applies at your specific school before you start training.

**Complete Your Athletic Medical Clearance**

Medical clearance to practice or compete is a distinct process from being admitted, enrolled, or even medically insured, and it has to be completed before you can take part.

Clearance generally involves several separate pieces, though the exact combination varies by school and governing body:
• A pre-participation physical examination
• Medical history review
• Injury history
• Medication disclosure
• Proof of insurance
• Immunization records
• Sickle cell trait documentation, where applicable
• Concussion-history acknowledgments
• Any school-specific forms

Who does what:
• Your coach sets training expectations and team culture, but cannot medically clear you to participate. That authority sits with your athletic trainer, team physician, or another designated healthcare professional.
• Being academically admitted, enrolled, or even eligible under governing-body rules does not equal medical clearance. They are separate processes that happen to run in parallel.
• Your athletic trainer or sports medicine staff typically owns the clearance process day to day. Your compliance office handles the governing-body paperwork side.

What's confirmed about governing-body requirements, current as of this research:
• NCAA member institutions require a medical examination for every student-athlete on initial entry to a sport, following minimum standards drawn from a nationally recognized pre-participation physical examination model. For a student-athlete's initial season of eligibility, the NCAA Sports Medicine Handbook establishes that this exam must occur within six months before the student's first practice, competition, or out-of-season conditioning activity. The NCAA also requires every student-athlete to either provide documented results of a prior sickle cell solubility test or be tested during their pre-participation exam; since August 2022, there is no waiver option for this requirement for incoming or currently enrolled student-athletes.
• NAIA's Official Handbook requires a physical examination by a physician before a student-athlete may participate in any intercollegiate sport, along with written medical clearance before participation. No specific number-of-months timing rule for the exam was located in current NAIA primary sources, so none is stated here. Confirm your school's specific deadline directly.
• NJCAA requires a valid physical examination on file before a student-athlete's first practice of each competing calendar year, and that physical remains valid for 13 months from the date it was administered.

Do not begin practicing until your school confirms your clearance is complete. If a deadline is approaching and something is still outstanding, raise it with your athletic trainer immediately rather than assuming it will sort itself out.

**Confirm Your Immunization Requirements**

Immunization requirements can come from more than one direction at once: state law, school policy, campus housing requirements, and sometimes public-health conditions at the time.

Confirm directly with your school's health center or student health services:
• Which vaccines are required, and which are only recommended
• What documentation format is accepted, including whether an overseas immunization record needs to be translated or independently verified
• The submission deadline, and what happens if you miss it
• Whether there's an exemption process, and what qualifies
• Whether a medical review or additional testing is required for any specific vaccine
• Where and how records need to be submitted or uploaded

There is no single, universal U.S. immunization list that applies to every student at every school. Requirements are set at the state and institutional level and can differ meaningfully between schools, so treat any list you see elsewhere, including from other students, as a starting point for questions rather than as your own requirements.

If you have questions about a specific vaccine or your own immunization history, a healthcare provider, either in Australia before you leave or at your school's health service after you arrive, is the right person to ask. This handbook doesn't provide vaccine recommendations beyond directing you to your school and a healthcare provider.

**Finding a Doctor and Pharmacy**

Knowing where to go for routine and non-emergency care saves real stress later, and it's worth sorting out before you need it.

What to locate:
• Your campus health center
• An in-network primary-care provider
• The nearest urgent care clinic
• The nearest emergency room
• A pharmacy that accepts your insurance
• A dentist
• Vision care
• A mental-health provider, covered further in the next section

Practical steps:
• Search your insurer's official provider directory rather than guessing which providers are in-network.
• Confirm network status before a non-emergency visit, since out-of-network care usually costs significantly more.
• Ask whether an appointment is required, particularly for campus health services.
• Bring photo ID and your insurance card to every appointment.
• If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacy about transferring an existing prescription and whether the same medication is available under a different brand name in the United States.
• Save the after-hours contact number for your campus health service and your insurer.

None of this applies to a genuine emergency. If you need emergency care, get it immediately and sort out insurance and network questions afterward. Module 17 covers emergency contacts and procedures in full.

**Mental Health Resources**

Mental health support is as much a part of health preparation as insurance or physical clearance, and knowing your options before you need them matters.

Where support is typically available:
• Campus counseling services
• Student health services generally
• Athletics mental-health or sports psychology support, where your school offers it
• The 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, available 24 hours a day by call, text, or chat at 988lifeline.org, for anyone experiencing a mental health, suicide, or substance-use crisis
• 911, for immediate danger
• Trusted adults, including the people identified in Section 1's safeguarding content
• Your athletic trainer, who can often point you to the right resource even if support isn't their own role
• Your DSO or resident-life staff, who can also refer you to the right service

A few things worth knowing:
• A coach is not the only route to support, and isn't required to be involved at all if you'd rather not.
• You're able to seek help directly, without needing a referral or anyone else's approval.
• Availability, cost, and confidentiality rules vary by service and by school, so ask directly what applies where you are.
• Rep1 is not an emergency or crisis service. If that ever changes, this handbook will be updated to reflect it, but it currently is not, and 988 or 911 are the right contacts for anything urgent.

If something feels serious, reach out. Waiting until a crisis to figure out where to go is exactly what this section is meant to prevent.

### Module 17 — Know Your Emergency and Safety Resources

Emergencies are already stressful. They get worse when you're also trying to figure out the right number or office in the moment. This module gives you the contacts to save now, a clear sequence for a medical emergency, and a quick-reference guide for who to contact for different kinds of problems, so you're never starting from zero.

**Emergency Numbers You Need Now**

Set this up in the next few minutes, not after something happens.

Universal contacts, the same everywhere in the United States:
• 911: for immediate danger, or a serious medical, fire, or police emergency
• 988: for suicide, mental-health, or substance-use crisis support, by call, text, or chat at 988lifeline.org

School-specific contacts to find and save now:
• Campus safety or campus police
• Residence-life or housing after-hours number
• Student health center
• After-hours nurse line or telehealth service, where your school offers one
• Counseling center
• DSO or international-office emergency contact, where your school offers one
• Athletic trainer or sports medicine staff
• Coach or team operations contact
• Safe-ride or campus escort service, where your school offers one

Not every school offers all of these. An after-hours DSO line, a telehealth service, or a campus escort service exists at some schools and not others; confirm what your specific school actually provides rather than assuming.

Before your first week is over:
• Save every number you can confirm directly in your phone
• Write a physical backup list and keep it somewhere separate from your phone
• Save your full campus and housing address, including building and room number
• Confirm which of these services actually operate after hours and on weekends
• Share the relevant numbers with a parent or guardian so they have them too

911 and 988 work the same way everywhere in the country. Everything else on this list depends on your specific school, so the only way to know for certain is to check directly and save what you find.

**What to Do in a Medical Emergency**

If you're ever in a genuine medical emergency, follow this sequence.

1. Call 911 immediately for a life-threatening or serious medical emergency.
2. Give the dispatcher your exact location. If you're not sure of the address, describe the building, landmark, or nearest cross street.
3. Follow the dispatcher's instructions. They may talk you through basic care until help arrives.
4. Don't move the injured person unless staying in place creates immediate danger.
5. Notify campus safety or residence staff once it's safe and appropriate to do so.
6. For an athletics-related injury, contact your athletic trainer after emergency services are already on the way, not instead of calling 911.
7. Bring or locate identification and insurance information only if doing so doesn't delay getting help.
8. Contact a parent, guardian, or trusted adult once the immediate situation is safe.
9. Keep discharge instructions and any medical paperwork for follow-up care.

A few things worth being direct about:
• Call 911, not your athletic trainer, coach, teammate, or Rep1, during a life-threatening emergency. Those contacts matter, but not as a substitute for emergency services.
• Don't drive yourself during a serious emergency.
• Don't delay calling or getting help because you're unsure how insurance will work. That question gets sorted out afterward, not during.
• Reporting the incident to your school or filing an insurance claim happens after immediate safety is addressed, not before.

Module 16 covers health insurance and medical clearance in full. This section is about the emergency itself, not what happens with your coverage afterward.

**After-Hours Medical Care**

Not every health concern is a 911 emergency, and not every campus health center is open when you need it. Knowing your options ahead of time helps you make a better decision in the moment.

Your general options, in different situations:

Call 911
For immediate danger, serious difficulty breathing, a severe injury, loss of consciousness, heavy bleeding, or anything else that seems life-threatening. If you're genuinely unsure whether something is life-threatening, treat it as one and call.

Go to an emergency room
For a serious condition that needs immediate hospital-level evaluation, but where the situation doesn't require an ambulance. This is a judgment call, and when in doubt, calling 911 or a medical professional for guidance is safer than guessing.

Use urgent care or telehealth
For a problem that needs attention soon but genuinely can't wait for the campus health center to reopen, and doesn't need a hospital. Availability depends on your insurance and what your school and area offer.

Wait for the campus health center
For routine or non-urgent concerns, when it's genuinely safe to wait until regular hours.

This is general guidance, not a way to diagnose your own situation. When you're uncertain whether something is an emergency, contact a qualified medical professional or emergency service rather than relying on this handbook alone.

Before you need any of this:
• Check your school's official after-hours care instructions in advance
• Look up your insurer's official provider network, covered in Module 16
• Confirm hours before you travel to any clinic
• Know your transport options if you don't have a car
• Never delay emergency care to check network status first

What's actually available after hours, whether it's a nurse line, telehealth, an urgent care partnership, or nothing beyond the emergency room, varies by school. Confirm directly with your student health center what your specific options are.

**Know Who to Contact**

The right contact depends entirely on the problem. This section is a quick-reference guide, organised by situation, so you're not guessing in the moment. Where the right answer depends on your specific school, that's noted, confirm it directly rather than assuming this list applies exactly to you.

IMMIGRATION AND SCHOOL STATUS
• Immigration or SEVIS check-in issue: contact your DSO or international office first. Escalate to qualified immigration counsel if there are consequences for your status.
• Missed school arrival or international check-in deadline: contact your DSO first. Escalate to school leadership or counsel if your DSO directs you to.

HOUSING AND CAMPUS LIFE
• Housing or roommate issue: contact your resident assistant, housing office, or residence-life staff first. Escalate to residence-life leadership, or campus safety if safety is involved.
• A parent can't reach you after your expected arrival: your housing office, residence life, or DSO can run a welfare check; that's their process to use, and campus safety can escalate from there.

ATHLETICS AND HEALTH
• Athletic medical-clearance issue: contact your athletic trainer or sports medicine staff first. Escalate to athletics compliance or your designated medical staff.
• Athletic injury that isn't an emergency: contact your athletic trainer. For a serious or life-threatening injury, call 911, don't wait to find your trainer first.
• General health-insurance question: contact your student insurance office or school health office first. Escalate to your insurer directly.
• Non-emergency illness: contact your campus health center, an in-network provider, urgent care, or your school's official after-hours service, whichever applies.
• Medical emergency: call 911.
• Mental-health crisis: call or text 988. If there's also immediate physical danger, call 911.

SAFETY AND REPORTING
• Immediate safety concern: call 911, or contact campus safety, depending on how urgent it is. If in doubt, call 911.
• A concern involving a coach, teammate, another adult, or a school employee: go to a trusted adult or the reporting channel described in Section 1's safeguarding content first. This may escalate to Title IX, athletics compliance, student affairs, campus safety, or law enforcement, depending on what's involved.
• A campus emergency alert: follow the official instructions in the alert itself. Contact campus safety if you have questions afterward.

DOCUMENTS AND EVERYDAY PROBLEMS
• Lost passport: contact your DSO and Australian consular services. Escalate to qualified counsel if it affects your status or travel plans.
• Banking issue: contact your bank first. If it isn't resolved, there are official consumer-protection channels that can help; Module 18 covers this in more detail.
• Transportation issue: contact your school's transport office or the local transit authority.
• Phone or campus-technology issue: contact your carrier or your school's IT help desk.
• You're not sure who owns the problem: your DSO, student affairs, or residence life are all reasonable starting points, and any of them can point you to the right office even if it isn't theirs.

A few role boundaries worth keeping straight:
• Your DSO handles your school's immigration processes. Qualified immigration counsel advises and represents you, but doesn't make government decisions.
• Coaches don't decide medical clearance, insurance, immigration, or eligibility questions. They can point you toward the right office, but the decision sits elsewhere.
• Athletic trainers and sports medicine staff own clearance and sports-injury processes. Athletics compliance owns governing-body and institutional compliance.
• Housing staff own housing operations. Campus safety owns campus safety. 911 owns immediate emergencies.
• Rep1 isn't an emergency service or an escalation contact for any of the situations above. If a problem needs a school office, a healthcare provider, an emergency service, or qualified counsel, go to them directly.

Which office actually handles a given situation can vary by school, especially for anything not listed as a universal number. When this list doesn't match what your school told you directly, follow what your school told you.

**Severe Weather and Campus Alerts**

Severe weather in the United States varies enormously by region, and what you need to know depends heavily on where your school is. Common hazards across different parts of the country include tornadoes, hurricanes, flooding, winter storms, extreme heat, wildfire smoke, and severe thunderstorms. This section isn't a full guide to any one of these; it's about knowing how to get and follow official information when something happens.

Set this up early:
• Enrol in your school's official emergency alert system
• Turn on emergency notifications on your phone
• Save your school's emergency-management page
• Learn your housing building's evacuation routes and shelter locations
• Understand the difference between an instruction to evacuate and an instruction to shelter in place, they call for opposite actions
• Follow official school and local government alerts as your primary source of information
• Keep basic emergency supplies, water, a torch, a portable charger, and any medication you need
• Keep your phone charged, and have a backup power option if that's realistic for you
• Tell a parent or guardian how your school communicates during an emergency, so they know what to expect

The National Weather Service issues a watch when the risk of hazardous weather has increased significantly but its timing or location is still uncertain, giving you time to prepare. It issues a warning when a hazardous event is already happening or is highly likely to happen very soon, meaning it's time to take protective action, not just to stay alert.

A few things to keep in mind:
• Official campus instructions control during an on-campus emergency, follow them over anything else you're hearing.
• Don't leave a safe location to get to practice, class, or a team activity while an active warning is in effect. Coaches don't override official emergency instructions, and shouldn't be asked to.
• Don't rely only on team group chats or social media for emergency information. They're often slower and less accurate than the official alert system.

Module 15 covers general campus safety orientation, including enrolling in your school's alert system. This section focuses specifically on severe weather; if you haven't set up your campus alerts yet, do that first.

**Common Mistakes**

Watch for these, they cover most of the avoidable problems that come up around emergencies and safety.

• Not saving 911, 988, campus safety, and housing contacts before you need them
• Assuming campus safety and 911 are interchangeable in every situation
• Calling a coach instead of emergency services during a genuine emergency
• Waiting for a parent or Rep1 to respond before doing anything during an emergency
• Delaying care because you're unsure how insurance works
• Not knowing your full housing or campus address
• Assuming the health center is open at night
• Using an old or incorrect campus number you found somewhere other than an official source
• Ignoring official emergency alerts because they feel like background noise
• Relying only on team chats or social media for emergency information

Save your contacts today, not the day you need them. That single step prevents most of what's on this list.

**Key Takeaways**

Know when to call 911, and don't hesitate to. Know when and how to use 988 for a mental-health or crisis situation, and that 911 still comes first if there's immediate physical danger. Save your school-specific emergency and after-hours contacts before you need them. Know which office owns which kind of problem, so you go to the right one the first time. Enrol in your school's official emergency alerts and follow them over team chats or social media.

### Module 18 — Set Up Banking, Phone, and Everyday Logistics

Everyday logistics don't feel important until something depends on them, a school message you didn't get, money you can't access, a ride you don't have. This module covers the practical setup that makes the rest of your semester run smoothly: phone, campus technology, transport, shopping, your mailing address, banking, money transfers, identification, and a realistic budget.

**Getting a U.S. Phone Number**

Getting a working U.S. number early makes everything else in this module easier, from opening a bank account to registering for campus services.

Common options:
• A prepaid mobile plan, paid month to month with no long-term contract
• A monthly postpaid plan, usually cheaper per month but often requiring credit history or a deposit
• An eSIM, where your phone and carrier both support it, sometimes lets you add a U.S. number without removing your Australian SIM
• Keeping your Australian number active temporarily, useful for accounts that verify by SMS to that number
• Using a dual-SIM phone, if yours supports it, to run both numbers at once

Compare plans on:

• International calling and texting rates back to Australia

• Whether your phone is compatible and unlocked

• What happens to your number if you cancel or switch

After you get a U.S. number:

• Set up voicemail

**Set Up Your Campus Technology**

Getting locked out of your school email or portal in the first week is more disruptive than it sounds, so set this up properly from day one.

Set up and confirm:
• Your school email account
• Your student portal login
• Multi-factor authentication (MFA)

Exactly what technology your school provides, and how MFA and account recovery work, varies by institution. Confirm the specifics with your school's IT help desk in your first week.

Module 17 covers what to do if you lose phone access during an emergency. This section is about day-to-day account access, not emergencies.

**Getting Around Without a Car**

Common options, depending on your school and area:

• Public transport

**Groceries and Everyday Shopping**

A short first-week shopping trip, planned properly, saves you from a lot of small frustrations later.

Where to shop:

• Pharmacies, for medication and toiletries, also covered in Module 16
• Campus stores, for convenience items, often at a premium

A few practical things that catch people out:

• Check your meal plan's limits if your school has one. It may not cover everything, and using it efficiently takes a bit of planning

A simple first-week essentials list, by category rather than brand:
• Food and water
• Toiletries
• Laundry supplies
• Bedding
• Basic cleaning supplies
• Any medication or pharmacy needs
• Training and recovery basics
• Chargers and power adapters

This is a starting-point list, not a nutrition plan. What you actually need depends on your housing, your meal plan, and your own situation.

**Set Up Your U.S. Mailing Address**

Getting your address right matters more than it sounds like it should. A wrong or inconsistent address can delay a package, a bank card, or an official document.

A few different addresses can apply to you at once:
• Your residential address, where you actually live
• Your mailing address, where you receive mail, which may or may not be the same place
• A campus mailbox, common in residence halls, with its own format and collection process
• A package-delivery address, which sometimes differs from your mail address, particularly for larger items

Schools often format residence-hall addresses in a specific way, with a building name, room number, or box number that has to be exact. Confirm your school's official format rather than guessing from the building's street address alone.

Before you need it:
• Confirm where packages are actually collected, and what identification you need to bring
• Confirm how a replacement bank card, document, or other important item would be delivered to you
• Use your school's exact official address format on every account and form, not your best guess at it

A few things worth being direct about:
• Your campus mailing address may not be the same as your physical residence address. Confirm which one a bank, DMV, insurer, or government office actually wants before you give it to them
• Setting up USPS mail forwarding moves your mail, but it doesn't update your address with your school, the government, your bank, or anyone else. Each organisation needs to be updated separately
• Module 13 covers keeping your address current with your DSO and SEVIS record specifically. That's a separate, immigration-specific update, not something this section repeats

When your address changes, update it directly with your school, your bank, your insurer, and any government office that has it on file. Don't assume one update covers all of them.

**Opening a U.S. Bank Account**

A U.S. bank account makes everyday life considerably easier, but what you'll need to open one depends on the bank, so treat everything here as a general guide, not a checklist that guarantees approval anywhere.

Common account types:
• A checking account, for everyday spending and paying bills
• A savings account, for money you're not spending immediately
• A debit card, linked to your account
• Online and mobile banking, to manage it all remotely

Banks commonly ask for some combination of:
• Your passport
• Your visa or other immigration documents
• Your Form I-20
• A U.S. address
• Student identification
• Foreign tax-identification information, in some cases
• An initial deposit
• A Social Security number or Individual Taxpayer Identification Number, if applicable to your situation

The SSN and ITIN question, clearly:
• A Social Security number is generally issued to people authorised to work in the United States. It is not issued simply so you can enrol in school or open a bank account. If you're an F-1 student, you're not automatically eligible for one, and eligibility depends on things like an actual on-campus job offer, approved CPT, or an OPT work authorisation, confirmed through your DSO.
• An Individual Taxpayer Identification Number, or ITIN, is issued by the IRS for federal tax purposes. It does not authorise you to work, and it isn't a general-purpose identity document. Apply for one only if you actually have a federal tax reason to, not simply as a substitute for an SSN because a form asks for one.
• Bank requirements vary. Some financial institutions may accept a passport, immigration documents, proof of address, and another identifying number without requiring an SSN or ITIN at all. Others may ask for one. There's no single rule that applies to every bank.

Before opening any account, confirm:
• Monthly account fees and how to avoid them
• Any minimum balance requirement
• ATM access, including fee-free networks near your campus
• Overdraft settings, and whether you can turn overdraft coverage off
• Transfer limits, particularly for money coming from Australia
• Whether the account is FDIC-insured, which most U.S. bank deposit accounts are, up to the standard federal insurance limit

Account approval is never guaranteed, and requirements genuinely differ between banks. Never use anyone else's Social Security number, ITIN, or identity to open an account. This handbook doesn't recommend a specific bank.

**Money Transfers From Australia**

Getting money from Australia to your U.S. account sounds simple, but the total cost isn't always obvious from the advertised price.

What actually affects what you receive:
• The exchange rate applied to the transfer
• The transfer fee charged upfront
• Any fee the receiving bank charges on top
• How long the transfer takes to arrive
• Transfer limits, daily or per-transaction
• Identity verification requirements, especially for larger transfers

A genuinely important point: a transfer advertised as 'no fee' or 'zero fee' can still cost more overall if the exchange rate applied is worse than the market rate. Compare the total amount that actually arrives, not just the fee shown upfront.

Before sending or receiving a transfer:
• Double-check the recipient's name and account details exactly. Errors can delay or complicate a transfer significantly
• Use a regulated, established transfer provider or bank
• Keep the receipt and any disclosure documents
• For a first transfer or a new provider, consider testing with a smaller amount first
• Never send money in response to pressure, urgency, or an unexpected request, even one that appears to come from you, your family, or someone official. That pattern is a common scam tactic, covered further in Section 1

U.S. remittance transfers are subject to federal consumer protections requiring providers to disclose the exchange rate, fees, and the amount expected to arrive, along with rights to cancel or resolve errors within set timeframes. This handbook doesn't recommend a specific transfer provider. Compare a few before choosing one, every time, since rates and fees change.

**Getting a Driver's License or State ID**

Whether and how you get a driver's license or state ID depends entirely on which state your school is in, so treat this section as an overview of the questions to ask, not a set of rules that apply everywhere.

A few distinct things, easy to confuse:
• A driver's license, which lets you drive and also usually works as identification
• A state identification card, for people who want official state ID but don't need to drive
• A school identification card, which works for campus purposes but isn't a substitute for either of the above
• REAL ID, a federal security standard some states apply to licenses and ID cards. Ask your state's motor vehicle agency whether REAL ID affects you, particularly if you plan to fly domestically or enter certain federal facilities

What you might be asked for, depending on the state:
• Identity documents
• Immigration documents
• Proof of lawful presence in the United States
• Either a Social Security number or documentation that you're ineligible for one
• Proof of residence in that state
• Documentation from your school
• A knowledge test, a road test, or both, for a license
• Fees

A few things worth being direct about:
• An Australian driver's license doesn't automatically give you an ongoing right to drive in every U.S. state. Some states allow visitors to drive on a valid foreign license for a limited period, sometimes alongside an International Driving Permit; rules genuinely differ by state, so don't assume your Australian license covers you indefinitely.
• Insurance and vehicle registration are separate requirements from the license itself, and apply if you're actually going to drive.
• Even if you don't plan to drive, a state ID can be genuinely useful for everyday identification.

Use your specific state's official motor vehicle agency to confirm exactly what applies to you. This handbook can't tell you your state's rules, since they're genuinely different from state to state, and this isn't legal advice about your individual situation.

• Believing every bank requires a Social Security number
• Applying for an ITIN without an actual federal tax reason to
• Ignoring account fees, minimum balances, or overdraft settings until they cost you
• Comparing transfer fees without comparing the exchange rate, which is often where the real cost is
• Assuming an Australian driver's license is valid indefinitely in every state
• Using another state's DMV checklist instead of your own state's
• Forgetting irregular first-semester costs when budgeting
• Relying on a coach, teammate, or Rep1 to manage everyday logistics for you

None of this is complicated once it's set up. The mistakes above are almost all about timing, doing it in week one instead of waiting until you actually need it and don't have it.

**Key Takeaways**

Set up reliable phone and campus technology access in your first week, including account recovery. Learn your transport options before you actually need them. Know the difference between your mailing address and your residential address, and confirm which one each organisation wants. Open and manage a bank account carefully, understanding that SSN and ITIN requirements aren't universal. Confirm your specific state's driver's license and ID rules directly, since they vary. Build a realistic first-semester budget that accounts for irregular costs.

Confirm and write down: your U.S. phone plan, your school email and MFA setup, your primary transport route, your school's official mailing-address format, what your bank actually requires, and your state's DMV requirements if relevant to you.

## Section 5: Succeed at School, Football, and Life

### Module 19 — Manage Your Academic Workload as a Student-Athlete

Once you're enrolled and training, a new set of rules starts to matter: the ones that keep you eligible to keep playing, and the ones that keep you making real progress toward your degree. Getting accepted and cleared to compete is not the same as staying eligible for every year that follows. This module covers how continuing eligibility actually works, how time-demand rules shape your week, how to track real progress toward your degree, how to build a schedule that holds up, where to find support on campus, and what to do the moment you start falling behind rather than after.

**Continuing Eligibility vs. Initial Eligibility**

Getting cleared to compete as an incoming student-athlete and staying eligible once you're enrolled are two different processes, and it's easy to assume that clearing the first one means you're set for good.

Initial eligibility is a one-time certification, completed before you enrol, confirming you meet your governing body's academic and amateurism requirements to compete as a first-year student-athlete. Module 7 covers that initial certification process in detail. This module picks up from the point you're already enrolled and competing, when a separate process takes over: continuing eligibility, reviewed year to year (and sometimes term to term) based on your actual academic record, not your record from before you arrived.

Continuing eligibility generally looks at things like:
• Whether you're enrolled full-time
• Whether you're making real progress toward a specific degree, not just passing courses
• Your grade point average against your governing body's and institution's minimum standards
• Whether you've used up your eligibility clock or seasons of competition

Who actually makes these calls:
• Your athletics compliance office reviews your record against governing-body and institutional rules and certifies continuing eligibility
• Your academic advisor tracks whether your specific courses count toward your specific degree
• Your coach can be a source of support and can flag concerns early, but does not make the compliance determination

These requirements aren't identical everywhere. They differ by governing body (NCAA, NAIA, NJCAA), by division within a governing body, by your specific institution, and by your individual academic record and degree program. Nobody else's timeline is a reliable guide to yours.

What to do:
• Ask your athletics compliance office directly which continuing-eligibility rules apply to you, and get the answer in writing or by email where you can
• Ask your academic advisor how your current course schedule counts toward your specific degree
• Keep your own written record of the guidance you're given, including dates and who you spoke with

Treat this as a standing check-in, not a one-time task. Your courses, your record, and sometimes the rules themselves can all change.

**Understand Your Time-Demand Limits**

Not every hour you spend on football counts the same way, and the rules about how much time your sport can require of you are set by your governing body and division, not by any one coach or program.

A few categories worth knowing:
• Countable athletically related activities: practice, competition, required weight training and conditioning, and similar activities that count toward your governing body's own weekly time limits
• Required but non-countable activities: things like compliance meetings, which may still be mandatory without counting toward the same hourly limit
• Genuinely voluntary activities: activities that aren't supposed to be required, monitored for attendance, or penalized if you skip them, though it's worth confirming a 'voluntary' activity is actually treated that way at your school
• Travel, film review, meetings, and treatment or recovery time: these can be classified differently depending on your specific governing body's rules, so don't assume they're automatically countable or automatically excluded

Governing bodies and divisions set genuinely different limits from one another. NCAA Division I and NCAA Division II apply different weekly limits to each other, and NAIA and NJCAA each set their own separate time-demand rules again, distinct from the NCAA's. Because these figures and their exact definitions are periodically reviewed and updated, this handbook doesn't state a specific number here. The right number for you depends entirely on your governing body, division, and sport, and can change.

What this means for you:
• Ask your athletics compliance office directly what the current time-demand limit is for your governing body, division, and sport
• Ask specifically how travel, film, meetings, and treatment time are classified at your school
• If your actual week consistently looks heavier than what compliance describes, or a demand feels inconsistent with the rules you were given, write down the specific dates, activities, and hours involved before you raise it

Time-demand rules exist to protect your ability to actually be a student, not just an athlete. Knowing where the lines are, and asking compliance for the current number rather than guessing, is what lets you notice if something's off.

**Staying on Track Toward Your Degree**

Academic eligibility and simply passing your classes are related, but they're not the same thing. What actually matters for continuing eligibility is real progress toward a specific, declared degree, not just an acceptable GPA in whatever courses you happened to take.

NCAA student-athletes are subject to a progress-toward-degree framework (NCAA Bylaw 14.4.3.2) that requires completing a designated share of your specific degree's requirements by set points across your years of eligibility, on top of minimum grade-point and credit-hour benchmarks each year. This is an NCAA-specific rule; NAIA and NJCAA institutions set their own progress and standing requirements, which shouldn't be assumed to match. Your own institution can also set requirements stricter than your governing body's baseline.

A few things that can affect more than you'd expect:
• Dropping a course
• Withdrawing from a course
• Repeating a course
• Changing your major or declared degree
• Taking fewer credit hours than a full course load in a given term

Any of these can affect your academic standing, your continuing athletic eligibility, and, if you're an international student, your immigration status, all at once and not always in the same way.

If you're an F-1 international student specifically: full-time enrollment is a condition of your status, and your Designated School Official (DSO) is the one who can authorize an exception. Under U.S. immigration rules, a DSO may approve a reduced course load only for a documented initial academic difficulty (limited to once per program level), a documented temporary illness or medical condition, or because you need fewer courses to finish your program in your final term. Athletic time demands on their own are not, by themselves, an approved reason for a reduced course load. Speak with your DSO before making any change to your enrollment, even if the change seems minor or temporary.

Who to talk to before you act:
• Your academic advisor, about how a specific course change affects your degree progress
• Your athletics compliance office, about how it affects your athletic eligibility
• Your DSO, before any enrollment change, if you're in F-1 status

Nobody expects you to track every rule yourself. What matters is asking before you act, not after.

**Building a Realistic Weekly Schedule**

Good scheduling doesn't fix a workload that's genuinely too much, but it does stop a manageable one from feeling impossible. This is a practical process, not a rule, so treat it as a starting point to adjust to your own week.

Build your week in this order:
1. Enter your fixed commitments first: classes, mandatory practice and competition, required team meetings, and anything else you don't control.
2. Add study blocks before your open time disappears. Study time scheduled as 'whatever's left' tends to shrink to nothing.
3. Add meals, sleep, recovery or treatment time, and travel time between classes, training, and housing. These aren't optional extras; they're what makes the rest of the schedule sustainable.
4. Identify the points where things genuinely conflict, a mandatory class overlapping a mandatory team commitment, for instance, rather than assuming it will work itself out.
5. Raise real conflicts early, with your academic advisor and your coach or compliance office, rather than waiting until you've already missed something.
6. Review the whole schedule weekly. Your first draft won't survive contact with a real week; adjust it as you go rather than rebuilding it from scratch every time something changes.

What to include in your weekly plan:
• Class time
• Study time, ideally scheduled the same way you'd schedule practice
• Practice and competition
• Travel time
• Meals
• Sleep
• Treatment, recovery, or training-room time
• Tutoring or advising appointments
• Personal admin (banking, laundry, appointments)
• Time to actually communicate with coaches, advisors, or teammates when something needs to be raised
• A buffer for the unexpected, because something always comes up

If your schedule genuinely doesn't fit no matter how it's arranged, that's a signal to raise directly with your academic advisor and athletics compliance office (see this module's sections on time-demand limits and falling behind), not a scheduling problem to solve alone.

**Academic Support Services on Campus**

Almost every school offers some form of academic support for student-athletes, but what's actually available, and which office provides it, varies a lot from one institution to the next. Don't assume your school works the same way as one a teammate or friend describes.

Roles you're likely to encounter, in some form:
• Academic advisor: tracks your degree progress and course planning; does not decide athletic eligibility
• Student-athlete academic support staff or a dedicated athletics study center, where your school has one: often coordinates tutoring, study halls, or structured check-ins specifically for athletes
• Tutors or learning specialists: help with specific coursework; don't make eligibility or enrollment decisions
• Faculty: the people who actually teach and grade your courses, and the first call if something in a specific class is going wrong
• Athletics compliance: certifies eligibility and monitors governing-body rules; doesn't run tutoring or teach courses
• Coach: can support and encourage, but doesn't decide academic standing, eligibility, or enrollment
• International student office or DSO: supports your immigration status and visa compliance specifically
• Disability or accessibility services, where you're eligible: arranges accommodations for a documented condition
• Campus counseling or mental-health services: supports your wellbeing, separate from your academic or athletic standing

What to actually do:
• Ask your academic advisor or athletics compliance office what academic support exists specifically at your school, and how to access it
• Don't wait for a service to be offered to you; most of these are opt-in, not automatic
• If you're not sure which office owns a specific problem, your academic advisor or athletics compliance office is a reasonable place to start and can point you the rest of the way

Rep1 can help you know what questions to ask and what kind of support typically exists, but Rep1 doesn't provide these services itself and isn't a substitute for your school's own advising, tutoring, or compliance offices.

**What to Do if You Fall Behind**

Falling behind, academically or otherwise, is common and very fixable early. It becomes a much bigger problem mainly when it's left unaddressed. If something's slipping, work through this sequence as soon as you notice it, not after your next set of grades.

1. Identify exactly what's falling behind: a specific class, your overall course load, your degree progress, or something else entirely. Being specific makes it easier for anyone to actually help you.
2. Contact the professor or the relevant academic support office directly. This is usually the fastest way to address a problem in a specific course.
3. Tell your academic advisor. They can see how the issue affects your broader degree progress, not just one class.
4. Contact your athletics compliance office if there's any chance the issue could affect your eligibility. Raising it early gives them room to help; raising it late often doesn't.
5. If you're an international student in F-1 status, speak with your DSO before making any change to your enrollment, including dropping or withdrawing from a course. As covered in this module's staying-on-track section, a reduced course load has specific, limited approved reasons, and your DSO is the only person who can authorize one.
6. Use tutoring, accessibility services, or campus counseling where relevant, rather than trying to manage everything alone.
7. Write down the guidance you're given at each step, including dates and who you spoke with.
8. Build a short recovery plan with specific dates and next actions, ideally with whichever office is helping you most directly.

If things ever go beyond an academic or logistical problem:
• Immediate danger: call 911
• A mental-health, suicide, or substance-use crisis: call or text 988, available 24 hours a day
• For ongoing, non-crisis support: your school's campus counseling service is a normal, confidential first stop
• Rep1 does not provide treatment, crisis intervention, or counseling, and isn't the right contact for any of the above

Most academic problems don't need crisis-level language, they need an early, specific conversation with the right person. Use that first.

**Common Mistakes**

Watch for these, they cover most of the avoidable problems that come up around eligibility, time demands, and academic standing.

• Assuming that clearing initial eligibility means you're automatically fine for every year after
• Treating NCAA rules, including specific hour or progress figures, as if they apply identically to NAIA or NJCAA
• Relying on a coach as your only source of eligibility or enrollment advice
• Waiting until the end of the term, or until grades are already in, to ask for help
• Dropping, withdrawing from, or changing a class before checking how it affects your degree progress, athletic eligibility, and immigration status together
• Assuming a 'voluntary' activity is actually optional at your specific school without confirming it
• Not writing down schedule conflicts or unusual demands when they happen, then struggling to describe them accurately later
• Focusing only on GPA while losing track of actual progress toward your specific degree
• Assuming every school offers the same academic-support services, office names, or process
• Treating Rep1's guidance, or this handbook, as an official eligibility, compliance, or immigration determination

None of these mistakes are complicated to avoid. Almost all of them come down to asking early, asking the right office, and writing down what you're told.

**Key Takeaways**

Initial eligibility and continuing eligibility are different processes; staying eligible depends on your actual ongoing academic record, reviewed by your athletics compliance office. Time-demand rules vary by governing body and division, so confirm the exact current limits with your compliance office rather than assuming a single number applies everywhere. Real progress toward your specific degree matters, not just passing courses, and NCAA's progress-toward-degree rule is NCAA-specific. If you're an international student, speak with your DSO before any enrollment change; a reduced course load has specific, limited approved reasons and athletic time demands alone aren't one of them. Build a weekly schedule around your fixed commitments first, and review it often. Academic support exists at almost every school but looks different everywhere, so ask directly what's available. If you start falling behind, act immediately: identify the specific issue, contact the right office, and involve your DSO before changing your enrollment.

**Next Action**

This week, confirm three things directly with the right office: your current continuing-eligibility standing with athletics compliance, how your course schedule counts toward your specific degree with your academic advisor, and, if you're an international student, that any planned schedule change has been discussed with your DSO first.

### Module 20 — Navigate Team Culture and Your Role on the Roster

Every team has its own culture: its own hierarchy, its own sense of humour, its own way of handling conflict. Some of that is healthy and worth learning to navigate. Some of it isn't, and no team's 'tradition' makes harmful conduct acceptable. This module covers how to read team hierarchy without losing yourself in it, the difference between hard competition and unhealthy pressure, how to recognise hazing and know your options if you encounter it, what your coach actually decides and what they don't, how to handle ordinary conflict, and which office to involve when something goes beyond what you can or should handle on your own. No two U.S. programs run exactly the same way. Treat the specifics here as what to expect in general, and confirm how your own program actually works.

**Understanding Team Hierarchy and Earning Respect**

Almost every college team has some form of informal hierarchy: seniors and veterans typically carry more standing than incoming freshmen, and playing time is usually earned rather than given. That's normal and not, by itself, a problem to solve.

What healthy hierarchy generally looks like:
• Veterans getting more say in team culture and informal leadership, without that authority extending to your coach's decisions on selection or playing time
• New players being expected to show up prepared, work hard, and learn team systems quickly
• Respect that's earned through effort and reliability, not demanded through intimidation
• Team leaders (often captains) modelling standards, not enforcing them through humiliation

As an incoming Australian player, a few things are worth expecting:
• You will likely know less about team-specific systems, terminology, and culture than teammates who've been there longer. That's a starting point, not a deficiency.
• Earning standing takes time and consistent effort. It is not usually accelerated by trying to prove yourself through a single dramatic moment.
• Every program's specific culture, rituals, and unwritten rules are different. What was normal at a previous team, in Australia or the U.S., is not a reliable guide to what's normal here.

Hierarchy becomes a problem when it's used to justify humiliation, exclusion, or unsafe treatment of newer players rather than simply reflecting experience and earned trust. This module's section on recognising hazing covers exactly where that line sits.

What to actually do:
• Observe before you assume. Learn how your specific team actually operates before deciding what's expected of you.
• Ask veteran teammates or your captain directly if you're unsure what's expected in a given situation.
• Bring genuine effort and reliability. That's what actually earns standing on most teams, more than anything else.

**Healthy Competition vs. Unhealthy Pressure**

Competing hard for a roster spot or playing time is the entire point of college athletics, and it can feel intense even when everything about it is healthy and normal. The goal here isn't to avoid pressure. It's to recognise when pressure has crossed into something else.

Healthy competition generally looks like:
• Being pushed to improve specific, identifiable skills
• Direct, sometimes blunt, feedback about your performance
• Genuine competition for reps and playing time against teammates
• Coaches or veterans holding you to a real standard
• Disappointment or frustration after a loss or a bad performance that fades and doesn't define how you're treated afterward

Unhealthy pressure generally looks like:
• Feedback that targets you as a person rather than your performance
• Public humiliation, especially repeated or designed to embarrass rather than instruct
• Deliberate exclusion from the team socially or informationally, as a form of punishment
• Pressure to play through symptoms that could indicate a serious injury, or to hide one
• A pattern where one person is targeted more than the situation reasonably explains

A useful check: healthy pressure is about the work. Unhealthy pressure is about you, personally, as a target. Healthy pressure usually leaves room for you to improve and be treated normally afterward. Unhealthy pressure often doesn't.

One difficult loss, one hard conversation, or one demanding coach is not automatically a problem. A sustained pattern that targets you personally, or that consistently crosses from performance feedback into humiliation, is worth raising, using this module's guidance on handling conflict and, if needed, involving compliance or administration.

**Recognizing and Reporting Hazing**

Hazing is a specific, defined problem, not just a label for anything uncomfortable. Knowing the actual definition matters, because it protects you from both underreacting to something serious and overreacting to something that's just difficult.

What hazing actually is: conduct committed against someone joining, or trying to maintain membership in, a team or organisation that is humiliating, intimidating, or demeaning, or that endangers a person's health or safety. Hazing includes active participation and passive participation (going along with it, or standing by while it happens to someone else), and it counts whether or not the person appeared to agree to it.

A few points that matter more than they might seem to:
• Consent doesn't make otherwise unsafe or prohibited conduct acceptable. Someone agreeing, or seeming to agree, to participate doesn't change whether the conduct is hazing.
• Calling something a tradition, an initiation, team bonding, or voluntary doesn't make it acceptable if it's humiliating, degrading, coercive, or dangerous. These labels are exactly how hazing is usually justified, not evidence that it's fine.
• Hazing can be physical (forced physical exertion, exposure, or contact beyond normal training), emotional or degrading (humiliation, forced embarrassment, demeaning tasks), coercive (pressure, explicit or implied, that you must participate to belong), or dangerous (anything involving alcohol, risk of injury, or risk to health).
• Severity isn't only physical. Mental and emotional harm from hazing can be just as serious as a physical injury.

What hazing is not: a single hard practice, blunt feedback, legitimate team rules, or an uncomfortable moment that isn't humiliating, coercive, or unsafe. Not every difficult experience on a team is hazing, and treating ordinary hard coaching or normal team standards as hazing makes it harder to recognise the real thing.

Policy and law: the NCAA describes hazing prevention as a shared responsibility between the NCAA and its member institutions, and hazing is against the law in most U.S. states, though the exact definition and penalties vary by state. Your specific school will also have its own hazing policy and its own reporting process, and that institutional process, not this handbook, is what actually governs how a report at your school is handled. Ask your athletics compliance office or student conduct office where to find your school's specific policy.

What to do if you experience or witness hazing:
• If anyone is in immediate danger, or there's a medical emergency, call 911 (or your campus's emergency number) first, before anything else.
• If it's not an emergency, use your institution's own reporting process. That may mean your athletics compliance office, athletics administration, student conduct office, campus safety, or, if the conduct is sex-based, the Title IX office. Your coach is one possible contact, but is not automatically the only or the correct one, particularly if the coach or team culture itself is part of the problem.
• Write down what happened as soon as you reasonably can: dates, what occurred, who was involved, and who else witnessed it. This helps whichever office reviews it and helps you recall details accurately later.
• You do not have to confront a group or an individual yourself, especially where there's any risk to your safety. Reporting through the appropriate office is the intended path, not a personal confrontation.

On Title IX specifically: Title IX covers sex discrimination, including sexual harassment that is severe, pervasive, and objectively offensive enough to deny equal access to education, along with specific categories like sexual assault. Not all hazing automatically falls under Title IX. Hazing that is sex-based and meets that threshold may be a Title IX matter; hazing that isn't sex-based, or doesn't meet that threshold, is generally addressed through your institution's own conduct or hazing policy instead. If you're not sure which applies, your Title IX office or student conduct office can tell you, and you don't need to work that out yourself before reporting.

Rep1 cannot promise a specific outcome, confidentiality, anonymity, or protection from retaliation. Ask the office you report to directly what protections and confidentiality actually apply at your institution, rather than assuming.

**Your Coach Relationship: What They Decide and What They Don't**

Your coach has real authority over your football experience, but not unlimited authority over every part of your life on campus. Knowing the actual boundary helps you know who to ask, and who not to blame or credit, for a given decision.

What your coach typically does decide:
• Team selection and roster spots
• Playing time and position
• Practice structure, competition strategy, and performance expectations
• Team rules and standards, within your institution's broader policies
• Whether to refer you to another office (compliance, academic support, athletic training, counseling) when something comes up

What your coach typically does not decide:
• Your athletic eligibility. That's certified by your athletics compliance office against governing-body and institutional rules.
• Your academic eligibility or academic standing. That sits with your academic advisor, your professors, and your institution's academic policies.
• Your immigration status or any change to your enrollment as an international student. That's your DSO's role, not your coach's.
• Findings in a student conduct or Title IX matter. Those are decided through your institution's conduct and Title IX processes, not by your coach.
• Your medical clearance to play. That's a medical decision made by an athletic trainer or team physician, not a coaching decision.
• Your mental health treatment or diagnosis. That's the role of a licensed counselor or medical provider.
• Your scholarship or financial aid decisions. Those go through your institution's financial aid and compliance offices under governing-body and institutional rules, even where a coach's recommendation may be part of that process.

A coach who tells you they control one of these things, directly or by implication, is describing their program's practice at most, not the actual rule. If you're ever unsure whether something is genuinely a coach's call, the offices listed above, or this module's section on when to involve compliance or administration, are the places to check.

None of this is about undermining your coach's authority where it does apply. It's about knowing which conversations belong with your coach and which ones need a different office entirely.

**Handling Conflict With Teammates or Coaches**

Conflict with a teammate or coach is normal on a competitive team and doesn't need to be treated as a crisis. Most of it is resolved through a direct, calm conversation rather than escalation.

A reasonable approach for ordinary conflict:
1. Address it directly and privately with the person involved, where it's safe and appropriate to do so. Most misunderstandings and frustrations are resolved faster this way than by involving anyone else first.
2. Focus on specific behavior and its impact, not character. 'When X happened, it affected Y' lands better, and is more accurate, than a broad accusation.
3. Choose your moment. Right after a loss, in front of the team, or mid-practice is rarely the right time for a serious conversation.
4. Listen to the other side. Most conflict involves two people who each have a partly accurate view of what happened.
5. If a direct conversation doesn't resolve it, or doesn't feel safe or appropriate to have, involve a team captain or your position coach as a next step before going further.
6. Keep a simple, dated record of anything that keeps recurring. You don't need this for a one-off disagreement, but it matters if a pattern is building.

When a direct conversation is not the right approach:
• Where there's a real difference in power that makes a direct conversation unsafe or unrealistic (for example, with a coach who controls your playing time, or a much more senior teammate)
• Where the issue involves anything covered in this module's hazing section
• Where there's any risk to your physical safety
• Where the same issue keeps recurring despite you raising it directly

In any of those situations, move to this module's section on when to involve compliance or administration rather than continuing to handle it alone.

**When to Involve Compliance or Administration**

Several offices exist specifically to handle situations that go beyond a direct conversation with a teammate or coach. Which one is right depends on what's actually happening, and exact office names and structures vary by institution, so treat the roles below as what to expect in general.

Athletics compliance: handles rules-based questions, including eligibility and governing-body requirements, and can often direct you to the right office if you're unsure where an issue belongs.

Athletics administration: oversees the athletics department more broadly, including team culture and conduct concerns that involve a coach or program, beyond what compliance handles.

Student conduct office: investigates and adjudicates alleged violations of your institution's code of conduct, including many hazing complaints, separate from the athletics department.

Title IX office: handles reports of sex discrimination and sexual harassment meeting Title IX's specific definition, as covered in this module's hazing section. If you're unsure whether an issue is a Title IX matter, this office, or student conduct, can tell you; you don't need to determine that yourself first.

Campus safety or law enforcement: the right contact for anything involving immediate danger, threats, violence, or a medical emergency. Call 911 or your campus's emergency number for anything urgent.

Athletic trainer or team physician: the right contact for injury, medical clearance to play, and physical health concerns, not your coach.

Campus counseling or mental health services: confidential support for stress, mental health, or the emotional impact of a difficult team situation, separate from any conduct or compliance process.

International student office or DSO: the right contact for anything touching your visa status or enrollment, including if a conduct process could affect either.

What Rep1 can and can't do: Rep1 can help you understand these roles, help you prepare what to say, help you recognise warning signs, and point you toward the right office. Rep1 cannot investigate a complaint, decide whether someone broke a rule or law, determine your eligibility, or act as your coach, DSO, compliance officer, conduct officer, Title IX coordinator, lawyer, doctor, or counselor, and cannot guarantee a particular outcome. If you're not sure who to contact, athletics compliance or student conduct is a reasonable place to start, and they can point you the rest of the way.

**Common Mistakes**

Watch for these. They cover most of the avoidable problems that come up around team culture, hazing, and coach authority.

• Assuming a previous team's culture, in Australia or elsewhere, tells you what's normal at your new program
• Treating every uncomfortable or difficult moment as hazing, which makes it harder to recognise the real thing
• Assuming a 'voluntary' or 'tradition' label means an activity can't be hazing
• Going along with something that feels wrong because it seems like everyone else has accepted it
• Confronting a group alone over something unsafe instead of using your institution's reporting process
• Assuming your coach controls decisions that actually belong to compliance, academic advising, your DSO, athletic training, or Title IX
• Treating a single hard coaching moment or one tough loss as proof of a toxic environment
• Waiting for a serious pattern to become severe before raising it with anyone
• Assuming Rep1, or any one office, can promise a specific outcome, confidentiality, or protection from retaliation
• Not writing down what happened, and when, until long after it's hard to recall accurately

Most of these come down to the same fix: check what's actually normal at your specific program, and ask the right office early rather than guessing or waiting.

**Key Takeaways**

Team hierarchy is normal and usually reflects earned experience, not a problem to solve. Healthy competition pushes your performance; unhealthy pressure targets you personally, and a sustained pattern of the latter is worth raising. Hazing is conduct that's humiliating, intimidating, demeaning, or dangerous, and it counts regardless of apparent consent or a 'tradition' label; use your institution's own reporting process, and call 911 for any immediate danger. Not all hazing is automatically a Title IX matter; your Title IX or student conduct office can tell you which applies. Your coach decides selection, playing time, and team rules, but not your eligibility, immigration status, medical clearance, conduct or Title IX findings, or financial aid. Handle ordinary conflict directly and privately where it's safe to do so, and escalate to compliance, administration, student conduct, Title IX, campus safety, or your DSO when it isn't. Rep1 can help you understand these roles and prepare to act, but cannot investigate, decide outcomes, or guarantee confidentiality or results.

**Next Action**

Before your next issue comes up, not after: find out, directly from your athletics compliance office or student handbook, exactly how your specific school defines hazing, where its reporting policy is published, and which office handles student conduct versus Title IX versus compliance concerns. Write down the names and contact details for at least two of them.

### Module 21 — Manage Homesickness and Stay Connected to Home

Homesickness is one of the most common, least talked about parts of being an international student-athlete, and it has nothing to do with toughness. You're managing distance from family, a new culture, a full academic load, and competitive football all at once, often in that order of difficulty on any given week. This module covers why the experience can hit international athletes differently, why adjustment moves forward and backward rather than in a straight line, how to stay connected to home without cutting yourself off from your life here, how to build real support on campus, how to find your school's own mental health resources, and exactly when ordinary homesickness has become something that needs more support. Reaching out early is not a sign you made the wrong decision. It's what makes the rest of the experience work.

**Why Homesickness Hits International Athletes Differently**

Homesickness is a normal response to leaving home. For an international student-athlete, several things usually stack on top of it at the same time.

What tends to stack up:
• Real distance and cost. You can't drive home for a weekend, and a flight back to Australia is expensive and long. That changes how you think about missing family events.
• Time-zone separation. When your family is awake, you're often asleep, training, or in class, which makes casual daily contact genuinely harder, not just emotionally harder.
• Different food, language, humour, and social norms, on top of everything else you're adjusting to. Even fluent English speakers hit moments where a joke, a reference, or an assumption doesn't land the way it would at home.
• Academic and athletic pressure arriving together. You're building a new academic routine and trying to earn your place on a roster in the same semester, with little of the buffer you had at home.
• Loss of your existing support system. The people who normally noticed when something was off, family, long-term friends, a home club, aren't physically present anymore.
• Pressure to appear tough. Athletes in particular can feel that admitting to homesickness looks like weakness or a lack of commitment to the team. It isn't either.

It's worth being clear about the difference between missing home, which is uncomfortable but doesn't stop you functioning, and being unable to function safely, which is a different and more serious situation covered later in this module.

What to actually do:
• Name what specifically feels different this week: is it the food, the isolation, a bad training week, or something else? Being specific makes it easier to act on.
• Notice when it tends to spike: after a loss, around a family event back home, during a break when teammates go home and you can't.
• Keep basic routines going (sleep, meals, training, class) even in a hard week; they're easier to hold onto than to rebuild.
• Tell one trusted person early, a teammate, a coach, a friend, rather than carrying it alone.
• Don't judge the whole experience by one difficult week. A rough patch is information, not a verdict on whether you made the right decision.

**The Stages of Cultural Adjustment**

Cultural adjustment is often described using a rough framework of stages. Treat it as a practical way to make sense of what you're feeling, not a clinical model or a fixed timeline you're supposed to follow.

A useful, flexible framework:
• Initial excitement: the novelty of a new country, new team, and new independence often carries the first weeks or months.
• Frustration or fatigue: the everyday effort of operating in a new culture and system catches up, and small things start to feel harder than they should.
• Gradual adjustment: systems, people, and routines start to feel familiar rather than foreign.
• Increased confidence: you're operating with less conscious effort and more genuine comfort.
• Periodic setbacks: even after adjusting well, specific events (an injury, a holiday spent away from family, a conflict with a teammate, a hard academic stretch) can bring frustration or homesickness back.

What matters more than the labels:
• You may move back and forth between these rather than progressing in order, and that's normal, not a sign you're doing it wrong.
• Not everyone experiences every stage, or experiences them at the same intensity.
• There's no required or expected timeline. Someone adjusting well in month two and struggling again in month eight hasn't failed at anything.
• A setback doesn't erase the progress you'd already made; it's a normal part of an ongoing process, not a reset to zero.

A practical self-check, worth returning to periodically:
• What's changed recently, for better or worse?
• What's genuinely improving, even slowly?
• What still feels difficult right now?
• What kind of support would actually help this week: a conversation, a routine, professional support, or just time?

Using this kind of check-in regularly, rather than only when things feel bad, makes it easier to notice a genuine pattern instead of reacting to a single hard day.

**Staying Connected With Family Across Time Zones**

Staying close to home matters, but it works best as one part of your life here, not a replacement for building one. The goal is connection without dependence.

Practical ways to stay connected:
• Choose a predictable call or video window that both time zones can realistically sustain, rather than trying to be available constantly.
• Plan around both time zones honestly: a time that's convenient for you at 11pm before an early lift is not sustainable long-term, even if it feels fine the first few weeks.
• Set expectations with family about delayed replies. If they know you're in class or training rather than ignoring them, a slower reply causes far less worry on both ends.
• Use text, voice notes, or photos for everyday updates when a live call doesn't fit your schedule that day. Connection doesn't require a call every time.
• Share ordinary, everyday updates, not only the highlights or the crises. Family relationships stay strongest when they include the boring parts of your week, not just the big news.
• Protect the things a late-night or early-morning call can quietly damage: sleep, class, study time, training, and recovery. A connection habit that consistently costs you sleep before training isn't sustainable.
• Plan ahead for major family dates (birthdays, anniversaries, significant events) so missing them in person feels less abrupt.
• Agree on a backup contact plan for genuinely urgent situations, so everyone knows how to reach you (or your emergency contact) if something time-sensitive comes up outside your normal contact window.

There's no universal 'right' amount of contact. What matters is that your contact with home supports you, rather than becoming the only thing holding you together. If constant contact with home is making it harder to build a life here, that's worth naming to yourself honestly, not ignoring.

**Building a Support Network on Campus**

Relying on one person, whether that's a single teammate, a partner, or your family back home, as your only support is a common but risky pattern. If that one relationship changes or that person isn't available, you can end up with nothing to fall back on. A network spread across several people and roles is far more resilient.

People and offices who can each cover a different part of this:
• A teammate, for everyday connection and shared experience
• Your coach or support staff, for team-related support and pointing you toward the right resource
• Your athletic trainer, for anything physical or recovery-related
• Your academic advisor, for academic planning and support
• A faculty member, for anything specific to a class
• Your international student office or DSO, for immigration, enrollment, and international-student-specific questions
• Residence-life staff, for anything housing-related
• Campus counseling services, for structured emotional or mental-health support
• A cultural or international student organisation, if your campus has one, for people who understand the specific experience of being far from home
• A faith or community group, if that's genuinely relevant to you
• At least one trusted friend outside the team, so your entire support system isn't tied to one part of your life

A practical support-map exercise. Write down one real name (or at least the right office) for each of these:
• Who do I talk to for everyday connection?
• Who helps with academics?
• Who helps with athletics?
• Who helps with immigration or international-student matters?
• Who helps with health or mental health?
• Who do I contact in an emergency?

Don't assume your school has the same offices, names, or services as a teammate's previous school or a friend's school back home. Confirm what actually exists at your specific institution.

**Campus Mental Health and Counseling Resources**

Every school handles counseling and mental health support differently. Service names, how you book an appointment, what it costs, how many sessions you get, and how confidentiality works all vary by institution, so this section is about how to find and evaluate your own school's actual setup, not a single process that applies everywhere.

How to find out what your school actually offers:
• Search your school's official website for terms like counseling, student health, behavioral health, crisis support, or student wellness.
• Look specifically for after-hours or crisis instructions, not just regular business-hours services.
• Check whether international student services or accessibility/disability services are separately listed, since either may be relevant depending on your situation.

Questions worth answering before you need the service urgently:
• How do I actually book an appointment, and how long does that typically take?
• Is counseling free, included in a student fee, or charged separately?
• Is there a session limit, and what happens if I need more than that?
• What happens outside business hours or during a genuine emergency?
• What are the actual limits on confidentiality? (Every service has some, typically around imminent risk to yourself or others; ask directly rather than assuming either total secrecy or none at all.)
• Can they refer me elsewhere if what I need isn't something they provide?

A few things worth being clear about:
• Availability, cost, and confidentiality rules genuinely differ between schools; don't assume your experience will match a teammate's from a different institution.
• Your coach, a teammate, your DSO, a parent, and Rep1 are all valuable in different ways, but none of them are a substitute for a licensed counselor or medical professional.
• If a situation involves immediate danger, don't wait to find the right office or the right appointment slot. Use this module's escalation guidance instead.

If you want a concrete example of what a counseling center's information typically looks like, most official university student-health or counseling-center websites publish exactly this kind of detail (hours, booking process, and after-hours contact) directly on their own site; treat any single school's page as one example of the pattern, not a rule for every institution.

**When Homesickness Becomes Something More**

Most homesickness is uncomfortable but manageable, and it eases with time, routine, and connection. Sometimes it develops into something that benefits from professional support. Noticing the difference matters more than labeling it yourself.

Signs that it may be time to involve additional support, rather than just riding it out:
• The distress is persistent, not just an occasional hard day
• It's intensifying rather than easing over time
• It's interfering with sleep
• It's interfering with eating
• It's interfering with class attendance
• It's interfering with practice or your normal day-to-day functioning
• You're withdrawing and isolating from people around you
• It's connected to increased alcohol or substance use
• It's connected to thoughts of self-harm or suicide
• There's any situation involving immediate danger to yourself or someone else

These signs don't prove a diagnosis, and this handbook isn't making one. What they do mean is that it's appropriate and reasonable to talk with a qualified professional, the same way a persistent physical symptom would be worth getting checked rather than diagnosed by guesswork.

How to match the situation to the right kind of help:

Campus counseling or another qualified professional: use this when you're struggling but not in immediate danger, when the issue is affecting your daily functioning, and when what you need is assessment, ongoing support, or a referral to the right kind of care.

988 (in the United States): call or text 988 when you, or someone you know, is experiencing a suicide, mental health, or substance-use crisis and needs immediate crisis support, even if the situation isn't one requiring an emergency response. The 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline is available 24 hours a day by call, text, or chat.

911: call 911 when there's immediate danger, a life-threatening emergency, or a situation where someone may act right away to harm themselves or someone else, or whenever urgent emergency medical or safety response is genuinely needed. 911 takes priority whenever immediate physical danger is present, even alongside a mental health crisis.

988 and 911 serve different purposes: 988 does not replace 911 in a genuine emergency, and not every difficult week or instance of homesickness requires crisis-line or emergency intervention. Most homesickness never reaches this point, and reaching this point doesn't mean you've failed at adjusting, it means it's time to bring in the right kind of support.

If you are ever in this situation yourself, or supporting a teammate who is, you are not expected to manage it alone: use 988 or 911 as appropriate, and follow up with your campus counseling service or a trusted adult afterward.

**Common Mistakes**

Watch for these. They cover most of the avoidable problems that come up around homesickness, cultural adjustment, and staying connected.

• Assuming homesickness means the move was a mistake
• Expecting adjustment to happen in a straight line, then feeling like a setback means you've failed
• Calling home constantly while avoiding building any local relationships
• Cutting off contact with home completely to try to 'force' adjustment
• Hiding real distress to appear tough in front of teammates or coaches
• Depending on one teammate, coach, or partner as your only source of support
• Waiting until a genuine crisis before finding out what your campus resources even are
• Assuming your school's counseling service works exactly like a friend's or teammate's did at a different school
• Expecting your DSO to provide therapy or treat a mental-health concern
• Expecting a coach or Rep1 to diagnose or treat a mental-health condition
• Letting sleep, food, recovery, and basic routine slide during a hard stretch
• Confusing 988 with 911, or hesitating to use either when they're actually needed
• Treating a social-media post or forum comment as professional mental-health advice
• Changing enrollment, travel, or course load plans without speaking to your DSO first, if the change could affect your immigration status

If you take one thing from this module: reaching out early, to a person or an actual service, is the single most effective thing you can do. Almost none of this needs to become a crisis before it's worth mentioning to someone.

**Key Takeaways**

Homesickness is common among international student-athletes and isn't a sign of weakness or a wrong decision. It usually comes from several things stacking together: distance, time zones, cultural difference, and academic and athletic pressure arriving at once. Cultural adjustment moves forward and backward, not in a straight line, and setbacks after a specific event (injury, holidays, conflict, academic pressure) are normal, not failure. Stay connected with home in a way that supports you without limiting your ability to build a life here. Build a support network across several people and offices rather than depending on just one. Campus counseling and mental-health resources vary by school, so find out directly what yours actually offers before you need it urgently. Persistent, intensifying distress, or anything connected to self-harm, suicide, or substance misuse, is worth professional support: use campus counseling for non-immediate struggles, 988 for a mental-health or crisis situation, and 911 for immediate danger. Rep1 can help you prepare and point you to the right resource, but does not diagnose, treat, or replace any of these services.

Include: one family contact, one teammate or friend, one coach or athletics contact, one academic contact, your DSO or international student office, your campus counseling contact, your campus's after-hours or crisis contact, 988, and 911. Also write down one simple routine (a specific habit, not just an intention) you'll lean on during a difficult week.

### Module 22 — Handle Injury, Setbacks, and Return to Play

Injury is a real possibility in any contact or high-intensity sport, and it doesn't mean something has gone wrong with your athletic career. What matters is what happens next. Reporting promptly, following medical guidance, and understanding who actually makes each decision protects both your health and your future eligibility. Medical care, participation status, governing-body eligibility, insurance, and immigration status are five separate decision areas that happen to intersect around an injury, each with its own office and its own process. This module walks through what typically happens after an in-season injury, what medical hardship waivers and redshirt terminology actually mean (and don't mean), how to work with your athletic training staff, the emotional side of a setback, what to confirm about insurance, how to stay connected to your team while sidelined, and when asking about a second opinion may be reasonable. Return-to-play decisions belong to qualified medical professionals and your institution's own procedures, not to a coach, a teammate, or Rep1.

**What Happens When You Get Hurt During the Season**

Exact procedures vary by institution, but a typical sequence looks something like this:

1. Report the issue. Tell your athletic trainer or team medical staff as soon as symptoms appear. Don't wait to see if it gets better on its own.
2. Receive an initial evaluation. Your athletic trainer or a team physician assesses what happened and decides what happens next.
3. Follow any restrictions you're given, even if they feel overly cautious.
4. Complete any referrals, imaging, or specialist appointments you're sent to.
5. Keep communicating. Update your athletic trainer if anything changes, gets worse, or doesn't improve as expected.
6. Keep your own records: what was diagnosed, what restrictions were given, and by whom.
7. Ask who controls each decision. Different offices own different pieces of this (see the role summary below).
8. Confirm what this means for your academic schedule, your insurance, and your immigration status if any of those are affected.

These are genuinely separate things, easy to blur together:
• Injury reporting: telling the right person promptly.
• Medical evaluation: a qualified professional assessing what's actually wrong.
• Treatment: the care itself.
• Participation status: whether you can currently train or compete, decided by medical staff, not by you or your coach.
• Eligibility: a separate governing-body and compliance question, covered in the next section.
• Insurance: a separate question about who pays, covered later in this module.
• Immigration or enrollment status: only relevant if the injury affects your course load or enrollment; covered near the end of this module.

If what happened is a genuine emergency, a serious injury, loss of consciousness, a suspected spinal injury, difficulty breathing, or anything that seems life-threatening, call 911 immediately. Don't wait for your athletic trainer or coach first, and don't wait for symptoms to become severe before reporting them. The single most avoidable mistake in this section is delaying a report because a symptom seems minor or embarrassing, or because you don't want to lose your spot. Report early. What happens after that is a process designed to protect you, not punish you.

**Understanding Medical Hardship Waivers and Redshirts**

"Redshirt" gets used casually to mean sitting out a season without losing a year of eligibility, but it doesn't operate as one single, universal rule. What actually applies depends entirely on your governing body, and the specifics change, so treat everything below as a general framework, not a guarantee.

NCAA: NCAA bylaws include a hardship waiver process that can allow an additional year of competition for a season cut short by injury or illness, where the injury or illness happens within roughly the first half of the season and limits how much the student-athlete has already competed. A core requirement is contemporaneous medical documentation, meaning records created at the time of the injury or illness, not written up afterward. Specific participation limits and deadlines are set by NCAA legislation and can change; your athletics compliance office has the current figures and the actual application process. Do not assume a specific percentage, contest count, or deadline without confirming it directly with compliance.

NAIA: for seasons beginning August 1, 2021 onward, the NAIA replaced its old medical hardship waiver process for current NAIA competition with an automatic Season of Competition rule. Under this rule, a student-athlete is not charged with using a season of competition if they participated in 20 percent or fewer of the maximum allowable contests for their sport that year, and no medical documentation or hardship application is required to receive it. A narrow exception still allows a medical hardship request through the NAIA for an injury that happened at a non-NAIA institution (for example, before a transfer), but that exception has its own specific conditions. If you compete under NAIA rules, ask your compliance office whether the automatic rule already covers your situation before assuming you need to apply for anything.

NJCAA: the NJCAA also has its own hardship-type provision for a season cut short by injury or illness, generally requiring medical documentation and a limit on how much competition already occurred. The exact current criteria were not confirmed on njcaa.org during this module's research, so no specific figures are stated here. Confirm the current NJCAA hardship rule directly with your athletics compliance office.

What stays true across every governing body:
• Your athletics compliance office, not Rep1 and not your coach, is the office that explains your actual case and submits or coordinates any required paperwork.
• Medical records can support a hardship request, but a physician's documentation does not, by itself, guarantee approval. The decision sits with the governing body or conference, following its own process.
• Ask early about what documentation is required and what the deadline is. Waiting until late in the season to ask is one of the most common ways students lose an otherwise valid claim.
• Never assume a specific numeric threshold applies to your situation, and never assume NCAA, NAIA, and NJCAA rules are interchangeable. They aren't.

**Working With Athletic Training Staff Through Recovery**

Your athletic trainer is a qualified healthcare professional, not a coach's assistant. Athletic trainers are recognised as allied healthcare professionals who evaluate, treat, and rehabilitate injuries, working under the direction of or in collaboration with a physician. Getting the most out of your recovery mostly comes down to honesty and consistency:

• Report symptoms honestly, including ones that feel minor, embarrassing, or inconvenient.
• Follow the restrictions you're given, even when you feel ready sooner.
• Prepare questions before appointments so you're not trying to remember them on the spot.
• Track your own appointments, rather than relying entirely on someone else to remind you.
• Understand your recovery plan: what it involves, roughly how progress will be assessed, and what you're expected to do between sessions.
• Ask specifically what activities are currently permitted. "Resting" can mean very different things depending on the injury.
• Report new or worsening symptoms immediately rather than waiting for your next scheduled appointment.
• Understand who actually provides final clearance to return. It's your athletic trainer, team physician, or another designated healthcare professional working through the institution's own process, not your coach and not you.
• Keep your academic advisor and coaching staff appropriately informed so accommodations or schedule adjustments can be made where needed.

This handbook doesn't set your recovery timeline, and neither should anyone outside your medical team. Recovery speed varies by injury, by person, and by how consistently restrictions are followed, so treat any timeline you're given as a general estimate to confirm with your own athletic trainer, not a fixed deadline.

**The Mental Side of Injury and Setback**

Injury doesn't just affect your body. It can affect your daily routine, your confidence, your sense of who you are as an athlete, your mood, and how connected you feel to your team. Reactions genuinely differ from person to person and from injury to injury; there's no single "normal" way to feel about a setback, and this isn't something you should try to self-diagnose.

Some things worth knowing:
• Frustration, low mood, isolation, and uncertainty are common reactions to injury, not signs of weakness.
• Reaching out for support early, before things feel unmanageable, is a reasonable and normal step, not an overreaction.
• Campus counseling and mental-health services (covered in Module 16) are available to student-athletes, not just to students in crisis.
• A coach, teammate, or Rep1 noticing you're struggling is not the same as any of them being qualified to address it. Support and encouragement from people around you matter, but professional support is a different, and sometimes necessary, thing.

If distress connected to an injury becomes persistent, intensifies, or involves thoughts of self-harm or suicide, treat it the same way this handbook treats any mental-health crisis: campus counseling for non-immediate struggles, 988 for a mental-health or crisis situation that needs immediate support, and 911 for immediate danger. 988 does not replace 911 in a genuine emergency, and needing support during a hard stretch doesn't mean you've failed at recovering.

**Insurance and Your Injury: What to Confirm**

Module 16 covers general health insurance and athletic injury coverage vocabulary in full; this section doesn't repeat that vocabulary, it applies it to an actual injury you're dealing with right now. Use this as a checklist to work through with your athletics compliance office, athletic trainer, or insurance administrator, not as something to work out alone.

Confirm:
• Which policy is expected to pay first: yours or your parents', the school's student health plan, or an athletics-specific policy.
• Whether the school carries secondary, excess, or catastrophic coverage on top of primary insurance, and how that applies to your specific injury.
• Network restrictions: whether your treating providers are in-network, and what happens financially if they're not.
• Whether a referral or pre-authorization is required before a specific treatment, procedure, or specialist visit.
• Your deductible, copayment, and coinsurance amounts, and how they apply to this specific course of treatment.
• Exclusions: whether anything about this injury or its treatment is excluded from coverage.
• How to submit a claim, and who actually does it, you or the school.
• Who receives bills and Explanation of Benefits statements, and who should get copies.
• How treatment during team travel is handled, and whether out-of-network care during travel is treated differently.
• Who to contact if a claim is denied, and what the appeal process looks like.
• Whether any international-student insurance requirements apply on top of the above.

A few things that are not automatically true, no matter how it might feel in the moment:
• The school does not automatically pay for everything related to an athletic injury.
• Being covered by athletics-specific insurance does not mean every cost is covered.
• Treatment received through athletics staff is not automatically free.

Actual coverage depends on your specific policy, your institution's specific arrangements, the circumstances of the injury, provider network status, required authorizations, and the governing documents of whatever plan applies to you. This handbook and Rep1 cannot interpret your individual policy or guarantee what will or won't be paid; that's a conversation for your insurance administrator or your school's student insurance or compliance office. Where something can't be confirmed generally, the right move is to ask the specific question directly rather than assume an answer.

**Staying Connected to the Team While Injured**

Being sidelined can feel isolating, but staying connected to your team is usually possible without breaking any medical restriction. Ways to stay involved:

• Attend team meetings and film sessions you're cleared to attend.
• Participate actively in your rehabilitation, on the schedule your medical team sets.
• Support your teammates, at practice or on gameday, in whatever way is appropriate for your restrictions.
• Keep communicating with your coaching staff about how you're doing, rather than going quiet.
• Ask your coach or athletic trainer about safe, appropriate roles you can take on while recovering, such as helping with preparation or supporting less experienced teammates.
• Keep up your academic routine. It's easy to let it slide when your athletic routine is disrupted.
• Avoid isolating yourself from the team socially, even if training together isn't currently possible.

One thing worth being direct about: your coach is not obligated to guarantee your role, your playing time, or your roster spot while you're injured or once you return. Coaches make team decisions within their own authority, and no one, including this handbook or Rep1, can promise a specific outcome. What you can control is staying honest about your restrictions, staying engaged with your team in appropriate ways, and not pretending to be medically ready before you actually are, since that puts your recovery and your longer-term health at risk.

**When to Seek a Second Opinion**

Asking about a second medical opinion is a normal, reasonable step, not a sign of distrust in your care team. Situations where it may be worth considering include:
• Your diagnosis feels unclear or unconfirmed.
• You're facing a major procedure or a significant treatment decision.
• Symptoms are persistent, worsening, or not improving as expected.
• You've received conflicting recommendations from different providers.
• You genuinely feel uncertain and want more explanation before deciding anything.

How to approach it:
• Tell your current provider you'd like a second opinion. Reputable providers generally expect and support this.
• Ask about transferring your records, imaging, and test results to the new provider ahead of time.
• Confirm whether your insurance requires a referral or prior authorization for a second opinion, and whether the new provider is in-network.
• Confirm with your athletic trainer or compliance office who coordinates this process for a student-athlete specifically, since a second opinion involving athletic participation isn't purely a personal medical matter.
• Keep following your current medical restrictions the entire time. A second opinion in progress is not a reason to loosen or ignore existing restrictions.
• If the second opinion differs from the first, that's useful information, not a decision by itself. Confirm with your athletic trainer and compliance office who makes the actual, final institutional decision about your participation status; getting a second opinion does not mean you can clear yourself.

This isn't about doctor-shopping until you hear what you want, and it isn't a reason to disregard your first clinician's restrictions while you wait. A second opinion can confirm your existing diagnosis just as easily as it can raise a new question, and either outcome is a genuinely useful result.

**Common Mistakes**

Watch for these. They cover most of the avoidable problems that come up around injury, recovery, and return to play.

• Hiding symptoms to avoid losing your spot
• Playing through symptoms that genuinely aren't safe to play through
• Assuming a coach can clear you to return to participation
• Missing scheduled appointments
• Ignoring medical restrictions because you feel fine
• Not keeping your own copies of medical records and restrictions
• Waiting too long in the season to speak with compliance about a possible hardship waiver
• Assuming a hardship waiver or Season of Competition exception is automatically approved
• Treating "redshirt" as one universal rule that works the same way at every school and in every governing body
• Assuming the school automatically pays every bill related to an athletic injury
• Ignoring insurance notices, bills, or Explanation of Benefits statements
• Using an out-of-network provider without checking first
• Changing your enrollment, course load, or academic plans without speaking to your DSO first, if the change could affect your immigration status
• Isolating yourself from the team instead of staying connected in appropriate ways
• Treating a social-media post or forum comment as medical guidance
• Assuming a second opinion automatically overrides your existing medical restrictions
• Expecting Rep1 to diagnose an injury, clear you to play, interpret your insurance policy, or determine your eligibility

Immediate action checklist after any injury:
□ Report the injury to your athletic trainer or medical staff right away
□ Get an initial evaluation and understand your current restrictions
□ Start a simple written record: what happened, what you were told, and by whom
□ Ask which office owns each decision that applies to you (medical, eligibility, insurance, immigration)
□ Confirm your insurance basics for this specific injury
□ Let your academic advisor know if the injury affects your coursework
□ Speak with your DSO first if any enrollment change is being considered
□ Reach out for support, personal or professional, if the setback is affecting you emotionally

The common thread across almost every mistake on this list: report early, confirm directly with the office that actually owns each decision, and keep your own records. None of it requires guessing.

**Key Takeaways**

Report an injury promptly. Delaying a report to protect your spot on the roster usually makes things worse, not better. Medical care, participation status, governing-body eligibility, insurance, and immigration status are five separate decision areas, each with its own office. Coaches, teammates, and Rep1 do not provide medical clearance, determine eligibility, or guarantee a hardship waiver, insurance payment, or outcome. Medical hardship and redshirt rules differ significantly by governing body: NCAA has a hardship waiver process built around contemporaneous medical documentation and confirmed with compliance, current NAIA uses an automatic 20 percent Season of Competition rule instead of a traditional hardship waiver, and NJCAA's specific current criteria should be confirmed directly with compliance. Insurance coverage for an athletic injury depends on your specific policy and institution, never assume the school or athletics automatically pays. Recovery has both a physical and an emotional dimension, and reaching out for support early is a normal, reasonable step. A second opinion is a reasonable option in the right circumstances, but it doesn't erase your existing medical restrictions or let you clear yourself. If an enrollment change is ever on the table because of an injury, speak with your DSO first. Rep1 can help you prepare, organise your questions, and understand these roles, but does not diagnose, treat, clear you to play, or determine eligibility.

### Module 23 — Build a Life and Identity Beyond Football

Football can take up an enormous amount of your time, energy, and sense of self, especially once you're training and competing at this level. That's not a problem by itself, but a problem shows up if football becomes the only thing you are, because every athletic career, no matter how it goes, ends at some point. This module is about building an identity, a set of interests, and a support network that don't collapse the moment your role on the team changes, whether that change is an injury, a coaching decision, a transfer, or simply graduating. It also asks you to think honestly, without guessing at legal or eligibility specifics, about your own long-term athletic outlook, and to know what to do if your pathway looks different than you expected. None of this means treating football as less important while you're playing it. It means making sure you have something solid left standing regardless of how the athletic side of the story turns out.

**Why You Need an Identity Beyond the Depth Chart**

It's easy, especially in a demanding program, to let "athlete" become the entire answer to "who am I?" That's a genuine risk, not just a cliche. When your whole sense of self is tied to your role on the team, a benching, a coaching change, an injury, a redshirt year, or graduation doesn't just change your schedule, it can feel like it erases you. That reaction has a name researchers and counselors use: identity foreclosure, where someone commits so completely to one role that they never develop the others.

What a broader identity actually protects you from:
• A bad week of practice or a coaching decision defining your entire sense of worth.
• Panic if your role, playing time, or roster spot changes.
• Having nothing to fall back on emotionally during an injury or a difficult season.
• Graduating, transferring, or finishing your athletic career with no idea who you are without it.

What a broader identity is not:
• It isn't being less committed to football. Some of the most consistent, resilient competitors are the ones who don't need every single outcome to validate them personally.
• It isn't a signal to your coach that you're not focused. Nobody needs to know you're doing this work; it's for you.
• It isn't a backup plan you only need if things go badly. It's part of a genuinely healthy approach to this whole period of your life, regardless of how your athletic career turns out.

The rest of this module walks through practical ways to build that broader identity: exploring real interests and community outside athletics, being honest with yourself about your athletic trajectory, building relationships that will still be there regardless of your roster status, thinking through what to do if your pathway changes, and staying anchored to your own values along the way.

**Exploring Interests and Community Outside Athletics**

Building a life outside athletics doesn't require a dramatic reinvention. It starts with noticing what you're actually interested in, and then protecting a small amount of real time and energy for it, the same way you'd protect time for lifting or film study.

An identity inventory. Answer these honestly, in a few words each:
• What did you genuinely enjoy doing before football took up most of your schedule?
• What subjects, in or out of class, do you find yourself actually curious about?
• If you had one free afternoon a week with no training obligation, what would you actually want to do with it?
• Who are you around people who have never seen you play football?
• What's something you're good at, or interested in, that has nothing to do with athletic performance?
• Is there a community, club, faith group, cultural group, or activity from home you could reasonably reconnect with or find an equivalent of here?

You don't need long answers. The point is to notice a few real threads, not to design an entire second life this week.

A skills-translation exercise. Football is already building skills that transfer directly to almost anything you do next, even though it rarely feels that way day to day. For each skill below, write one specific example from your own experience:
• Discipline and consistency: showing up and doing the work on days you don't feel like it.
• Time management under real constraints: fitting study, training, recovery, and a social life into the same week.
• Receiving direct feedback and adjusting: taking correction from a coach without it becoming personal.
• Performing under pressure: executing when it actually counts, not just in practice.
• Working inside a team structure: understanding your role, communicating with teammates, and handling conflict.
• Recovering from failure: coming back from a bad game, a bad rep, or a mistake without letting it define the next one.

Writing these down, with a real example attached to each, is genuinely useful later: in a resume, in an interview, in a scholarship application, or simply in how you talk about yourself to people who've never seen a snap of football.

Practical ways to actually build this, not just think about it:
• Join one club, class, or activity that has nothing to do with athletics, even if it's small or low-commitment at first.
• Keep at least one non-football-related conversation topic genuinely current in your life, something you could talk about for five minutes without mentioning the team.
• Spend time with people outside the program occasionally, not to replace your team relationships, just to make sure they aren't the only relationships you have.
• Revisit something you used to enjoy before football took over your time, even in a smaller form than before.

**Thinking Honestly About Your Long-Term Athletic Outlook**

This section asks you to be honest with yourself about where your athletic career actually appears to be heading. That's uncomfortable, and it's normal to resist it. It doesn't mean giving up on your goals, and it doesn't replace what your coaching staff, athletics compliance office, or academic advisor can tell you directly. It means checking your own outlook against the real information already available to you, instead of only against hope.

Trajectory-evaluation questions. Answer these as honestly as you can, ideally in writing:
• What is my actual current role: starter, regular rotation, situational, or primarily practice squad or scout team?
• Has my playing time trended up, stayed steady, or trended down over the last year?
• What has my coaching staff actually told me directly about my role and development, as opposed to what I've assumed or hoped they meant?
• How does my current role compare to where I expected to be at this point when I arrived?
• What would an honest outside observer, someone with no emotional stake in my answer, say about my current trajectory?
• Realistically, how many players at my level and position progress to the next level? Is my personal goal based on the typical case or an exceptional one?
• If my athletic trajectory doesn't go the way I originally hoped, what have I actually built so far that exists independently of it?

A few things worth being direct about:
• Nobody, including a coach, a teammate, a parent, Rep1, or this handbook, can predict your individual athletic outcome with certainty. This exercise is about being honest with the information you already have, not about producing a guaranteed forecast.
• Your coaching staff, not this handbook, is the actual authority on your role and development within your program. If you genuinely don't know where you stand, asking them directly is a reasonable and normal conversation, not a confrontational one.
• A rough current outlook doesn't mean stop working. Plenty of athletes change their trajectory with continued effort, and plenty of others build excellent lives that simply don't include football at the level they once hoped for. Both are legitimate outcomes.
• This exercise is meant to inform the next few sections, particularly what to do if your pathway changes, not to produce a single verdict you treat as final.

**Building Relationships and Networks for Life After Football**

The relationships that matter most for life after football are frequently not the ones that feel most obviously important while you're playing. Teammates matter, but a network built entirely inside the program is a network that gets much smaller the moment your athletic career ends.

People and offices worth building a genuine relationship with, starting now, not later:
• At least one professor or instructor, beyond just attending class. Office hours, a genuine question about the subject, or simply being someone they recognise, all count.
• Your academic advisor, as an actual relationship, not just someone you see once a semester to sign a form.
• Your school's career services or careers office, ideally before you need them urgently.
• Alumni from your program or your degree area, particularly ones who aren't still playing or coaching.
• Teammates who are further along, especially ones who've already navigated a role change, an injury, or the transition out of athletics themselves.
• People from internships, part-time work, volunteering, or campus activities unrelated to athletics.

Practical ways to build this over time:
• Attend at least one career fair or careers-office event per year, even if you're not job-hunting yet.
• Ask for an informational conversation with someone whose path interests you, current students and recent graduates are often more willing to do this than you'd expect.
• Keep a simple, current record of your own experience: a resume or equivalent, updated as you go, not reconstructed from memory the week you need it.
• Build a professional online presence appropriate for your field, separate from purely social accounts, even in a basic form.
• Reach out to one person from this list at least once a semester, even briefly, so the relationship doesn't go cold from disuse.

A coach, a teammate, or Rep1 can be part of this network, but none of them replaces the academic, career, and professional relationships above. Building this now, while you have easy access to campus and program resources, is far easier than trying to build it later, after you've graduated or moved on from the team.

**What If the Pathway Changes? Considering Your Options**

Every athletic pathway can change, sometimes by choice and sometimes not: a coaching change, an injury, a shift in role, a transfer, or simply deciding your priorities have changed. This section isn't about predicting which of these will happen to you. It's about knowing, in general terms, who to talk to if one of them does, so you're not figuring it out for the first time in a moment of stress.

A later part of this academy covers the transfer portal, Name, Image, and Likeness (NIL), and the practical mechanics of changing programs or navigating your athletic and academic path after a major change. That material isn't available yet within this handbook. What matters right now is understanding the shape of your options and who actually answers each kind of question, not the specific rules or procedures themselves.

Three broad ways a pathway can change, and who to actually talk to for each:
• Your situation changes, but you stay on your current path. Something shifts (an injury, a role change, a hard semester) but your overall plan doesn't. Talk to your academic advisor about coursework impact, your athletic training staff about anything medical, and your coach about your role, each in their own lane.
• You stay in football, but the program or level changes. You're considering, or facing, a change in team, school, or level of competition. This is exactly the kind of decision the academy's upcoming transfer-portal and eligibility content will cover in detail. Until then, the people who can actually help you think it through are your academic advisor (how a change would affect your degree progress), your DSO (if you're on a student visa, since a change in enrollment or institution can have real immigration consequences that need to be handled correctly and in advance), athletics compliance (governing-body transfer rules), and your family.
• Football stops being part of your plan, in whole or in part. Whether by choice, injury, or a change in what you want, this is a legitimate outcome, not a failure. An academic advisor can help you think about your degree path without athletics attached to it. A career-services or careers office can help you think about what comes next professionally. A financial advisor, or your family, can help you think through anything financial that depended on athletic participation, such as scholarship terms, since those are institution-specific and need to be confirmed directly with your school, not assumed.

A short self-check for this section, without needing to resolve any of it today:
• If my role changed significantly next season, who would I talk to first?
• Do I know who my DSO is, and would I know to speak with them before making any enrollment change if I'm on a student visa?
• Do I know who my academic advisor is, separately from my athletic staff?
• Have I ever had a real conversation with my family about what happens if this pathway looks different than expected?

This section is deliberately a question for your own advisors and your family to help you answer for your actual situation, not something this handbook, a coach, a teammate, or Rep1 can resolve for you in general terms. Rep1 can help you organise your questions and understand which office to approach, but does not make transfer, enrollment, financial, or eligibility decisions on your behalf, and does not guarantee any particular outcome.

**Staying Grounded in Your Values and Your Why**

Somewhere back at the start of this whole process, you had a reason for pursuing American football. Over months or years of training, travel, homesickness, academic pressure, and the day-to-day grind, it's easy to lose direct contact with that original reason, even while still doing all the work that reason originally justified.

A few honest questions worth revisiting periodically, not just once:
• Why did I actually want to do this, in my own words, not the version I'd give in an interview?
• Whose expectations am I currently living up to: mine, my family's, my coach's, or some mix, and am I comfortable with that mix?
• What would I want someone to say about who I was during this experience, separate from any stats or results?
• Am I making decisions right now based on what I actually value, or based on fear of disappointing someone, or based on what I think I'm supposed to want?

Values don't need to be complicated to be useful. A short, honest list, things like family, faith, education, integrity, resilience, or community, works better as a real decision-making anchor than a long, impressive-sounding one you don't actually use.

How to actually use this day to day:
• When a decision feels hard (playing through discomfort, whether to transfer, how to handle a conflict with a coach or teammate), check it against your own values list before deciding, not just against what seems expected of you.
• Revisit your "why" during hard stretches specifically, homesickness, an injury, a demotion, a difficult semester, since that's exactly when it's easiest to lose contact with it and hardest to remember to check.
• Notice if your own words about why you're doing this have quietly become someone else's words. That's worth naming to yourself honestly, not ignoring.
• Staying grounded in your values doesn't mean giving less effort. Some of the steadiest, most resilient competitors are the ones most anchored to something bigger than the outcome of any single game or season.

This isn't a one-time exercise. Revisiting it every so often, especially during a hard stretch, is far more useful than doing it once and filing it away.

Watch for these. They cover most of the avoidable problems that come up around identity, outlook, and life beyond football.

None of this replaces your coaching staff, your academic advisor, your DSO, or your family. It's meant to make sure that whatever happens with your athletic career, you're not starting from zero on everything else.

## Section 6: NIL, Taxes, Transfers, Graduation, and Returning Home

### Module 24 — Understand Name, Image, and Likeness (NIL) as an International Student-Athlete

NIL, or Name, Image, and Likeness, is a real opportunity many student-athletes in the United States now have: the chance to be paid for their own name, image, or likeness, separate from any athletic scholarship. For an Australian student-athlete competing on a student visa, that opportunity comes with a genuine complication a domestic teammate does not have to think about, because U.S. immigration law and NCAA, NAIA, or NJCAA rules are two entirely separate systems, run by different authorities, that do not automatically agree with each other. This module walks through what NIL actually means, how your governing body and your institution treat it, why the specific question of whether a compensated NIL activity is safe for an F-1 student remains genuinely unresolved at the federal level, what to look for in any NIL contract, who to actually ask for help, and a simple, repeatable process for pausing and getting the right advice before you act. None of this is designed to make you afraid of NIL. It is designed to make sure that if an opportunity comes your way, you know exactly how to slow down, ask the right questions, and protect yourself before you say yes.

**Why NIL Is Different for International Student-Athletes**

Name, Image, and Likeness (NIL) has opened up a genuine opportunity for student-athletes in the United States: the chance to earn compensation for your own name, image, or likeness, separate from athletic scholarships. For a domestic teammate, that opportunity mostly comes down to governing-body rules, school policy, and good judgment.

For you, as an Australian student-athlete competing on a student visa (most commonly F-1 status), there is an additional layer that a domestic teammate simply does not have to think about: whether accepting compensation for a NIL activity is consistent with the terms of your visa. That question is not settled by your governing body, your school, or your coach, because none of them has authority over U.S. immigration law.

This module will not turn you into an immigration lawyer, and it does not need to. What it will do is help you recognise three separate questions that are easy to blur together:
• Does your governing body and your school allow this NIL activity?
• Does your contract and compensation arrangement make sense?
• Does accepting this activity or payment create any risk under your visa status?

The first two questions have real, findable answers. The third one, for F-1 students, currently does not have a clear public answer from any U.S. government authority, and Section 4 of this module explains exactly why. Because of that, the single most important skill this module teaches is not a rule to memorise. It is knowing when to pause, and exactly who to ask, before you perform an activity or accept a payment. Getting an opportunity wrong under NCAA or NAIA rules usually costs you eligibility. Getting it wrong under immigration law can put your entire ability to study and compete in the United States at risk. That difference is why this module exists, and why it treats the international-student side of NIL as seriously as it does.

**What NIL Is and Is Not**

NIL stands for Name, Image, and Likeness: the parts of your identity that a brand, business, or individual can pay you to use for a genuine promotional or commercial purpose. According to the NCAA, an NIL deal exists when you are compensated, in money, products, or services, for allowing a company, brand, or person to use your name, image, or likeness for something like a social media post, an appearance, or an endorsement.

Common examples a domestic athlete may encounter include:
• Paid social media posts or content for a brand
• Autograph signings, camps, or clinics you host
• Appearances in commercials or promotional events
• Endorsement or sponsorship agreements

For a genuine NIL arrangement to be allowed, a few things generally need to be true at the same time:
• You are being paid specifically for the use of your name, image, or likeness, not simply for playing
• The deal has a real business purpose tied to an actual product, service, or event
• The compensation is reasonable for someone with a similar profile, not inflated
• You are allowed to use an agent or marketing professional to help arrange NIL activity

What NIL is not, and what remains prohibited regardless of how a deal is labelled:
• Payment with no real promotional work attached
• A vague promise that your NIL will be used "at some point," with no defined plan
• Pay-for-play: being compensated to attend a school, join a team, or for your on-field performance itself
• Compensation clearly outside the normal range for a comparable deal

The distinction that matters most is this: NIL compensates you for your identity and your promotional value. It does not, and cannot, compensate you simply for playing your sport. A deal that looks like NIL on paper but is really disguised pay-for-play or a recruiting inducement is not protected as NIL, no matter what it is called in the paperwork.

One more distinction matters specifically for you. Everything above describes what NIL means as a general governing-body concept, mostly written with a domestic student-athlete in mind. For an F-1 student, the immigration analysis of any specific NIL activity remains a separate question, covered in full in Section 4. An activity being labelled NIL, and being genuinely compliant with your governing body's rules, does not by itself make that activity safe under immigration law. Keep that distinction in mind as you read the rest of this module: governing-body rules answer whether an activity is legitimate NIL. They do not answer whether you, specifically, are authorised to be paid for it.

**Governing-Body and Institutional NIL Rules**

Your NIL rules depend on which governing body your school competes under: the NCAA, the NAIA, or the NJCAA. Conferences and individual institutions can add their own requirements on top of that. There is no single, universal NIL rulebook that applies to every U.S. college athlete. You need to know which rules actually apply to you.

NCAA. As of this module's most recent research, NCAA student-athletes must disclose qualifying NIL deals through NIL Go, the College Sports Commission's compliance platform, once compensation from a single payer reaches $600 in total. The exact reporting window depends on your situation. As examples: a high school prospect heading to Division I must report within 14 days of starting full-time classes or before their first Division I game, whichever comes first; a two-year college student-athlete moving to Division I reports the same way once enrolled; and if you transfer between Division I schools, your five-business-day reporting obligation for new or changed deals continues right through the transfer process. These figures and windows are exactly the kind of detail that changes, so confirm the current threshold and deadline with your athletics compliance office or the NIL Go platform directly rather than relying on this handbook for an exact number.

NAIA. The NAIA was the first collegiate association to adopt NIL rules, in 2020, and continues to allow student-athletes to earn compensation through endorsements, appearances, social media partnerships, camps, and autograph signings without losing eligibility, provided the activity fits the same genuine-NIL, not-pay-for-play framework described in Section 2.

NJCAA. At the time of this module's research, a directly verifiable, current NJCAA NIL bylaw page could not be confirmed. Do not treat any NJCAA-specific figure or deadline in this section as reliable. If you compete under NJCAA rules, confirm your school's current NIL requirements directly with your athletics compliance office before entering into any arrangement.

Whichever governing body applies to you, a few things stay true across all three:
• Institutional NIL offices can add their own disclosure or review requirements on top of your governing body's rules, and those requirements vary school to school. Confirm your own institution's process directly, not just the governing-body baseline.
• NIL rules exist to protect your eligibility, and they change on a real cycle. What is accurate today may not be accurate next season. Always confirm the current rule before acting, not just before your first deal.
• Meeting your governing body's NIL requirements, and getting your institution's sign-off, tells you that an activity is compliant with athletics rules. It tells you nothing about whether the activity is authorised under your visa. Those are two separate approvals, from two separate authorities, and Section 4 explains why that distinction matters so much for you specifically.

Do not blend NCAA, NAIA, and NJCAA rules together in your own head, and do not assume a rule you heard about from a teammate at a different kind of school applies to you. Confirm your own governing body's current rule, from your own athletics compliance office, every time.

**F-1 Status and NIL: The Unresolved Question**

This is the most important section in this module, so read it carefully even if you skim the rest.

Here is the direct, current situation. No U.S. government authority, including the Department of Homeland Security, U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS), or the Student and Exchange Visitor Program (SEVP), has issued a clear public rule stating whether an F-1 student may lawfully accept compensation for NIL activity. This is not a case of the rule being hard to find. Multiple independent legal sources confirm that federal immigration authorities have not directly addressed NIL compensation for F-1 students, and have indicated only that the issue continues to be reviewed. That silence is the actual, current state of the law, not a gap in this handbook's research.

Because F-1 status only permits specific, pre-approved categories of employment (the underlying federal regulation is 8 CFR 214.2(f)), and NIL is not one of the categories that regulation currently lists, accepting compensated NIL activity while on F-1 status carries a real, currently unresolved risk of being treated as unauthorised employment. Unauthorised employment is a serious immigration violation. This module cannot tell you that this risk does not apply to you, and it cannot tell you that all compensated NIL activity is automatically prohibited either. Neither claim would be honest, because no controlling authority has settled the question either way.

What definitely does not resolve this question, no matter how reasonable it might sound:
• Your governing body allowing the NIL activity. Governing-body permission is not immigration permission.
• Your school or athletics compliance office approving the deal. Institutional approval is not immigration permission.
• Having a signed contract. A signed contract is not immigration permission, and does not protect your visa status.
• The brand calling the payment a "gift" instead of compensation. Relabelling the payment does not settle the issue.
• The income being described as "passive," such as royalties or a licensing fee. That label does not automatically resolve the question either.
• The activity happening online or remotely. Online or remote activity does not automatically remove the immigration risk.
• How you are paid, whether by direct transfer, a platform, gift card, or product. Payment method does not determine legality by itself.
• A teammate, another international athlete, or someone you know having done something similar without a problem. Someone else's experience, including a successful one, is not legal clearance for your situation.

Do not look for a workaround to any of this. Do not consider routing payment through a family member, a friend, another country, a company, or a platform to avoid the question. That does not remove the underlying risk, and it can create new problems of its own.

These separate questions are easy to blur together, so keep them apart in your own mind:
• Athletics eligibility: whether the activity is allowed under your governing body's rules. Athletics compliance answers this.
• Institutional policy: whether your specific school requires disclosure or review. Your institution's NIL office answers this.
• Immigration status and employment authorization: whether accepting compensation is consistent with your F-1 status. No one at your school can fully answer this. It requires your DSO's general guidance and, in most cases, qualified immigration counsel.
• Contract obligations: what you are actually agreeing to and what happens if you cannot follow through. Covered in Section 5.
• Tax obligations: whether the compensation creates a filing or reporting requirement. Covered in the academy's tax module and by a qualified tax professional.

What to actually do: before performing any NIL activity or accepting any payment where your visa status could plausibly be relevant, which in practice means essentially any compensated NIL activity while you hold F-1 status, disclose the proposed activity to athletics compliance, and separately raise it with your Designated School Official (DSO) or international student office. Your DSO can explain your F-1 status and general processes, and can help you understand when a situation needs outside legal input, but your DSO does not replace qualified immigration counsel and cannot personally guarantee that a specific arrangement is lawful. Where the DSO recommends it, or where the activity involves real money and real risk, seek advice from a qualified immigration attorney, and do not assume any attorney is automatically qualified in immigration, contract, or NIL matters specifically; confirm their relevant experience. Unless an attorney has been formally retained and has actually reviewed your specific situation, do not treat any informal comment, including from Rep1, a coach, a teammate, or an agent, as legal clearance.

The governing principle for this entire module is this: when immigration authorisation is uncertain, pause before performing the activity or accepting payment. Uncertainty is the normal starting position for an F-1 student considering NIL right now, not a sign that you have done something wrong. Pausing to ask the right people the right questions is the responsible response to that uncertainty, not an overreaction to it.

**Contracts, Compensation, and Disclosure**

Module 5 already covered the fundamentals of reading a contract and understanding representation. This section builds on that directly for NIL agreements specifically, so it assumes you already know the basics of reading terms carefully and not signing under pressure.

Before signing or verbally agreeing to any NIL arrangement, work through what the deal actually involves:
• Who is offering the deal, and is it a real business or individual you can verify
• What you are actually required to do in exchange, specifically, not just generally
• What the brand or business receives from you
• The exact compensation: amount, and whether it is cash, product, or services
• When you actually get paid, and whether payment is tied to milestones
• How long the agreement lasts
• Whether the agreement is exclusive, meaning it stops you from doing similar deals with anyone else
• What content or social media obligations you have, and how many posts, appearances, or deliverables are expected
• How your image or likeness can be used, and for how long, including after the agreement ends
• What happens if either side wants to cancel or ends the agreement early
• What you are required to disclose to your governing body or institution, and by when
• What records you need to keep
• Whether the compensation creates a tax obligation
• Whether your visa status makes this specific activity a question for your DSO or immigration counsel before you proceed, per Section 4

A few practical rules that protect you regardless of how any of the above questions turn out:
• A contract can create real obligations even if you later realise the activity should not go ahead, including under your visa status. That is exactly why it matters to work through Section 4's questions before you sign, not after.
• Do not sign first and ask questions later. Read and understand the agreement, disclose it where required, and get any needed advice, before you commit.
• Keep your own written records: the offer, the signed agreement, disclosure confirmations, and payment records. Do not rely only on verbal promises or a text message summary of what you were told.
• If you use an agent or NIL representative, do not assume they are protecting every interest you have. An agent can help you negotiate commercial terms, but is not a substitute for your own athletics compliance disclosure, your own DSO conversation, or your own qualified legal or tax advice. Confirm any agent-registration or representation requirements your governing body or state may have.
• Keep the disclosure and reporting requirements from Section 3 in mind. A contract being signed does not remove your disclosure obligation.

This handbook cannot tell you whether a specific contract you are offered is enforceable, fair, or in your interest, and it is not a substitute for having an actual attorney review a real agreement before you sign something with real money or real obligations attached. What it can do is make sure you are asking the right questions before that point, not after.

**Who to Ask: What Each Role Can and Cannot Decide**

Different people and offices can help you with different pieces of a NIL decision. None of them can answer every question, and knowing which one to approach saves you time and protects you from relying on the wrong kind of advice.

Student (you)
You may: gather the paperwork, ask questions, disclose the proposed activity to the right offices, pause before accepting payment when anything is uncertain, seek qualified advice, and keep your own records.
You may not: authorise your own employment under immigration law, assume your school's approval settles your immigration status, or treat informal advice from a friend, teammate, or agent as legal clearance.

Coach
May: encourage you to disclose the activity, point you toward athletics compliance and your DSO, and support your athletic planning.
May not: approve a NIL deal under immigration law, interpret visa rules for you, give legal or tax advice, or guarantee any eligibility or earnings outcome.

Athletics compliance
May: explain your governing body's rules, explain your institution's NIL policy, explain what you must disclose and by when, and review the athletics-eligibility side of a proposed deal.
May not: authorise employment under immigration law, give immigration legal advice, or replace your DSO or immigration counsel.

DSO or international student office
May: explain your F-1 status and general requirements, explain school reporting and SEVIS-related processes, and tell you when a situation needs outside legal advice.
May not: guarantee that a specific NIL arrangement is lawful, replace individualised legal advice, or approve your athletics eligibility.

Attorney
May: provide individualised legal advice within their actual area of competence, once formally retained, including reviewing immigration or contract risk.
Not every attorney is experienced in immigration, contracts, or NIL specifically. Confirm their relevant experience, and do not treat a comment from an attorney as representation unless you have actually retained them for this matter.

Tax professional
May: help you understand reporting requirements, review how your income is characterised, and assist with filing and recordkeeping.
May not: authorise employment under immigration law or approve athletics eligibility.

NIL representative or agent
May: help you negotiate and understand commercial terms, within applicable law and your contract.
May not: determine immigration legality, replace athletics compliance, replace qualified legal or tax advice, or guarantee earnings.

Rep1
May: provide general education about NIL and the questions that matter, help you prepare questions for the right office or professional, help you identify which office or professional you actually need, and encourage disclosure and good recordkeeping.
May not: approve a NIL activity, authorise employment, give immigration advice, give tax advice, give legal advice, act as your athletics compliance office, act as your DSO, or guarantee any outcome.

If you are ever unsure which of these to approach first for a specific question, start with athletics compliance for anything about eligibility or disclosure, and your DSO for anything touching your visa status. Both can help point you toward a qualified attorney or tax professional if your situation needs one.

**Common Mistakes**

Watch for these. They cover most of the avoidable problems that come up around NIL for international student-athletes.
• "The NCAA allows it, so my visa allows it." Governing-body permission and immigration permission are two separate things.
• "My school approved it, so it is legal." Institutional approval does not resolve your immigration status.
• "It is online, so it does not count as work." Remote or online activity does not automatically remove the immigration risk.
• "It is passive income, so it is automatically safe." A passive-income label does not settle the question either.
• "The brand called it a gift, so there is no risk." Relabelling compensation does not change what it actually is.
• "My teammate did it, so I can do it." Someone else's experience, including a successful one, is not legal clearance for you.
• "An agent said it was fine, so I do not need the DSO." An agent cannot determine immigration legality, and does not replace your DSO or immigration counsel.
• "I can accept payment first and ask questions later." Pause before performing the activity or accepting payment, not after.
• "A contract protects my immigration status." A signed contract does not protect your visa status.
• "Rep1 can approve the deal for me." Rep1 can help you ask the right questions and find the right office or professional. Rep1 cannot approve a NIL activity, authorise employment, or give you legal, tax, or immigration advice.

Before you act on any NIL opportunity, work through this decision process:
1. Identify the proposed activity clearly: what you would actually be doing.
2. Identify exactly what you are being asked to do in exchange.
3. Identify whether compensation or any other benefit is involved, including products, services, or anything described as a gift.
4. Get the offer or contract in writing before agreeing to anything.
5. Disclose the proposed activity to athletics compliance.
6. Discuss the immigration implications with your DSO.
7. Get qualified immigration or legal advice where the situation calls for it.
8. Get qualified tax guidance where the situation calls for it.
9. Do not perform the activity or accept payment until you actually understand the risk.
10. Keep records of the advice you received, the disclosures you made, the contract, and any payments.

This decision process will not give you an instant yes or no, and it is not supposed to. It is a way of making sure you have asked the right people the right questions before you act, not a checklist that produces automatic approval once you have ticked every box. The one principle worth carrying with you through every NIL opportunity you are offered: when immigration authorisation is uncertain, pause before performing the activity or accepting payment. Slowing down here protects far more than a single deal. It protects your ability to keep studying and competing in the United States at all.

**Key Takeaways**

NIL lets you earn compensation for your own name, image, and likeness, but it is a different question from being paid to play, and a deal that looks like NIL but has no real promotional substance is not protected as NIL under any governing body's rules. Your governing body, NCAA, NAIA, or NJCAA, and your institution set the rules for disclosure and eligibility, and those rules can change and vary by school, so always confirm the current requirement with athletics compliance rather than relying on a fixed figure. Separately, and most importantly, no U.S. government authority has issued a clear public rule on whether an F-1 student may lawfully accept compensated NIL activity, and nothing, not governing-body permission, institutional approval, a signed contract, a "gift" label, a "passive income" label, online activity, or someone else's experience, resolves that uncertainty on its own. Before signing anything, understand exactly what a contract requires of you and keep your own written records. Athletics compliance, your DSO, an attorney, a tax professional, an NIL representative, and Rep1 can each help with a different piece of this, but none of them can authorise your employment under immigration law, and Rep1 specifically cannot approve a NIL activity for you. When immigration authorisation is uncertain, the responsible move is to pause before performing the activity or accepting payment, disclose it to the right people, and get the advice you need first.

**Next Action**

Build a NIL Review File before you act on any NIL opportunity. Include: the written offer or a summary of the activity and compensation being proposed; a plain description of exactly what you would be required to do; confirmation of whether you have disclosed the activity to athletics compliance, and when; your notes from a conversation with your DSO about the immigration side of the activity; the name of any attorney or tax professional you have been referred to, if your situation calls for one; and a simple record of any payments, contracts, or advice you receive as you go. Building this file does not mean the activity is approved. It means you have the information you actually need before deciding whether, and how, to move forward, and exactly who to ask if you are still unsure.

### Module 25 — Understand Your U.S. Tax Obligations as an International Student-Athlete

Money, benefits, and NIL compensation you receive while competing and studying in the United States can raise real U.S. tax questions, separate from your visa status and separate from athletics eligibility. This module will not tell you what to file. It will help you recognise when a tax question exists, understand a few key ideas (tax residency, common forms, the federal, state, and local distinction, and the U.S.-Australia tax treaty), and build the habits and records a qualified tax professional will actually need. Nothing here replaces that professional's advice for your own situation.

**Why U.S. Taxes Matter for International Student-Athletes**

It is easy to assume tax questions only apply to people who think of themselves as employees or business owners. That is not how it works for an international student-athlete in the United States. Wages, prize money, stipends, scholarships, free products, travel, housing, and NIL compensation can all raise tax questions, even if none of it feels like a job.

The IRS treats nonresident alien students temporarily present in the U.S. on an F, J, M, or Q visa as engaged in a U.S. trade or business, which is the starting point for whether a tax filing obligation exists. A tax question can exist even if you never receive a form for it, and even if the money is paid into an Australian or other foreign account. Neither of those things automatically removes a U.S. tax question.

It helps to keep three separate boxes in mind: your immigration status, your athletics eligibility, and your tax obligations. Athletics compliance and your DSO can each explain their own box. Neither of them, and no coach, and no governing body, can tell you your tax position. That is a separate question with its own rules, covered in this module.

This module will not calculate your tax residency or tell you which forms to file. Its job is to help you recognise when a question exists, understand a few core ideas, and build the habits, records, and questions that let a qualified tax professional actually help you when the time comes.

**Tax Status Is Not the Same as Immigration Status**

This is the most important idea in this module: your visa category and your tax classification are two different things, decided under two different sets of rules.

F-1 is an immigration classification. It says something about why you are allowed to be in the United States. It does not, by itself, decide whether the IRS treats you as a nonresident alien or a resident alien for tax purposes. That is a tax-law question, and it depends on your own facts, not your visa label.

One of the tools the IRS uses to decide U.S. tax residency is called the substantial presence test. In general terms, it counts the days you were physically present in the United States over a three-year window, using a set formula. Some categories of visa holders, including some full-time students on an F, J, M, or Q visa who genuinely comply with their visa's requirements, can exclude certain days from that count for a period of time under a rule the IRS calls the "exempt individual" rule. If you want to exclude those days, you generally need to file Form 8843 to explain the basis for doing so, whether or not you otherwise have to file a tax return.

None of this is permanent, and none of it is universal. Whether you currently qualify for that exclusion, for how long, and what your tax residency actually is this year, depends on your own history of days in the U.S. and your own facts. This module will not calculate that for you, for a hypothetical student or for you personally. If you need to know your own tax residency, that is a question for a qualified tax professional, not something to work out from your visa category alone.

The one habit worth building now: do not assume you know your tax status just because you know your visa status. They are answered by different rules, and getting either one wrong by assuming it matches the other is exactly how avoidable problems start.

**What Money and Benefits Can Create Tax Questions**

A wide range of money and benefits can raise a U.S. tax question. Wages. Stipends. Scholarships and fellowship grants. Prize money. NIL compensation. Appearance fees. Payment for social media posts. Free products. Housing, meals, or travel provided instead of cash. Reimbursements. Something a brand calls a "gift." Payments made through an online platform. Money paid into an Australian or other foreign account.

The label attached to a payment does not decide its tax treatment. Calling something a gift does not automatically remove it from consideration. Calling something passive income does not automatically mean it is taxed lightly, or at all, without a proper look at the facts. And not receiving a tax form for a payment does not automatically mean there is no obligation attached to it. There is no minimum dollar amount that triggers a filing requirement for a nonresident alien student, unlike the higher thresholds that can apply to U.S. citizens and residents.

Scholarships and fellowship grants are a good example of why facts matter more than labels. The portion of a scholarship that pays for tuition and the fees, books, supplies, and equipment your course actually requires can be treated differently from the portion that covers room, board, travel, or other living costs, or that pays you for teaching, research, or other services as a condition of the grant. An award letter is not a complete tax analysis by itself.

NIL compensation deserves the same care. Receiving NIL income can raise questions about how it is reported, whether it looks like independent-contractor income, whether self-employment tax applies, and whether estimated payments are expected during the year. None of those questions has one universal answer for every student. As one useful data point: a nonresident alien who has not become a U.S. resident is generally not subject to U.S. self-employment tax in the first place, though that can change if your own tax residency changes. That is exactly the kind of detail a qualified tax professional should confirm for your specific situation rather than something to assume either way.

The practical habit that matters more than memorising every category: write down what you actually received, and what, if anything, you were asked to do in exchange for it. That single habit does more to protect you than trying to guess the tax answer yourself.

**Tax Forms and Information Documents**

A few documents come up often enough that it helps to recognise them, without needing to become an expert in any of them.

Form W-2 reports wages from an employer. Form 1099-NEC and Form 1099-MISC report certain non-employee payments, which can include some NIL-related compensation. Form 1099-K can report payments processed through a third-party platform. Form 1042-S reports certain U.S.-source income paid to a nonresident alien, including some scholarship, fellowship, and treaty-related amounts. Form 8843 is the form used to explain excluded days under the "exempt individual" rule discussed earlier, and it is not itself an income tax return. Form 1040-NR is the actual nonresident alien income tax return, used when a nonresident alien has income that requires filing. Form W-8BEN is a form a payer may ask a nonresident alien to complete to document foreign status and, sometimes, a treaty position, and it should never be signed or submitted without understanding what it is certifying. If anyone ever asks you to complete a Form W-9, treat that as a signal to pause, because a W-9 certifies U.S. status, and completing one without understanding why is a common, avoidable mistake for a nonresident alien.

A few things are true across all of these. Some of these documents report information; they are not, by themselves, a tax return. Some are the actual return. Some certify a status rather than reporting an amount. Receiving a form does not automatically settle its correct tax treatment, and not receiving one does not automatically mean there is nothing to report. Before you rely on any form, check that your name, taxpayer identification number, the payment amount, and the payer's information are actually correct on it. Not every form in this list applies to every student, and this list is not instructions for which ones you personally need to file. That is a question for a qualified tax professional, using your actual documents.

**Federal, State, and Local Tax Considerations**

Federal tax and state tax are two separate systems, and in some places local tax is a third layer on top of both. Your school's state is not automatically the only state that matters. If you study in one state, compete or earn money in another, or work remotely for someone based elsewhere, you can end up with more than one state's tax rules to think about, and doing something online or remotely does not automatically remove a state's interest in it.

A U.S. tax treaty operates at the federal level. Some U.S. states honor the terms of a federal tax treaty, and some do not, so a treaty benefit that reduces or removes a federal tax question will not necessarily do the same thing at the state level. That is worth knowing before you assume a treaty "settles" everything.

This module will not build you a state-by-state guide, and it will not state a specific state's threshold or rule as if it applied to every student, because that kind of detail changes and needs to be checked against a current, direct source for your own situation. If your circumstances touch more than one state, that is exactly the kind of question to bring to a qualified tax professional, who can look at all of the states actually involved rather than just the one where your school happens to be.

**The U.S.-Australia Tax Treaty**

The United States and Australia have had an income tax treaty in force since 1983, updated by a protocol in 2001. A tax treaty like this one can reduce or remove U.S. tax on certain, specific categories of income for Australian residents, but it does that article by article, not as one blanket exemption covering everything you might receive.

One relevant provision is the treaty's Students article. In plain terms, it says that if you are an Australian resident (or were one immediately before coming to study in the U.S.) and you are temporarily in the U.S. for full-time study, payments you receive from sources outside the U.S., such as Australia, for your maintenance or education, are exempt from U.S. tax. Read that carefully: it covers money sent to you from outside the U.S. for study or living costs. It does not, on its own, cover wages you earn in the U.S., NIL compensation from a U.S. source, or a scholarship paid by your U.S. institution. Those are different categories of income and need to be checked against their own rules, not assumed to be covered by this article.

More broadly, claiming any treaty benefit does not automatically mean you have no U.S. tax return to file. A treaty position can itself require disclosure on your return, and a treaty benefit at the federal level does not automatically apply at the state level, as covered in the previous section. A treaty also has nothing to do with your immigration status. Treaty relief is a tax outcome, not an immigration authorisation, and it does not resolve any question about whether an activity was permitted under your visa.

This module will not apply a treaty article to your own situation, or promise you a specific exemption amount. If you think a treaty benefit might apply to income you have received, that is a question to bring to a qualified tax professional, with your actual facts in front of them, not something to conclude on your own from a general description like this one.

**Recordkeeping and Preparing for Professional Help**

You do not need to know the tax answer yourself. You do need to keep the records that let someone qualified work it out. Keep copies of: W-2s, 1099s, and 1042-S forms; scholarship or fellowship award letters; NIL agreements and any related payment statements; bank and payment-platform records; receipts and expense records, including travel and housing; prior tax returns you have filed; any visa or entry records a professional specifically asks you for; copies of any tax forms you submit; and any written advice you receive from a tax professional.

A simple habit that pays off: for every payment or benefit you receive, write down who paid you, how much, on what date, what kind of payment it was, what you actually did (if anything) in exchange, which tax form (if any) you received for it, which U.S. state the payment is connected to, whether it involved providing a service, and any related expenses. Bring that file, plus your questions, to a qualified tax professional.

Organise this before filing season starts, not during it. A tax professional can only work with the complete picture you actually give them. Your school's payroll or finance office can explain a form it issued to you, but it is not responsible for your whole filing picture, especially income from outside the school, like NIL deals or platform payments. Never leave a payment out of your own records just because no form was issued for it. And once you do file, keep a copy of the return itself alongside your supporting records.

**Common Mistakes**

Watch for these. They cover most of the avoidable problems that come up around U.S. taxes for international student-athletes.
• "I am on an F-1 visa, so I automatically know my tax status." Your visa and your tax classification are decided by different rules.
• "I did not receive a tax form, so I do not need to report anything." A missing form does not automatically mean there is nothing to report.
• "The money went to my Australian account, so it is not a U.S. tax issue." Where the money lands does not decide whether it is U.S.-source income.
• "My scholarship is automatically tax-free." Whether a scholarship is tax-free depends on what it pays for and your own facts, not just the label.
• "The brand called it a gift, so it is not taxable." Relabelling a payment does not change what it actually is.
• "The platform withheld money, so my taxes are finished." Withholding is not the same as a completed, correct tax filing.
• "A treaty means I do not have to file." A treaty can reduce or remove tax on specific income; it does not automatically remove your filing obligation.
• "My teammate used this form, so I should use it too." Another student's forms and facts are not yours.
• "My school approved the NIL deal, so the tax and immigration issues are solved." Institutional approval does not resolve either question.
• "Rep1 can tell me exactly what to file." Rep1 can help you get organised and find the right professional. Rep1 cannot determine your tax residency, prepare your return, or give you individualised tax advice.
• "Filing taxes makes the NIL activity legal under immigration law." Tax filing and immigration authorisation are separate questions; filing does not resolve the other.
• "I can wait until the deadline to organize my records." Waiting makes mistakes and missed information more likely, not less.

Before you try to work out your own tax picture, or hand anything to a professional, work through this process:
1. Gather all your tax and payment documents.
2. List every source of money, benefits, prizes, stipends, scholarships, and NIL compensation you have received.
3. Record the date, amount, payer, activity, and payment method for each one.
4. Separate school-issued forms from outside-payment records.
5. Keep your scholarship and expense documents together.
6. Identify every state connected to your work, school, residence, or payments.
7. Do not guess your own tax residency or treaty eligibility.
8. Ask a qualified tax professional which forms and returns actually apply to you.
9. Keep copies of filed returns and supporting records.
10. Track future deadlines and any estimated-payment questions that may apply.

The one principle worth carrying with you through all of this: filing taxes does not determine whether an activity was permitted under immigration law, and nothing in this process is designed to give you an instant yes or no. It is designed to make sure you have gathered the right information and asked the right person before you assume you know the answer.

**Key Takeaways**

Receiving money or benefits in the United States, including wages, scholarships, stipends, prize money, and NIL compensation, can raise real U.S. tax questions, separate from your visa status and separate from your athletics eligibility. Your immigration classification and your tax classification are decided under different rules, and neither one should be assumed from the other. A wide range of income types, forms, and situations can apply to you, but none of the specific categories, labels, or treaty provisions covered in this module resolve your own tax position by themselves, including the U.S.-Australia tax treaty's narrow Students article, which only covers certain payments from outside the U.S. for maintenance or education. Good recordkeeping, not guessing, is what actually protects you: keep your forms, agreements, payment records, and correspondence organised, and bring the complete picture to a qualified tax professional rather than trying to work out your own residency, treaty eligibility, or filing obligation from general information alone.

**Next Action**

Build a Tax Preparation File before tax season arrives. Include every payment or benefit you have received (wages, scholarships, stipends, prizes, NIL compensation, products, housing, travel), the tax forms connected to each one, your scholarship or NIL documents, every U.S. state connected to your work, school, or payments, and a list of questions for a qualified tax professional. Completing this file does not determine your tax residency, your treaty eligibility, or your final filing obligation. It means you will have the information a qualified tax professional actually needs, and you will know exactly who to ask when you are unsure.

### Module 26 — Understand the Transfer Portal as an International Student-Athlete

Deciding whether to change schools is one of the biggest decisions a student-athlete can make. It touches your athletics, your academics, your finances, and, because you are an international student, your immigration status too. This module explains what the transfer portal actually is, what entering it does and does not mean, how governing-body and institutional rules differ, and why the athletics side of a transfer and the F-1/SEVIS side of a transfer are separate processes that both need to be managed correctly. It will not tell you whether you should transfer, and it will not resolve your own eligibility, admission, aid, or immigration outcome. It will help you ask the right questions, in the right order, before you act.

**Why Transfer Decisions Require Careful Planning**

A transfer can affect far more than which team you play for. It can affect your academic progress, your financial aid, your housing, your graduation timeline, and, because you hold a student visa, your immigration status. These are separate systems, run by separate offices, and none of them automatically follows along when one of the others changes.

Entering the transfer portal or a similar notification process can also change your relationship with your current team and institution. Once you notify your school of your intent to transfer, that decision is visible to your coaches and, depending on your governing body's rules, may become visible to other schools as well. It is not something you can quietly test and take back without any consequence.

This is exactly why the decision deserves real planning rather than a fast reaction. Frustration with playing time, a single disappointing conversation with a coach, or attention from another school on social media are common reasons students consider transferring, but none of them tell you whether a transfer is actually the right move for your degree, your finances, or your visa status. This module will not tell you that transferring is good or bad. Its job is to help you understand the consequences across every domain before you take the first step, and to remind you that different governing bodies and different institutions can handle the same situation differently.

**What the Transfer Portal Is and Is Not**

In the NCAA, the Transfer Portal is a centralised database where student-athletes who intend to transfer are listed. When you notify your current school that you want to transfer, your school enters your name in the Portal. Being listed signals to other schools that you are available and opens the door to contact from coaches. Other governing bodies use their own notification or tracking processes, which are not identical to the NCAA's Portal; the exact system that applies to you depends on your governing body, your division or association, your sport, and your institution.

It is just as important to understand what portal or notification entry does not do. It does not mean you have been admitted anywhere. It does not mean you are athletically eligible at another school. It does not mean a scholarship is waiting for you. It does not preserve your current roster spot. It does not guarantee any school will actually contact you. It does not transfer your immigration record, and it does not authorise you to work or study anywhere new. None of these things happen automatically just because your name appears in a database, and none of them are reversible without consequence once other people, like your current coaches, know you intend to leave.

Think of portal or notification entry as the start of a process, not the end of one. What happens next, whether another school reaches out, whether you are admitted, whether you receive aid, whether you are eligible to compete, and whether your visa status transfers cleanly, are each answered separately, by separate offices, using separate rules covered later in this module.

**Governing-Body and Institutional Transfer Rules**

NCAA, NAIA, and NJCAA transfer rules are not the same, and you should never assume one governing body's process applies to you if you compete under a different one. Rules in this area also change often, sometimes with very little notice, so treat anything you read, including this module, as a starting point to confirm, not a final answer.

For NCAA student-athletes, transfer rules and eligibility guidance are published by the NCAA and organised by division, since Division I, II, and III each have their own guides, flowcharts, and manuals. The NCAA's own transfer guidance is explicit that questions about your specific situation should go to the athletics compliance office at your current school or the school you plan to attend, not to a general handbook. Rules in this area can change quickly: in April 2026, the NCAA's Division I Cabinet adopted new rules penalising programs that sign a transfer student-athlete, add one to a roster, or allow one to participate in athletically related activities before that student is actually entered into the Transfer Portal, with automatic penalties for the coach and program involved, applying to transfers on or after 25 February 2026. That single change is a useful reminder that a rule you read about last season may already be out of date.

For NAIA and NJCAA student-athletes, this module cannot currently confirm the specific, current transfer and eligibility procedure directly from an official NAIA or NJCAA source. Do not assume the NCAA's Transfer Portal process, timing, or penalties described above apply to you if you compete under NAIA or NJCAA rules; they are separate associations with their own rules. If you are an NAIA or NJCAA student-athlete considering a transfer, the current, correct process must be confirmed directly with your athletics-compliance office, since this is exactly the kind of detail that changes and needs to be checked against a current, official source for your own situation.

Whichever governing body applies to you, remember that your conference and your own institution may add further procedures or requirements on top of the governing body's rules, and that a coach, even a well-meaning one, is not the final authority on transfer or eligibility rules. Confirm the current rule with your compliance office before you act on anything you have heard informally.

**Academic Eligibility, Admission, and Transfer Credits**

A transfer touches four separate things that are easy to blur together: whether a coach wants you, whether you are admitted, whether your credits transfer, and whether you are athletically eligible to compete. Each one is decided by a different office, using different criteria, and none of them guarantees the others.

A coach's interest in you is not an admission decision. Admission is made by the receiving institution's own admissions office, using its own standards, and a coach cannot override that process or promise you a place. Being admitted, in turn, does not automatically make you athletics-eligible; athletics eligibility is assessed separately by the governing body and the institution's own athletics-compliance office, as covered in the previous section.

Transfer credits raise their own separate question. The destination institution's registrar or academic office evaluates which of your existing credits transfer and, just as importantly, whether those credits count toward the specific degree you intend to pursue there. Credits sometimes transfer as general elective credit rather than toward your intended major, which can extend the time it takes you to graduate even if the total number of credits looks similar on paper. No single institution's transfer-credit policy is universal, and this module cannot tell you how a specific school will treat your specific transcript.

Before you commit to a transfer, ask the receiving institution's admissions and academic offices for a written, specific answer about your own credits and your own intended programme, rather than relying on a general impression or a coach's description of how things usually work. Academic-progress requirements set by your governing body may also still apply and are worth confirming with athletics compliance at the same time.

**Scholarships, Financial Aid, and Roster Consequences**

A transfer can affect your athletic scholarship, other institutional aid, your housing and meal plan, your roster status, and your access to training, medical support, and other athletics benefits, and none of these outcomes should be assumed in either direction.

It helps to understand how athletics scholarships actually work in the first place. An athletics scholarship is awarded by a coach, not by the NCAA or any governing body; the governing body sets limits on how many scholarships a program can offer in a sport, but your own coach decides who receives one, how much it is worth, and whether it is renewed each year. Scholarships are typically awarded year to year, and renewal is not automatic. If a school plans to reduce or not renew your scholarship, NCAA rules require written notice by a set date, and you generally have the right to appeal that decision through your school's own process, so pay close attention to any written notice you receive and do not rely on a verbal conversation alone.

The same caution applies at a destination school. A coach's verbal interest or a promising conversation is not a financial-aid award, and it is not the same as a confirmed roster spot. A written financial-aid offer, once you receive one, should be read carefully, since most student-athletes receive partial aid rather than a full scholarship, and a scholarship may not cover every cost of attendance. Other aid, such as academic scholarships or need-based grants, can also form part of the full picture, so ask each financial-aid office for a complete summary of everything you may qualify for, not just an athletics scholarship figure.

Before you rely on anything a coach has told you, either at your current school or a destination school, get the financial-aid terms, the roster expectation, and any other benefit in writing from the appropriate office. A verbal promise is not a substitute for a written answer, and comparing schools on the full cost of attendance, not just the headline scholarship number, will give you a much more accurate picture.

**F-1 Status, SEVIS Transfer, and Timing**

This is the most important section in this module. Your athletics transfer and your F-1 school transfer are two separate processes, and neither one completes the other.

Entering the transfer portal, having a coach express interest, being admitted, or even becoming athletically eligible at a new school does not, by itself, transfer your SEVIS record. A coach cannot transfer your SEVIS record. Athletics compliance cannot transfer your SEVIS record. Only your current school's Designated School Official (DSO) can release your SEVIS record, and only your destination school's DSO can then take it up and issue you a new Form I-20, once you have actually been admitted there.

Timing genuinely matters here. Under the federal rules that govern F-1 school transfers, a transferring student generally cannot remain in the United States unless they are able to resume classes at the new school within five months of the SEVIS release date or their program's completion date, whichever is earlier; for a student already on post-completion Optional Practical Training, the comparison is instead to five months from transferring out or the date OPT authorisation ends, whichever is earlier. This module will not calculate that date for you and will not tell you when to ask your DSO to set your release date; that is a conversation to have directly with your current and destination DSOs, who can apply the current rule to your own timeline. One additional fact worth knowing now, even though the full detail belongs in a later part of this academy: if you are on post-completion Optional Practical Training, transferring schools or beginning study at a new educational level automatically ends that OPT employment authorisation.

None of the steps in this section resolve each other. A completed SEVIS transfer and a new Form I-20 do not make you athletically eligible, athletics eligibility does not complete your immigration transfer, and admission to a new school does not by itself finish the SEVIS process either. Before you finalise any transfer, speak directly with both your current and destination institutions' international student offices, and do not attempt to release, terminate, or otherwise change your own SEVIS record without their guidance.

**Evaluating Schools, Offers, and Fit**

A good transfer decision looks at the whole picture, not just the athletics side. Before you commit anywhere, work through the academic programme and whether it fits your goals, your transfer credits and how they apply to your intended degree, your realistic graduation timeline, the coaching staff and your likely position or role, the actual depth at your position and your realistic opportunity to play, the scholarship offer and the total cost of attendance, housing and meal plans, the support available to international students, the strength of the athletics-compliance office, medical and training support, the location and how easy it is to travel from, how well the team culture fits you, and how the school and its staff coordinate on visa and SEVIS matters.

It also helps to recognise that interest, a conversation, a conditional offer, admission, a written financial-aid offer, a confirmed roster spot, athletics eligibility, and a completed immigration transfer are all separate stages, not the same thing. A coach telling you they want you on the team is not the same as an admissions letter, a written scholarship offer, or a completed SEVIS transfer, and none of those stages should be assumed just because an earlier one has happened.

Ask direct questions of each school, and write down the answers rather than relying on memory. No one, including a coach at either school, can guarantee you playing time, and a written institutional decision always carries more weight than a verbal conversation.

**Common Mistakes**

Watch for these. They cover most of the avoidable problems that come up around transferring as an international student-athlete.
• "Entering the portal means another school will recruit me." Portal entry makes you visible; it does not guarantee anyone will contact you.
• "A coach wants me, so I am admitted." Admission is a separate decision made by the admissions office.
• "A verbal scholarship offer is final." Only a written, signed award is final.
• "My credits will automatically transfer." Credit transfer and how those credits apply to your degree are evaluated separately by the receiving school.
• "I will automatically be immediately eligible." Eligibility depends on your governing body's rules and your own history.
• "My current scholarship and roster spot are protected." Neither is guaranteed once you notify your school of an intent to transfer.
• "The portal transfers my F-1 record." Only your DSO can release your SEVIS record; a portal or notification system has nothing to do with it.
• "A new school can issue everything before I am admitted." Admission generally comes first.
• "My DSO decides whether I can compete." Athletics eligibility is decided by your governing body and athletics compliance, not your DSO.
• "Athletics compliance can move my SEVIS record." Only a DSO can do that.
• "A new Form I-20 means I am athletically eligible." A Form I-20 is an immigration document; it says nothing about your athletics eligibility.
• "My teammate transferred this way, so the same rules apply to me." Rules depend on your own sport, governing body, and history.
• "I can wait until after committing to ask about credits." Ask before you commit, in writing.
• "I can wait until the last minute to coordinate the SEVIS transfer." Timing is strict; start the conversation with both DSOs early.
• "Rep1 can guarantee the transfer outcome." Rep1 can help you prepare and ask the right questions; it cannot guarantee recruitment, admission, eligibility, aid, or your immigration outcome.

Before you finalise a transfer, work through this process:
1. Clarify why you are considering a transfer.
2. Confirm which governing-body and institutional rules apply to you.
3. Ask your current athletics-compliance office what portal or notification entry may change.
4. Gather your transcripts, degree-progress information, and eligibility records.
5. Identify the academic programmes and transfer-credit risks at any school you are considering.
6. Compare written costs, aid, housing, and scholarship terms across your options.
7. Confirm the destination institution's admission process and requirements.
8. Speak with both institutions' international student offices about SEVIS timing.
9. Separate coach interest from admission, aid, eligibility, and immigration confirmation.
10. Get every important term in writing before you rely on it.
11. Build a realistic timeline that covers athletics, academics, aid, housing, and immigration together.
12. Do not finalise the move until you understand the risks that remain unresolved.

The principle to carry with you through all of this: entering the portal, being recruited, being admitted, receiving financial aid, maintaining your F-1 status, and becoming athletically eligible are separate steps. None of them completes another, and working through this process does not guarantee any particular outcome. It means you will go into the decision with real information instead of assumptions.

**Key Takeaways**

A transfer touches your athletics, academics, finances, and immigration status at the same time, and each of those is decided by a separate office using separate rules. Portal or notification entry is only the start of a process, not admission, eligibility, aid, or immigration authorisation. NCAA, NAIA, and NJCAA rules are not interchangeable, and current rules, especially transfer-window and notification rules, should always be confirmed directly with your compliance office rather than assumed from memory. Your F-1 school transfer, coordinated through SEVIS by your current and destination DSOs, is entirely separate from your athletics transfer, and neither one completes the other. Written confirmation, not a verbal conversation, is what actually protects you when it comes to admission, credits, aid, and your immigration timeline.

**Next Action**

Build a Transfer Decision File before you take any formal step toward transferring. Include your reason for transferring, the consequences at your current school, a side-by-side comparison of any schools you are considering, the academic programme and transfer-credit review for each, written cost and scholarship details, your questions for athletics compliance, your admission status at each school, your questions for both institutions' international student offices about SEVIS timing, important dates, and any risks that remain unresolved. Building this file is not portal approval, not admission, not athletics eligibility, not a scholarship, not a roster guarantee, and not immigration authorisation. It means you will go into any conversation with a coach, an admissions office, or a DSO with the right questions already prepared.

### Module 27 — Plan Your Graduation, Work Authorization, and Return Home as an International Student-Athlete

Finishing your degree is a major milestone, but it does not resolve everything at once. Your athletics career, your academic programme, your F-1 status, your work options, and your eventual return to Australia all run on separate timelines with separate rules. This module explains what actually happens when your programme ends, what the standard grace period does and does not allow, how post-completion Optional Practical Training works at a general level, how employment, NIL, and self-employment questions are different from simply having an Employment Authorization Document, why travel needs extra care near the end of your programme, and how to think about continuing study, transferring, changing status, or returning home. It will not tell you which path to choose, and it will not resolve your own eligibility, filing, or immigration outcome. It will help you plan early, ask the right people the right questions, and avoid the most common and costly mistakes.

**Why Graduation Creates Multiple Timelines**

Graduation feels like one moment, but it actually triggers several separate timelines at once: your athletics eligibility and season, your academic programme and degree conferral, your F-1 immigration status, any work authorisation you might pursue, and your personal and financial planning. These timelines do not automatically move together, and assuming they do is one of the most common mistakes students make at this stage.

Your athletics season might end before your degree does, or your degree might finish before your last season of eligibility. Walking at a graduation ceremony is a celebration, not necessarily the moment your academic programme officially ends in the eyes of your school or the government. Your F-1 status is tied to your programme end date, which is a specific, verifiable fact, not an estimate based on when the ceremony is scheduled. Because these timelines run separately, the safest approach is to confirm each one directly with the right office rather than assume they line up.

This module will not tell you whether to pursue work authorisation, further study, or a return home. Its purpose is to help you understand what actually controls each of these timelines and to help you plan with real information well before your programme ends, rather than trying to figure things out after the fact.

**Programme Completion, Your Form I-20, and the Grace Period**

Your Form I-20 lists a program end date. This date, not your graduation ceremony date, is what your immigration status is tied to. Your school's international student office (your DSO) is the office that confirms this date and updates it in SEVIS if your actual completion date changes, for example if you finish earlier or later than originally planned. Do not assume the ceremony date and the Form I-20 program end date are the same day, and do not try to change your own Form I-20; this is your DSO's role.

Once your programme ends, a standard grace period generally applies, during which you are considered to be maintaining your F-1 status while you take your next step. During this general post-completion period you may prepare to depart the United States, transfer to another SEVP-certified school, or begin a new educational level, among other permitted steps. If you instead pursue and complete a period of post-completion Optional Practical Training (covered in the next section), a separate 60-day grace period applies after that OPT authorisation ends, during which you may prepare to depart, change your education level, transfer schools, or apply to change to another status.

What neither grace period does is authorise you to work, accept NIL compensation, do contract work, or engage in self-employment. It also does not mean you can leave the United States and return later using the grace period itself; departure and re-entry raise separate questions covered later in this module. This module will not calculate your personal program end date or grace period dates; confirm both directly with your DSO, since these are specific facts about your own SEVIS record, not general rules this module can apply for you.

**Understanding Post-Completion OPT**

Post-completion Optional Practical Training (OPT) is temporary employment authorisation connected to your field of study, available after you complete your degree. It is not automatic. To be considered, you generally need to have completed your course of study (or all requirements except thesis or equivalent), obtain your DSO's recommendation entered into your SEVIS record, properly file Form I-765 with USCIS, and receive a valid Employment Authorization Document (EAD) before you begin any work. A DSO recommendation is not the same as USCIS approval, and filing Form I-765 is not the same as being approved. This module will not tell you when to file; the filing window is specific to your own program end date and DSO recommendation date, and must be confirmed with your DSO.

Once approved, post-completion OPT generally must be completed within a set period following your program completion, and you are allowed a limited amount of total unemployment time during that period before your authorisation is affected. Overtime does not offset unemployment, and periods of part-time or no work count against this limit. Keeping your DSO informed of your address and any change of employer is part of maintaining your status during OPT, not an optional courtesy.

Some students in certain STEM-classified degree fields may later be eligible for an additional STEM OPT extension. This is not automatic either: it depends on your specific degree classification, your employer meeting specific federal requirements (including E-Verify enrollment and a genuine employer-employee relationship), and a formal training plan filed with your DSO. This module will not tell you whether your degree or employer qualifies, and it will not walk you through the STEM OPT paperwork step by step; that determination and process should be worked through directly with your DSO and, where the situation is unclear, qualified immigration counsel.

**Employment, NIL, Self-Employment, and Work-Authorization Boundaries**

Receiving an Employment Authorization Document (EAD) does not mean every type of paid or unpaid activity is automatically covered. Work performed under post-completion OPT generally needs to relate to your field of study, and this module cannot tell you whether a specific job, role, or opportunity meets that standard for your own situation; that is a question for your DSO and, where needed, qualified immigration counsel.

Traditional employment, contract work, self-employment, and volunteering or unpaid work each raise their own questions under OPT rules. A job offer or a willing employer does not, by itself, create work authorisation, and an employer cannot grant you immigration permission no matter how confident they are that it will work out. NIL activity raises the same core question this academy has flagged since Module 24: federal guidance has not directly addressed how NIL compensation interacts with F-1 status, so this module does not state that NIL activity is automatically allowed or automatically prohibited under OPT. That open question, and how to approach it cautiously, is covered in full in Module 24; this module does not repeat that content, only flags that OPT does not resolve it either.

Paying tax on income does not create or prove work authorisation, and neither does having NIL approval from your school or governing body. If you are unsure whether a specific job, contract, or activity is permitted under your current status, the right approach is to ask your DSO before you start, not after, and to get the answer in writing where possible.

**Travel, Re-Entry, and Maintaining Records**

Travel near the end of your programme, during OPT, or while any immigration application is pending deserves extra caution. An unexpired visa stamp does not by itself guarantee you will be readmitted to the United States; admission is a separate decision made at the border. A valid Form I-20 does not guarantee re-entry either. Leaving the United States during a grace period, or while an application such as a change of status is still pending, can affect your options: for example, under the current federal framework, traveling abroad while a change-of-status application is pending can result in that portion of the application being treated as abandoned. Rules and enforcement practices in this area can shift, so confirm your specific travel plans with your DSO before you book anything, rather than assuming last year's advice still applies.

This module will not tell you whether to travel or predict how a border or consular officer will decide your case; that is not something any handbook can responsibly promise. What it can do is remind you to keep your own records in order: your passport, visa, current Form I-20, EAD (if applicable), evidence of your degree and program end date, and any letters or emails from your DSO. Keeping copies of these, including digital copies, makes it much easier to answer questions quickly if you are asked for them, whether at the border, by an employer, or by a future school.

**Continuing Study, School Transfer, Change of Status, or Departure**

As your programme ends, there are several distinct pathways to consider, and none of them completes another. You might continue studying at a higher level, transfer to a different school, apply to change your nonimmigrant status (for example, toward employment-based status), or depart and return to Australia. Each pathway has its own separate procedure.

If you are considering a transfer at this stage, Module 26 already covers the general transfer-portal and SEVIS-transfer process in depth and is not repeated here. One timing detail matters specifically at this stage: if you are on post-completion OPT and transferring, you generally need to be able to resume classes at the new school within five months of transferring out of your current school or by the date your OPT authorisation ends, whichever is earlier, and your OPT employment authorisation ends automatically once you transfer or begin a new educational level. Confirm your own timeline with both DSOs directly; this module does not calculate it for you.

Change of status (for example, toward an employer-sponsored visa category) is a separate federal filing process with its own timing rules, including a specific mechanism that can extend your F-1 status in narrow circumstances while certain petitions are pending. This module does not recommend a change-of-status strategy, does not calculate deadlines, and does not promise that filing anything guarantees you can remain in the United States while it is decided. If you are exploring this path, involve your DSO and qualified immigration counsel early, since timing and eligibility depend on your specific circumstances.

Departure is not a fallback option or a sign that something went wrong; it is one of several legitimate outcomes of this stage, and it is covered in the next section.

**Preparing to Return Home and Protecting Your Future Options**

Returning to Australia at the end of your programme is a legitimate and often strategic choice, not a failure. Many students build genuinely valuable careers, relationships, and opportunities back home using the degree, experience, and network built during their time in the United States. Preparing well protects your future options no matter what you eventually decide.

Before you leave, it helps to gather and safeguard: your academic transcripts and proof of your degree, your immigration documents (Form I-20 history, visa, EAD if you had one), your tax records, your employment records if you worked, your banking and financial account details, your health and insurance records and any prescriptions, your housing and lease closure paperwork, and your coaching and career references. Keep both physical and digital copies where possible, since some of these records are much harder to obtain again once you have left the country.

Departure also does not automatically end every U.S. obligation. Module 25 covers your U.S. tax obligations in depth and should be your primary reference; at a general level, departing aliens are generally expected to address their U.S. tax filing status before leaving, and an annual U.S. tax return may still be required even after you have departed. This module does not calculate your personal tax position or departure requirements; a qualified tax professional is the right resource for that. Similarly, this module does not tell you to close every U.S. account or abandon U.S. credit history, and it does not recommend a specific bank, tax preparer, or shipping company; those are personal decisions worth researching on their own merits.

**Common Mistakes**

Watch for these. They cover most of the avoidable problems that come up at graduation and afterward.
• "I graduated, so I can start working immediately." You need an approved EAD in hand before you begin work.
• "My DSO recommended OPT, so USCIS has approved it." A DSO recommendation is not USCIS approval.
• "I filed Form I-765, so I can begin work." Filing is not approval; you need the actual EAD.
• "My EAD allows me to do any job." Work generally needs to relate to your field of study, and not every activity is covered.
• "Any paid activity is fine once I have OPT." Self-employment, contract work, and NIL activity all raise separate questions.
• "NIL activity is automatically allowed on OPT." This is an open question this academy has flagged since Module 24, not a settled rule.
• "Paying tax on this income makes the work authorised." Tax reporting and work authorisation are separate matters.
• "My visa stamp is still valid, so re-entry is guaranteed." Admission is a separate decision made at the border.
• "The graduation ceremony date controls every deadline." Your Form I-20 program end date is the controlling fact, confirmed by your DSO.
• "The grace period lets me work." The grace period allows you to prepare for your next step; it does not authorise employment.
• "I can leave during the grace period and come back whenever." Departure during a grace period or pending application can affect your options.
• "My employer can fix my immigration status." An employer cannot grant immigration permission.
• "My coach can tell me whether a job qualifies for OPT." That is a DSO and, where needed, immigration-counsel question.
• "My teammate did this, so the same answer applies to me." Individual circumstances, degrees, and timelines differ.
• "Returning home means I failed." Returning home is a legitimate, often strategic outcome.
• "I can wait until after graduation to start planning." Confirm your program end date and options well before your programme ends.
• "Rep1 can approve my work authorisation." Rep1 can help you prepare and ask the right questions; it cannot approve OPT, guarantee an EAD, or guarantee any immigration outcome.
• "A pending application means I can keep working or stay indefinitely." A pending filing does not guarantee approval, continued work authorisation, or permission to remain.

Before you finalise your plan, work through this process:
1. Confirm your official program end date with your DSO.
2. List every deadline and decision point that applies to you.
3. Decide which pathway you are exploring: work authorisation, further study, transfer, change of status, or departure.
4. Gather your immigration, academic, employment, tax, and identification records.
5. Confirm whether any activity you are considering actually requires authorisation.
6. Ask your DSO what institutional steps apply to your situation.
7. Seek qualified legal advice where your situation is unclear or high-risk.
8. Confirm travel risks before you book anything or leave the country.
9. Keep written records of every application, approval, deadline, and piece of guidance you receive.
10. Prepare a return-home plan even if your primary goal is to remain in the United States.
11. Preserve your transcripts, references, tax documents, immigration records, and career materials.
12. Do not begin work, travel, or rely on any outcome until the required approvals are actually confirmed.

The principle to carry with you: entering the portal, being recruited, receiving a degree, being recommended for OPT, filing a form, and being approved are all separate steps. None of them completes another, and working through this process does not guarantee any particular outcome. It means you go into this final stage with real information instead of assumptions.

**Key Takeaways**

Graduation triggers several separate timelines at once: athletics, academics, immigration status, work authorisation, and personal planning do not automatically move together. Your Form I-20 program end date, not your ceremony date, controls your F-1 status, and grace periods exist but only permit specific next steps, not employment. Post-completion OPT requires a DSO recommendation, a USCIS filing, and an approved EAD before work can begin, and an EAD does not authorise every type of paid or unpaid activity, including NIL and self-employment. Travel near the end of your programme or during a pending application carries real risk and deserves extra care. Continuing study, transferring, changing status, and returning home are separate pathways, and returning home is a legitimate outcome, not a failure. Confirming facts directly with your DSO and qualified professionals, rather than assuming, is what protects you at every step.

**Next Action**

Build a Graduation Transition File before your programme ends. Where relevant, bring forward your NIL Review File, Tax Preparation File, and Transfer Decision File from earlier in this academy rather than starting from nothing. Include your confirmed program end date, the pathway options you are considering (work authorisation, further study, transfer, change of status, or departure), your immigration documents, your employment or OPT questions, your travel concerns, your tax records, a departure plan, important deadlines, your professional contacts (DSO, tax professional, attorney where relevant), and any risks that remain unresolved. Building this file is not work authorisation, not USCIS approval, not legal advice, not a visa, not re-entry permission, not a guarantee of employment, and not a guarantee that you may remain in the United States. It means you will go into your final year with real questions answered instead of assumptions.
